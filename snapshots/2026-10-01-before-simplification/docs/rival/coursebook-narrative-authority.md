## 1. The Problem With the Red Map

Every schoolroom in Britain once had one: the Mercator projection with a quarter of the world washed in the same confident pink-red, from Canada round to New Zealand, so that the sun genuinely never set on it. The map was a lie — not because the territories were not British, but because the single colour concealed the thing you most need to understand. **The British Empire was never one thing.** It was a ramshackle collection of at least seven different kinds of political arrangement, acquired over four centuries by at least six different methods, governed by wildly different rules, and held together less by power than by a series of bargains — with settlers, with princes, with chiefs, with merchants, with soldiers recruited from the very populations being ruled.

This chapter makes an argument, and you should be able to state it back by the end. It is this. **The British Empire was not planned. It was improvised — a rolling series of solutions to problems of debt, security and trade, executed largely by men on the spot whom London could neither fully control nor afford to disown. It expanded when the ratio of profit to cost looked good and when local collaborators could be found; it collapsed when that ratio inverted and those collaborators either withdrew their cooperation or demanded a price Britain could no longer pay.** Acquisition and dissolution, in other words, are not two separate stories. They are the same story, run forwards and then backwards.

That argument has consequences. It means we should be suspicious of the two most popular explanations of the empire, which are mirror images of each other. The first is the *heroic* account: a great civilising project, deliberately built. The second is the *conspiratorial* account: a single grand plot to loot the world. Both credit London with far more intention and far more control than it ever had. The historian Sir John Seeley, in a set of Cambridge lectures published in 1883 as *The Expansion of England*, produced the phrase that still haunts the subject: the English, he wrote, "seem, as it were, to have conquered and peopled half the world in a fit of absence of mind."

Seeley meant it as a criticism — he thought Britons paid too little attention to their empire. But taken too literally the phrase becomes an alibi, as though nobody chose anything. People chose constantly. The point is that they were choosing in the dark, locally, for short-term reasons, and the sum of their choices was an empire nobody had designed.

### How to read the numbers

At its territorial maximum in the early 1920s the empire covered roughly 13.7 million square miles — about a quarter of the world's land surface — and contained something in the region of 450 million people, again roughly a quarter of humanity. Treat both figures with care. They rest on colonial censuses of variable quality, and they lump together a Canadian farmer with full voting rights, an Indian peasant with none, and a nomad in the Sudan whom no British official had ever met. A quarter of the world was *claimed*. Rather less than that was *administered*. Less again was actually *controlled*.

## 2. Key Terms You Will Need

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Colony of settlement** | Territory where large numbers of Britons settled permanently, displacing or dominating existing populations (Canada, Australia, New Zealand, parts of southern Africa). |
| **Colony of rule / Crown colony** | Territory governed from London through an appointed governor, with a small European official class ruling a large non-European population (Jamaica after 1866, Ceylon, Nigeria). |
| **Protectorate** | A state that kept its own ruler but surrendered foreign policy and, usually, much else to Britain. Cheaper than a colony; legally, the inhabitants were not British subjects but "British protected persons". |
| **Indirect rule** | Governing through existing local authorities — emirs, chiefs, princes — rather than replacing them. Associated with Lord Lugard in northern Nigeria. Cheap, and it froze fluid societies into rigid "tribal" categories. |
| **Dominion** | A self-governing settler state, sharing the Crown but running its own affairs. Canada (1867), Australia (1901), New Zealand (1907), South Africa (1910), Irish Free State (1922). |
| **Mandate** | Territory taken from the German and Ottoman empires after 1918, held under League of Nations supervision with a formal duty to prepare it for self-government. Empire with a conscience clause and an expiry date. |
| **Informal empire** | Countries never coloured red, but shaped by British capital, navies and trade treaties — Argentina, Siam, Persia, much of China's coast. |
| **Mercantilism** | The pre-1840s idea that colonies existed to supply the mother country and buy from it, enforced by the Navigation Acts. Displaced by free trade. |
| **Imperial preference** | The 1930s reversal: tariff walls around the empire, agreed at Ottawa in 1932, to keep imperial trade inside the family. |
| **The Raj** | British rule in India, 1858–1947, comprising directly ruled "British India" (about three-fifths of the territory) and some 565 nominally independent princely states. |
| **Decolonisation** | The transfer of sovereignty from imperial power to successor state. Rarely a single moment; usually a negotiated sequence over years. |

## 3. The First Empire: The Atlantic Machine, 1583–1783

England came late and badly to overseas expansion. Spain and Portugal had a century's head start; the first English attempts were failures, sometimes comic ones. John Cabot, sailing under Henry VII, reached Newfoundland or Cape Breton in 1497 and found no gold. Sir Humphrey Gilbert claimed Newfoundland for Elizabeth I in 1583 and drowned on the way home. The Roanoke colonies of 1585 and 1587 vanished. Not until **Jamestown in Virginia in 1607** did an English settlement in the Americas survive, and it very nearly did not: of the first several thousand settlers, most died of disease, hunger or conflict with the Powhatan confederacy.

What changed everything was not gold or God but **a crop**. Tobacco in Virginia from the 1610s, and above all **sugar in the Caribbean** — St Kitts settled in 1624, Barbados in 1627, Jamaica seized from Spain in 1655 — created something new: plantations, meaning large agricultural factories that required enormous, disciplined, replaceable labour forces to produce a single export commodity for a distant market.

That requirement is the hinge on which the whole first empire turns. Indentured servants from Britain and Ireland were tried first, and were insufficient. From the 1640s the Caribbean colonies turned decisively to enslaved Africans. England entered the trade seriously with the Company of Royal Adventurers (1660), reorganised as the **Royal African Company in 1672**; when its monopoly was effectively broken in 1698, the trade exploded as private merchants of Bristol, London and above all Liverpool poured in.

The scale should be stated plainly. Over the whole period of the Atlantic slave trade, British and British-colonial ships carried away roughly **3.1 to 3.4 million** enslaved Africans. Something like **2.7 million** survived the Middle Passage to be landed alive; the difference is the death toll of the voyage itself. Britain was, for most of the eighteenth century, the largest slave-trading power in the world. This is not a footnote to the first empire. It *was* the first empire's economic engine.

### Case study: Barbados, or how an island was rebuilt around a plant

Barbados is 166 square miles — smaller than the Isle of Wight. In 1640 it grew tobacco and cotton on smallholdings worked by white indentured servants, and its population was mostly European. By 1680 it grew almost nothing but sugar, on consolidated estates, and the majority of its people were enslaved Africans. Within four decades the island's ecology, demography, law and social order had been entirely reconstructed around one commodity. The Barbados Slave Code of 1661 — which classified the enslaved as property and set out punishments for them while offering almost no protection — was then exported to Jamaica, Antigua and South Carolina. Small islands can be historically enormous. In the 1770s the tiny British Caribbean was worth far more in trade to Britain than the whole of North America.

### The system that held it together

The first empire was governed by an idea: **mercantilism**. The Navigation Acts of 1651 and 1660, and their successors, laid down that colonial goods must travel in British or colonial ships, that certain "enumerated" commodities (sugar, tobacco, later others) must be shipped to Britain first, and that colonists should buy British manufactures. The empire was conceived as a closed circuit designed to run a trade surplus for the metropolis.

Alongside it ran the **chartered company**: a device by which the Crown granted a group of merchants a monopoly and quasi-governmental powers, so that expansion could happen without the Crown paying for it. The East India Company (chartered 31 December 1600), the Royal African Company (1672) and the Hudson's Bay Company (1670) are the great examples. Remember this device. It recurs constantly — the Royal Niger Company, the British South Africa Company, the Imperial British East Africa Company — and it is the single clearest evidence for our thesis. **Britain repeatedly expanded by outsourcing empire to private companies because it did not want to pay for empire itself.** The pattern was always the same: the company overreached, got into difficulties, and the state had to take over the mess.

War did the rest. The eighteenth century was a long duel with France, and each round moved territory. The **Treaty of Utrecht (1713)** brought Britain Newfoundland, Acadia (renamed Nova Scotia), the Hudson Bay territories, Gibraltar (taken 1704), Minorca (1708) and the *asiento*, the contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America. The **Treaty of Paris (1763)**, ending the Seven Years' War, was the greatest single haul in British history: New France (Canada), Florida from Spain, all French claims east of the Mississippi, Senegal, and the Caribbean islands of Grenada, Dominica, St Vincent and Tobago.

In 1763 Britain had the largest empire in the world and no idea how to pay for it. Twenty years later it had lost the most valuable part.

## 4. The First Amputation: America, 1763–1783

Here is the first great dissolution, and it teaches a lesson that will recur in 1947 and 1960: **empires most often break at the point where the imperial power tries to make the colony pay its own costs.**

Victory in 1763 nearly doubled the national debt, to around £130 million. It also left Britain garrisoning a vast new frontier. Successive governments concluded, not unreasonably, that colonists who had been defended at British expense should contribute. Hence the Sugar Act (1764), the **Stamp Act (1765)**, the Townshend duties (1767) and the Tea Act (1773).

Every measure collided with a principle the colonists had absorbed from English constitutional history itself: no taxation without representation. The colonies had their own elected assemblies, long practice in self-government, and a political vocabulary borrowed straight from the English Civil War and 1688. They were not rebelling against English liberty; they were claiming it.

The sequence ran fast. The Boston Massacre (1770); the Boston Tea Party (December 1773); the Coercive Acts (1774); the First Continental Congress (1774); shooting at Lexington and Concord (19 April 1775); the **Declaration of Independence (4 July 1776)**. Britain might still have won a colonial war. It could not win a world war: France entered in 1778, Spain in 1779, the Dutch in 1780, and the Royal Navy — the thing that made empire possible — was suddenly stretched from the Caribbean to the Channel to India. At **Yorktown (19 October 1781)** Cornwallis surrendered to a Franco-American force whose escape route was closed by a French fleet. The **Treaty of Paris (3 September 1783)** recognised the independence of thirteen colonies.

Britain kept Canada, the Caribbean, and its Indian foothold — and, crucially, kept trading with the United States on a rising scale. That fact mattered enormously to how Britons thought afterwards. It suggested that political rule was not necessary for commercial profit, an idea that would take fifty years to mature into free-trade orthodoxy.

## 5. The Swing to the East: The Second Empire, 1757–1857

Historians call what happened next "the swing to the East". It was not a plan either.

### Case study: Bengal, or how a trading company became a government

The East India Company had traded on the Indian coast since the early 1600s from fortified "factories" at Surat, Madras (1639), Bombay (acquired 1661 as part of Catherine of Braganza's dowry) and Calcutta (1690). It was a tenant, not a ruler; the Mughal emperor was the sovereign. But the Mughal empire had been fragmenting since the death of Aurangzeb in 1707, and its successor states — Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, the Marathas, Mysore — were rivals in a competitive, militarised system. In such a system a well-drilled, well-financed force with reliable artillery and access to European credit was an extremely valuable ally.

At **Plassey on 23 June 1757**, Robert Clive defeated the Nawab of Bengal, Siraj-ud-Daula. It was less a battle than the execution of a conspiracy: Clive had already bought the defection of the Nawab's commander Mir Jafar and the backing of the Jagat Seth banking house. Note this carefully. **The founding victory of British India was won by collaboration, not conquest.** Seven years later, at Buxar (22 October 1764), the Company beat a combined force of the Nawab of Awadh, the deposed Nawab of Bengal and the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II — this time a real battle. And in the **Treaty of Allahabad (12 August 1765)** the emperor granted the Company the *diwani* of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa: the right to collect the land revenue of the richest province in India.

That grant is the true beginning of the British Empire in Asia. A commercial company now had a tax base of perhaps twenty million people. It could raise armies from Indian revenue and recruit Indian soldiers — *sepoys* — to fight for it. By 1800 the Company's armies numbered around 200,000 men, overwhelmingly Indian. **The British conquered India largely with Indian soldiers paid out of Indian taxes.** No proposition about the empire is more important, or more often forgotten.

What followed was not benevolent. In 1770 a catastrophic famine struck Bengal; contemporaries and later estimates put the death toll at a very large fraction of the population, perhaps a third, and the Company's rigid revenue demands and its servants' grain speculation made a natural disaster far worse. In Britain the "nabobs" — Company men returning with immense private fortunes — became a political scandal. Edmund Burke, prosecuting Warren Hastings in a famous impeachment (1788–1795), described the Company's servants descending on India as "birds of prey and passage". Hastings was acquitted, but the state moved in: the **Regulating Act (1773)** and **Pitt's India Act (1784)** placed the Company under government supervision. It is a pattern we have seen before and will see again — private empire, scandal, state takeover.

Expansion then proceeded by war and treaty over sixty years: Mysore defeated in four wars (Tipu Sultan killed at Seringapatam, 4 May 1799); the Marathas broken in 1803–05 and finally 1817–18; Sind annexed 1843; **the Punjab annexed in 1849** after two Anglo-Sikh wars. By 1849 the Company ruled or dominated almost the whole subcontinent. Around it, an Asian ring accumulated: Penang (1786), **Singapore (1819)**, Malacca confirmed by the Anglo-Dutch Treaty (1824), lower Burma in stages (1826, 1852) and upper Burma in 1885–86, **Hong Kong Island ceded by the Treaty of Nanking (29 August 1842)** after the First Opium War — a war fought, it should be said clearly, to force China to accept the import of a narcotic grown in British India.

## 6. Settlers, Slaves and the Politics of Conscience, 1783–1857

While India was being conquered, three other things were happening that would define the empire's next century.

**First, settlement resumed.** On **26 January 1788** the First Fleet landed at Sydney Cove, founding New South Wales as a penal colony — Britain having lost the American dumping ground for convicts. Some 160,000 convicts were transported to Australia before the practice ended (1868 in Western Australia). The colony was founded on the legal doctrine that the continent was *terra nullius*, "nobody's land", a fiction not overturned in Australian law until the High Court's *Mabo* judgment of 1992. The consequences for Aboriginal Australians — dispossession, frontier killings, and catastrophic mortality from introduced disease — ran for a century and a half.

Elsewhere the Napoleonic Wars delivered a strategic harvest, confirmed at Vienna in 1815: the **Cape Colony** (taken 1795, returned, retaken 1806, ceded 1814), **Ceylon** (coastal 1796, Crown colony 1802, Kandyan kingdom absorbed 1815), Mauritius, Trinidad, Tobago, St Lucia, Malta and British Guiana. Notice what these have in common. They are not farmland. They are **naval stations and route-nodes** — Gibraltar, Malta, the Cape, Ceylon, later Aden (1839) and Singapore. Britain was assembling a chain of bases that made the Indian Ocean a British lake. The empire's logic in this period is maritime and strategic before it is territorial.

**Second, and astonishingly, Britain turned against slavery.** The abolition movement — founded in 1787, driven by Quakers, evangelicals, formerly enslaved writers like Equiano and Ottobah Cugoano, mass petitioning, a sugar boycott by consumers, and above all by the relentless resistance of the enslaved themselves in revolts from Tacky's War (1760) to the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) to the Baptist War in Jamaica (1831–32) — achieved the **abolition of the British slave trade in 1807** and the **Slavery Abolition Act of 1833**, freeing some 800,000 people across the empire from 1 August 1834.

Do not let this become a simple redemption story, and do not let it become a simple cynicism story either. Both are available and both are wrong. Consider:

- Emancipation was accompanied by **£20 million in compensation** — paid not to the freed, but to the slave-owners. This was around 40 per cent of annual government expenditure, financed by a loan not fully paid off until 2015.
- The freed were forced into an "apprenticeship" that kept them working unpaid for their former owners until it was abandoned in 1838 under pressure.
- The Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron then spent six decades suppressing the slave trade of *other* nations, at real cost in ships and lives — and that anti-slavery mission became, in west and east Africa, one of the standard justifications for further intervention and eventual annexation. Abolition ended one form of coercion and licensed another.
- Slavery in India and other Company territories was excluded from the 1833 Act; it was addressed separately, and imperfectly, in 1843. And **indentured labour** replaced enslaved labour: between the 1830s and 1917 well over a million Indian workers were shipped under indenture to Mauritius, Trinidad, British Guiana, Natal and Fiji, on contracts many could not read and few could escape.

**Third, the settler colonies learned to govern themselves.** After rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada in 1837–38, Lord Durham's **Report on the Affairs of British North America (1839)** proposed that the settler colonies be granted "responsible government" — ministries answerable to elected local assemblies — while London retained foreign policy, defence and trade. Nova Scotia and the Province of Canada got it in 1848; the Australian colonies and New Zealand followed in the 1850s. The Durham formula is one of the great inventions of imperial history, and it is deeply double-edged. It kept white settlers loyal by giving them power. It simultaneously handed those settlers control over indigenous land and indigenous people, and removed London's ability to restrain them. Self-government for some was dispossession for others.

Meanwhile, in the empire's oldest and nearest colony, catastrophe. The **Great Irish Famine (1845–49)** killed around one million people and drove another million to emigrate, in a United Kingdom that was simultaneously the richest state in the world and continued to export food from Ireland while its people starved. Government relief was constrained by an ideological commitment to markets and by a widespread official belief that Irish poverty was a moral failing. Ireland is the standing rebuke to any account of the empire that places it safely overseas.

## 7. The Victorian Zenith: Rule, Rebellion and the Scramble, 1857–1902

### 1857: the year the mask slipped

In May 1857 sepoys of the Bengal Army mutinied at Meerut. Within weeks the rising had spread across the Gangetic plain, gathering to it dispossessed princes, peasants crushed by revenue demands, artisans ruined by machine-made cloth, and religious leaders alarmed by missionary activity and by the Company's blithe interference in custom. The immediate trigger — cartridges greased with animal fat, offensive to Hindu and Muslim soldiers alike — is remembered because it is vivid, but it lit a fire already laid.

Call it what you like: the Indian Mutiny, the Great Rebellion, the First War of Independence. The naming is itself an argument. What matters is what it revealed and what it caused. It revealed that British rule rested on a consent that could be withdrawn. It caused a reprisal of extraordinary savagery — villages burned, prisoners blown from the mouths of cannon, mass hangings along the Grand Trunk Road — and a permanent hardening of racial attitudes among the British in India.

Constitutionally, the consequences were decisive. The **Government of India Act (2 August 1858)** abolished East India Company rule and transferred India to the Crown. Queen Victoria's Proclamation of 1 November 1858 promised that her Indian subjects "of whatever Race or Creed, be freely and impartially admitted to Offices in Our Service" — a promise honoured slowly and grudgingly. The **Royal Titles Act (1876)** made Victoria Empress of India, proclaimed at the Delhi Durbar on 1 January 1877. And a new caution set in: no more annexations of princely states, and a firm alliance with conservative Indian elites. The Raj after 1858 was more powerful and more nervous than the Company had ever been.

### Free trade and the empire nobody mapped

For most of the mid-Victorian period, Britain did not want more colonies. It wanted markets. Having repealed the Corn Laws in 1846 and dismantled the old protective system, Britain became the world's workshop, banker and shipper. Where it could get trade without rule, it took trade without rule: treaty ports in China, gunboat diplomacy in Latin America, railway investment in Argentina, a de facto veto in Persia and the Ottoman Empire.

In 1953 two Cambridge historians, John Gallagher and Ronald Robinson, published an article that reorganised the whole field. Their formula for British policy was: **"trade with informal control if possible; trade with rule when necessary."** The map of red, they argued, is only the visible tip; British power operated at least as much through an "informal empire" that no atlas showed. Their second idea matters even more for our thesis: expansion was often driven not from London but by **crises on the periphery** — a collapsing local regime, a threatened trade route, a panicking man on the spot — to which London responded reluctantly and expensively.

Egypt is the textbook case. The Suez Canal opened in 1869 and instantly became the artery to India. In 1875 Disraeli bought the bankrupt Khedive's 44 per cent shareholding for £4 million. When Egyptian nationalist officers under Ahmed 'Urabi revolted against foreign financial control in 1881–82, Gladstone's Liberal government — a government elected on anti-imperial rhetoric — bombarded Alexandria and invaded, winning at **Tel el-Kebir on 13 September 1882**. Britain announced the occupation would be temporary. It lasted, in one form or another, until 1956.

### The Scramble for Africa, 1880–1902

In 1870 Europeans controlled perhaps a tenth of Africa, mostly coastal. By 1914 they controlled all of it except Ethiopia and Liberia. Britain took the largest share: Egypt (1882), the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan (reconquered 1896–98, condominium from 1899), British East Africa (protectorate 1895, "Kenya" from 1920), Uganda (1894), Zanzibar (1890), Nyasaland (1891), the two Rhodesias (from 1889 via Cecil Rhodes's British South Africa Company), Bechuanaland (1885), Nigeria (Lagos 1861; Royal Niger Company 1886; protectorates 1900; amalgamated 1914), the Gold Coast (1874, with Asante finally annexed in 1902), Sierra Leone, and the Gambia.

Why the sudden rush? Four causes, weighted:

1. **European rivalry.** The unification of Germany (1871) and Belgian activity in the Congo turned Africa into a board on which European states scored points against each other. The **Berlin Conference (November 1884 – February 1885)** did not carve up Africa, contrary to popular belief; it set the *rules* for carving it up, notably that claims required "effective occupation". That rule turned paper claims into expeditions.
2. **Strategy, above all the route to India.** Egypt and the Cape were the bookends; much of the rest was acquired to protect them, or to deny them to rivals. The scramble for east Africa was substantially a scramble for the headwaters of the Nile.
3. **The man on the spot.** Rhodes, Frederick Lugard, George Goldie, missionaries, consuls and company agents repeatedly created facts that London then had to ratify or repudiate. Ratifying was usually cheaper politically.
4. **Technology and medicine.** Quinine prophylaxis, steamships on rivers, the telegraph, and after 1884 the Maxim gun collapsed the cost of conquest. At **Omdurman on 2 September 1898** an Anglo-Egyptian force lost some 48 dead; the Mahdist army lost around 10,000. That ratio, more than any ideology, explains the speed of the scramble.

Africans were not passive. The Asante fought four wars against Britain, the last — the War of the Golden Stool in 1900 — led by the queen mother Yaa Asantewaa. The Zulu destroyed a British column at Isandlwana on 22 January 1879. The Ndebele and Shona rose in the *Chimurenga* of 1896–97. The Mahdist state ruled Sudan independently from 1885 to 1898 and killed Gordon at Khartoum. Resistance failed militarily. It succeeded in shaping what colonial rule then looked like: where conquest was costly, Britain governed indirectly and lightly, through the very elites it had failed to destroy.

### The war that frightened Britain

The **South African War (11 October 1899 – 31 May 1902)** was supposed to be a short police action against two small Boer republics sitting on the world's largest goldfield. It took nearly three years, 450,000 imperial troops, and £200 million. When the Boers turned to guerrilla warfare, Kitchener responded with farm-burning and with **concentration camps**: around 28,000 Boer civilians died in them, the great majority children under sixteen, and at least 14,000 Black Africans died in separate, worse-supplied camps — a figure long left out of the accounts and still uncertain.

The camps were exposed by **Emily Hobhouse**, an English welfare campaigner whose 1901 report to the Distress Fund committee described conditions in flat, unbearable detail and warned that to keep the camps going was "murder to the children". She was denounced as a traitor and refused re-entry to South Africa. Her intervention is a reminder that criticism of empire was not invented later by historians; it was contemporary, British, and often female.

The war left Britain victorious, isolated and shaken. Joseph Chamberlain told the Colonial Conference of 1902: "The Weary Titan staggers under the too vast orb of its fate." Recruiting had revealed the poor physical condition of the urban working class, prompting a domestic debate on "national efficiency". After 1902 Britain hurried to reduce its enemies: alliance with Japan (1902), *entente* with France (1904), agreement with Russia (1907). **The empire had grown so large that it could no longer be defended alone.** That sentence is the bridge to everything that follows.

### Case study: The Treaty of Waitangi, or the empire in two languages

On **6 February 1840**, at Waitangi in the Bay of Islands, representatives of the Crown and around 540 Māori chiefs signed a treaty. There were two texts. The English text ceded "sovereignty" to the Queen. The Māori text, which almost every chief actually signed, ceded *kāwanatanga* — governorship — while guaranteeing chiefs *tino rangatiratanga*, full chieftainship, over their lands and treasures. Those are not the same thing.

Was this deliberate deception or genuine mistranslation into a language that had no word for European sovereignty? Historians disagree. What is certain is the outcome: settler numbers rose, land hunger grew, and from 1845 to 1872 the New Zealand Wars were fought over exactly the ambiguity the treaty had left. Under the New Zealand Settlements Act 1863 more than a million hectares of Māori land were confiscated. The treaty then lay largely dormant in law for a century — until the Waitangi Tribunal, established in 1975, made it the basis of a still-continuing process of settlement and apology.

Waitangi is the empire in miniature: a genuine agreement, differently understood by each side, honoured or dishonoured according to the balance of power that came after, and argued about ever since.

## 8. The Empire at Its Greatest Extent, 1919–1922: A Map in Words

Close your eyes and build it. The empire in 1922 had six great limbs.

**The Atlantic and American limb.** Canada, self-governing since 1867 and by now effectively a sovereign state, running from Newfoundland (a separate Dominion until 1949) across to British Columbia. Down the Caribbean: Jamaica, the Bahamas, Trinidad, Barbados, the Leewards and Windwards, British Honduras on the mainland of Central America, British Guiana on the shoulder of South America. Far to the south, the Falkland Islands.

**The African limb** — the famous "Cape to Cairo" band, complete after 1918 when German East Africa became the British mandate of Tanganyika. From Egypt (occupied 1882, nominally independent 1922 with four matters reserved to Britain), south through the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, Uganda, Kenya, Tanganyika, Northern Rhodesia, Southern Rhodesia, Bechuanaland, into the Union of South Africa. On the west coast, four separate blocks: the Gambia, Sierra Leone, the Gold Coast, and Nigeria — the last much the largest, with a Muslim north ruled indirectly through emirs and a Christian and animist south ruled more directly, a fault line that would matter greatly after 1960.

**The Middle Eastern limb**, new and unstable: mandates for Palestine, Transjordan and Iraq, carved from the Ottoman Empire and confirmed at San Remo in 1920; protectorates and treaty relationships along the Gulf from Aden (1839) round to Kuwait; Cyprus, occupied since 1878 and annexed in 1914.

**The Indian limb**, the heart of it all: the Raj, comprising directly administered British India and 565 princely states whose rulers kept their thrones under British "paramountcy"; plus Burma (governed as part of India until 1937), and Ceylon administered separately.

**The Southeast Asian and Pacific limb.** The Straits Settlements and the Malay states, Sarawak and North Borneo, Hong Kong. Then Australia, federated on 1 January 1901, and New Zealand, a Dominion from 1907, each with its own small sub-empire in the Pacific: Papua, Nauru, Western Samoa, Fiji, the Solomons, the Gilbert and Ellice Islands.

**And the stitching**: a chain of coaling stations, cable relays and naval bases — Gibraltar, Malta, Cyprus, Aden, Ceylon, Singapore, Hong Kong, the Falklands, St Helena, Ascension — which are the real skeleton of the thing. The empire was a *system of communications* before it was a territory. When the communications failed, the territory followed.

## 9. The Long Crisis: Two Wars and the Arithmetic of Weakness, 1914–1945

The empire reached its maximum extent immediately after the First World War and began dying at the same moment. That is not a paradox. It is the central fact of the twentieth century.

### 1914–1918: victory that cost more than it gained

The empire mobilised on an astonishing scale. Around **1.4 million Indians** served, in France, Mesopotamia and East Africa. Canada raised some 620,000 men, Australia around 416,000, New Zealand over 100,000 from a population of barely a million, South Africa about 136,000. Hundreds of thousands of Africans served as soldiers and, in far greater numbers, as carriers in the East African campaign, where mortality among carriers was appalling.

Three consequences followed, all corrosive.

**First, the Dominions came home changed.** Gallipoli (1915) and Vimy Ridge (1917) became founding national myths precisely because they were experienced as *national*, not imperial. The Dominions signed the Treaty of Versailles separately and joined the League of Nations in their own right.

**Second, the promises came due.** Britain had made incompatible commitments to buy support: to Sharif Hussein of Mecca (the McMahon correspondence, 1915), to France (Sykes–Picot, 1916), and to the Zionist movement (the **Balfour Declaration, 2 November 1917**, promising "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine while stipulating that nothing should prejudice "the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities"). The mandate that followed was thus structurally impossible from the day it began, and Britain spent 28 years failing to square it.

**Third, and most important, India changed.** Indians had been told they fought for self-determination. What they received was the Government of India Act 1919 — a cautious "diarchy" sharing some provincial powers — accompanied by the Rowlatt Acts extending wartime emergency detention. On **13 April 1919** at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer ordered fifty riflemen to fire without warning into a walled enclosure packed with an unarmed crowd. The official Hunter Commission put the dead at 379; the Indian National Congress estimated over a thousand. Dyer was relieved of command but received a public subscription of some £26,000 raised by admirers in Britain.

Amritsar is the moment moderate Indian opinion broke. Mohandas Gandhi, until then a loyalist who had recruited for the war effort, launched the first of his mass non-cooperation movements in 1920. The Congress ceased to be a debating club of lawyers and became a mass party.

### Ireland: the first modern decolonisation

The Easter Rising of 1916 was militarily hopeless and politically transformative, largely because of the executions that followed. Sinn Féin swept the 1918 election in Ireland, established its own parliament, and fought a guerrilla war from 1919 to 1921 against police, army and the notorious "Black and Tans". The **Anglo-Irish Treaty of 6 December 1921** created the Irish Free State as a Dominion on 6 December 1922, partitioning six northern counties which remained in the United Kingdom; a civil war between pro- and anti-Treaty forces followed. Ireland then walked steadily away: a new constitution in 1937, neutrality in the Second World War, and the Republic of Ireland Act taking effect in 1949.

Ireland established the template: guerrilla war Britain could suppress but not end at acceptable cost; negotiated Dominion status short of the demand; partition as the price; and a long constitutional drift to full sovereignty afterwards. Every element recurs.

### Redefining the family: 1926 and 1931

The Dominions now needed a new legal status to match their real independence. The Imperial Conference of 1926 produced the **Balfour Definition**, one of the most quoted sentences in imperial history:

> "They are autonomous Communities within the British Empire, equal in status, in no way subordinate one to another in any aspect of their domestic or external affairs, though united by a common allegiance to the Crown, and freely associated as members of the British Commonwealth of Nations."

The **Statute of Westminster (11 December 1931)** made it law. Note the racial line running through this: equality was extended to the settler Dominions and withheld from India, whose Congress had already declared for *purna swaraj*, complete independence, in January 1930. The Commonwealth was, at its creation, a white club — and that fact set the terms of the argument for the next twenty years.

The 1930s brought a defensive turn. The Depression shattered free trade; at Ottawa in 1932 Britain and the Dominions built a system of **imperial preference**, and the sterling area drew the empire's finances into a bloc. The empire was becoming an economic fortress at precisely the moment its military position became indefensible against three simultaneous enemies.

### 1939–1945: the empire wins the war and loses itself

The empire's contribution was again enormous. The Indian Army expanded to about **2.5 million volunteers**, the largest volunteer army in history. African troops fought in Burma and Ethiopia. The Dominions supplied armies, air crews and food.

But the war exposed the empire's central weakness with brutal clarity.

- **Singapore fell on 15 February 1942**, with around 80,000 troops surrendering to a smaller Japanese force. Churchill called it the worst disaster in British military history. Its symbolic effect across Asia was immeasurable: the guarantee of European invincibility had been publicly voided by an Asian power.
- **Britain went bankrupt.** By 1945 the country owed some £3.4 billion in "sterling balances" — much of it to India and Egypt, who had supplied goods and services on credit. **The empire was now Britain's creditor rather than its asset.**
- **The Bengal famine of 1943** killed an estimated 2.1 to 3 million people. Its causes were multiple — the loss of Burmese rice imports, cyclone damage, hoarding, provincial trade barriers — but wartime shipping decisions taken in London, and Churchill's own reluctance to divert vessels, made relief slower and thinner than it needed to be. Whatever weight one assigns to each cause, a government that cannot feed its subjects has forfeited the central claim of its own propaganda.
- **The war's ideology cut against empire.** The Atlantic Charter of August 1941 affirmed "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live". Churchill insisted this did not apply to the empire; Roosevelt, and every nationalist leader who read a newspaper, thought otherwise. At the Mansion House on 10 November 1942 Churchill declared: "I have not become the King's First Minister in order to preside over the liquidation of the British Empire." He was in office when the largest part of it was liquidated.
- **India withdrew its consent.** The Quit India movement of August 1942 was suppressed with some 90,000 arrests, but suppression required troops Britain could not spare, and it made clear that after the war India would be governed only by force. When Indian soldiers of the Japanese-sponsored Indian National Army were put on trial in 1945–46, they were widely treated as heroes; and in February 1946 the Royal Indian Navy mutinied at Bombay. That is the moment the game ended. **The instrument of British rule in India — Indian personnel — could no longer be relied upon.**

## 10. Dissolution I: The Asian Hinge, 1947–1957

The Labour government elected in July 1945 was not straightforwardly anti-imperial; it hoped to *reform* the empire into a development project that would help pay for the welfare state. But India was beyond reform.

Negotiations foundered on a question Britain had spent decades making harder: what happened to India's Muslims, roughly a quarter of the population, in a democracy dominated by Congress? Muhammad Ali Jinnah's Muslim League, having demanded a separate state since the Lahore Resolution of 1940, called Direct Action Day on 16 August 1946; the ensuing communal killing in Calcutta and beyond convinced Britain that time had run out. Lord Mountbatten, arriving in March 1947, brought the transfer date forward from June 1948 to **15 August 1947**. Cyril Radcliffe, who had never been to India, was given five weeks to draw a border through Punjab and Bengal; his award was published two days *after* independence.

Independence came at midnight on 14/15 August 1947, and Jawaharlal Nehru spoke the words every Indian schoolchild still learns: "At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom."

The cost was appalling. Between **10 and 12 million people** were displaced in the largest forced migration in modern history; deaths from communal violence are estimated between several hundred thousand and two million, with around a million the most commonly cited figure. Historians argue fiercely about responsibility: British haste, the failure to provide adequate troops for the boundary areas, decades of governing through religious categories and separate electorates, and the decisions of Indian and Pakistani politicians all belong in the account.

Then the cascade. **Burma independent on 4 January 1948** — and, uniquely among the major territories, it left the Commonwealth entirely. **Ceylon on 4 February 1948.** The **Palestine mandate surrendered** to the United Nations and abandoned at midnight on 14/15 May 1948, with Britain unable to reconcile its commitments and unwilling to pay the cost of holding the ring; war followed immediately.

One brilliant improvisation kept the structure alive. When India resolved to become a republic, membership of a Commonwealth defined by allegiance to the Crown became impossible. The **London Declaration of April 1949** solved it: republics could belong, with the monarch recognised simply as "Head of the Commonwealth". At a stroke the white club became a multiracial association — and gained an African and Asian majority that would, within a decade, be used to pressure Britain over Rhodesia and South Africa.

Not everything was surrendered. In Malaya, whose tin and rubber earned dollars the sterling area desperately needed, Britain fought the **Malayan Emergency (1948–60)** against a largely Chinese-supported communist insurgency, resettling around half a million people into guarded "New Villages". Britain won — the rare counter-insurgency success — and then handed power in **1957** to the conservative, Malay-led government the campaign had cultivated. Malaya shows the pattern at its clearest: fight the nationalists you cannot live with; hand over to the ones you can.

## 11. Dissolution II: Suez, the Wind of Change and the African Exit, 1956–1968

### Suez: the hinge of the whole story

In July 1956 Egypt's President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal Company. Britain, France and Israel secretly agreed a plan at Sèvres: Israel would invade Sinai, and the two European powers would intervene as "peacekeepers" to seize the canal. The operation began on 29 October 1956. Militarily it worked.

Politically it was a catastrophe. The United States, furious at being deceived and at the timing (the Soviet invasion of Hungary was under way), refused to support sterling. A run on the pound began. Washington blocked Britain's access to IMF credit until it agreed to withdraw. Britain accepted a ceasefire on 6 November and withdrew by December. Prime Minister Anthony Eden resigned in January 1957.

Suez did not by itself cause decolonisation — plans for African independence were already forming. What it did was settle three questions permanently: Britain could not act against American wishes; Britain could not hold a position against determined nationalism plus international opinion; and the psychological premise of great-power status had been publicly falsified. In 1962 the American Secretary of State Dean Acheson made the wound public in a speech at West Point: "Great Britain has lost an Empire and has not yet found a role."

### The wind of change

Sudan had already become independent on **1 January 1956**. On **6 March 1957** the Gold Coast became **Ghana**, the first sub-Saharan African colony to gain independence, under Kwame Nkrumah, whose slogan captured the strategy of a generation: "Seek ye first the political kingdom, and all things shall be added unto it."

On **3 February 1960**, addressing the South African parliament in Cape Town, Harold Macmillan delivered the speech that named the decade: "The wind of change is blowing through this continent, and, whether we like it or not, this growth of national consciousness is a political fact. We must all accept it as a fact, and our national policies must take account of it."

Then came the flood. **Nigeria (1 October 1960)**, Sierra Leone (27 April 1961), Tanganyika (9 December 1961), Jamaica (6 August 1962), Trinidad and Tobago (31 August 1962), Uganda (9 October 1962), **Kenya (12 December 1963)**, Malawi (6 July 1964), Zambia (24 October 1964), Malta (21 September 1964), the Gambia (18 February 1965), Guyana (26 May 1966), Botswana (30 September 1966), Lesotho (4 October 1966), Barbados (30 November 1966), Mauritius (12 March 1968), Swaziland (6 September 1968). In roughly a decade Britain divested itself of most of an empire it had spent three centuries assembling.

Why so fast? Because a hard-headed calculation had changed. In 1957 Macmillan asked the Cabinet Office for a cost-benefit analysis of colonial possessions; the answer was, essentially, that it made little economic difference either way. Colonies were no longer obviously profitable; holding them against nationalist movements was expensive and, after France's experience in Algeria (1954–62) and Britain's own in Kenya and Cyprus, visibly ugly. Meanwhile Britain's economic future was being redefined as European: the first application to join the EEC came in 1961. Trade with Europe was growing; trade with the empire was not. And Britain's Cold War priority was to keep new states out of the Soviet camp, which meant granting independence *early enough* to hand power to a friendly government rather than a radicalised one.

### Case study: Kenya, or the violence at the end

Kenya is the place to look if you want to understand what happened where a substantial white settler population stood in the way.

Settlers had been granted the fertile "White Highlands" from the 1900s, dispossessing Kikuyu farmers and creating a landless class. The **Mau Mau uprising (Emergency declared October 1952)** was as much a Kikuyu civil war as an anti-colonial revolt. The British response was systematic. Around 1.5 million Kikuyu were moved into fortified villages or detention camps in a network known as "the Pipeline". Over 1,000 people were hanged. Detainees were subjected to beatings, forced labour and, as documents later released proved, torture. In 1959 eleven detainees were beaten to death at Hola camp, and the scandal helped destroy the political case for holding on.

Compare the death tolls: 32 European settlers were killed by Mau Mau over the whole Emergency. Official figures put insurgent deaths above 11,000; independent estimates run far higher.

In 2011 a group of elderly Kenyans sued the British government. During the case the Foreign Office admitted it held a secret archive of colonial-era documents at Hanslope Park — thousands of files removed from colonies at independence in a systematic purge codenamed, in Kenya's case, Operation Legacy. In June 2013 the government announced a settlement of **£19.9 million to 5,228 claimants** and the Foreign Secretary, William Hague, expressed regret in the House of Commons for torture and ill-treatment.

Kenya became independent on 12 December 1963 under Jomo Kenyatta — a man the British had imprisoned as a Mau Mau leader on evidence widely regarded as fabricated, and who then governed as a conservative ally of Britain and the West.

## 12. Dissolution III: The Long Tail, 1968–1997

Decolonisation did not stop in 1968; it dwindled.

In **January 1968**, in the wake of devaluation, the Wilson government announced withdrawal from military commitments **"East of Suez"**. It was an accountant's decision with strategic consequences: the Gulf protectorates, defended by Britain for 150 years, became independent during 1971 as Bahrain (August), Qatar (September) and the United Arab Emirates (December). Aden had already gone on 30 November 1967, after a four-year insurgency, becoming South Yemen — one of the few departures that resembled a defeat.

Two cases refused to resolve cleanly.

**Rhodesia.** On 11 November 1965 Ian Smith's white-minority government issued a **Unilateral Declaration of Independence** rather than accept Britain's condition of eventual majority rule. Britain refused to use force against fellow Britons — a decision that enraged African Commonwealth members — and relied on sanctions that leaked. It took a fourteen-year guerrilla war by ZANU and ZAPU, and the collapse of Portuguese rule in Mozambique, to force the Lancaster House settlement of 1979. **Zimbabwe became independent on 18 April 1980.**

**The Falklands.** On 2 April 1982 Argentina invaded the islands, whose 1,800 inhabitants were determined to remain British. Britain retook them in a ten-week war costing 255 British and 649 Argentine lives. It was, in a strict sense, an anti-decolonisation war — fought to keep a territory — and it is the clearest evidence that "empire" and "self-determination" cannot always be sorted into opposing columns.

The last significant Caribbean and Pacific territories went in the late 1970s and early 1980s: the Bahamas (1973), Grenada (1974), Seychelles (1976), Solomon Islands, Tuvalu and Dominica (1978), St Lucia, St Vincent and Kiribati (1979), Vanuatu and Zimbabwe (1980), Belize and Antigua and Barbuda (1981), St Kitts and Nevis (1983), Brunei (1984).

And then the last great transfer. Hong Kong Island had been ceded in 1842 and Kowloon in 1860, but the overwhelming majority of the colony's land area was the **New Territories, leased for 99 years in 1898**. The lease expired on 30 June 1997, and the colony was not viable without it. The **Sino-British Joint Declaration of 19 December 1984** provided for the transfer of the whole territory under a "one country, two systems" formula. At midnight on **30 June/1 July 1997**, in torrential rain, the flag came down. Britain's Asian empire had begun with a company's trading post and ended with a lease running out.

Fourteen **British Overseas Territories** remain, from Bermuda to Pitcairn to the Falklands to the Sovereign Base Areas on Cyprus. Most are small and want to stay. One is not simple: the Chagos Archipelago, detached from Mauritius in 1965 to create the British Indian Ocean Territory, whose entire population was removed between 1968 and 1973 so that the United States could build a base on Diego Garcia. The International Court of Justice held in a 2019 advisory opinion that the detachment was unlawful, and in May 2025 Britain signed an agreement transferring sovereignty to Mauritius while leasing back the base. The empire's last chapters are still being written.

## 13. Why Did It End? Four Explanations Weighed

Examiners love this question, and the best answers refuse to choose only one.

**1. Metropolitan decline: Britain could no longer afford it.** Strong. Two world wars converted the world's greatest creditor into a major debtor; by 1945 Britain owed billions to its own colonies. Defence commitments outran resources from 1902 onwards. The 1957 Defence White Paper and the 1968 East of Suez decision were driven by budgets. *But* Britain also chose to spend heavily on defence into the 1960s, and fought expensive campaigns in Malaya, Kenya and Cyprus. Poverty explains the direction of travel better than the timing.

**2. Peripheral nationalism: the colonised drove Britain out.** Strong, and indispensable. Congress, the Muslim League, Sinn Féin, the Convention People's Party, EOKA, ZANU: without organised mass politics there is no decolonisation. *But* nationalist movements were often weak, divided, and in some places (the Gulf, much of the Caribbean) barely demanding independence when it was granted. In several territories Britain left before it was pushed.

**3. International pressure: a changed world would not permit empire.** Strong for the post-1945 period. The United States was structurally anti-colonial (though inconsistently so, preferring stable allies to swift independence); the Soviet Union offered nationalists an alternative patron; the United Nations, with a growing Afro-Asian bloc, made colonialism diplomatically indefensible — Resolution 1514 of 1960 declared it a denial of fundamental human rights. Suez proved the constraint was real.

**4. Loss of will and of collaborators.** The subtlest and, in this chapter's view, the most powerful. Empire always depended on local intermediaries: Indian princes, African chiefs, Malay sultans, Egyptian officials, and above all Indian and African soldiers and clerks. When those intermediaries could get more from nationalism than from Britain, the machinery of rule seized. Simultaneously, the metropolitan belief that ruling others was legitimate — which had been robust in 1900 — had largely evaporated by 1960, corroded by the war against Nazi racism, by the welfare state's redefinition of what government owed people, and by the sheer indefensibility of the imperial position in public argument.

> **► THINK ABOUT THIS**
> Historians Wm. Roger Louis and Ronald Robinson coined the phrase "the imperialism of decolonization" to describe how Britain sought to preserve influence, bases and commercial position while surrendering formal sovereignty. If a country becomes independent but its currency, its army's training, its export markets and its elite's education remain oriented towards London, how much has changed? Now consider the counter-argument: is that framing at risk of denying new nations the agency they had just won?

## 14. The Ledger: What the Empire Did

A serious chapter must state consequences without flinching in either direction.

**It killed and coerced at scale.** The slave trade; the suppression of 1857; the famines in Ireland (1845–49) and India (1876–78, 1896–1902, Bengal 1943), where colonial commitments to market ideology and, in 1943, wartime priorities turned harvest failures into mass death; the concentration camps of the South African War; Amritsar; the Kenyan detention camps. Colonial rule was ultimately a rule of force, and when consent failed the force appeared quickly.

**It moved people, mostly not by choice.** Around three million Africans carried on British ships. Over a million Indians shipped under indenture. More than twenty million emigrants from the British Isles between 1815 and 1914. Then the return current: the *Empire Windrush* docking at Tilbury on 22 June 1948 with 1,027 passengers, mostly Caribbean, travelling as British subjects — a status the British Nationality Act, passed weeks later in 1948, would codify as citizenship of the United Kingdom and Colonies. Modern multicultural Britain is a direct product of empire — the empire coming home.

**It extracted, and it also invested — and the balance is contested.** India's share of British trade, the drain of wealth identified by Dadabhai Naoroji in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901), the deindustrialisation of Indian textiles: on one side. Railways (India had the world's fourth-largest network by 1947), irrigation, ports, universities: on the other. But note *who paid* and *who decided*. India's railways were built with capital guaranteed a return by Indian taxpayers, routed to serve export and military needs. Investment is not the same as development, and neither is the same as consent.

**It left institutions, languages and borders.** English as a world language; common law; parliamentary forms; cricket; the Commonwealth of 56 member states, which now includes countries — Mozambique, Rwanda, Gabon, Togo — that were never British at all. And borders: the Radcliffe Line, the Durand Line, the ruler-straight frontiers of Africa, the unresolved geography of Palestine and Cyprus. Many of the world's most dangerous fault lines were drawn in London, Cairo or Simla in a hurry.

**And it was, from the beginning, argued about.** Burke against Hastings; abolitionists against the slave lobby; Hobhouse against Kitchener; the Congress of 1885 using English liberal arguments against English rule. There was never a settled British consensus that the empire was good. Judging the past by the standards of the present is a real historical error — but the record shows that the past contained plenty of people applying critical standards at the time.

## 15. Summary: The Argument in Twelve Sentences

1. The British Empire began as a commercial and maritime enterprise in the Atlantic, and its first great engine was sugar produced by enslaved Africans.
2. Britain repeatedly expanded by delegating empire to chartered companies because it did not want to pay for it, and repeatedly had to nationalise the results.
3. War with France, not design, delivered the largest single accessions (1713, 1763, 1815).
4. The loss of America in 1783 showed that empires break when the metropolis tries to make colonies pay their own costs — and that trade could survive independence.
5. Conquest in India was achieved through collaboration, Indian soldiers and Indian revenue, which is why 1857 and 1946 were both existential crises.
6. Abolition in 1807 and 1833 was genuine and was also entangled with compensation to owners, indentured labour, and a new licence for intervention in Africa.
7. Responsible government from 1848 secured settler loyalty by transferring power to settlers — at the direct expense of indigenous peoples.
8. The mid-Victorian empire preferred informal influence to formal rule; the Scramble for Africa was driven by rivalry, strategy, men on the spot, and a collapse in the cost of conquest.
9. The empire reached its maximum extent in 1922 at the very moment two world wars destroyed the financial and military basis for holding it.
10. Indian independence in 1947 was the hinge: it removed the manpower, the revenue and the strategic purpose that had organised the whole eastern system.
11. Suez in 1956 destroyed the illusion of independent great-power action; the African exit that followed was rapid because the cost-benefit calculation, the international climate, and the availability of acceptable successors had all changed together.
12. The empire ended for the same reason it grew: because the balance between profit, cost and consent shifted — and because the people it ruled, who had always been essential to it, withdrew their cooperation.

> **► FINAL MARGIN QUESTION**
> Return to your answer to Question 1.1. Would you now defend "absence of mind" as a description of *acquisition*? Would you use it of *dissolution*? Write 150 words on whether the same phrase can fairly describe both halves of the story.

## 17. Further Reading

**Start here**
- John Darwin, *Unfinished Empire: The Global Expansion of Britain* (Allen Lane, 2012). The best single book for a student: thematic, argumentative, sceptical of grand designs.
- Ashley Jackson, *The British Empire: A Very Short Introduction* (OUP, 2013). Genuinely short, genuinely good.
- Piers Brendon, *The Decline and Fall of the British Empire, 1781–1997* (Cape, 2007). Narrative, readable, mordant.

**Going deeper**
- John Darwin, *The Empire Project: The Rise and Fall of the British World-System, 1830–1970* (CUP, 2009).
- C. A. Bayly, *Imperial Meridian: The British Empire and the World, 1780–1830* (Longman, 1989).
- P. J. Cain and A. G. Hopkins, *British Imperialism, 1688–2015* (3rd edn, Routledge, 2016).
- Wm. Roger Louis (ed.), *The Oxford History of the British Empire*, 5 vols (OUP, 1998–99), plus the Companion Series volumes on gender, environment, migration and Ireland.
- John Gallagher and Ronald Robinson, "The Imperialism of Free Trade", *Economic History Review* 6:1 (1953) — a short article that changed the field.
- Ronald Hyam, *Britain's Declining Empire: The Road to Decolonisation, 1918–1968* (CUP, 2006).

**Slavery, resistance and the Atlantic**
- Olaudah Equiano, *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano* (1789).
- Eric Williams, *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944) — the thesis that slave profits financed industrialisation; much contested, still indispensable.
- Catherine Hall et al., *Legacies of British Slave-ownership* (CUP, 2014), and the UCL database of the same name.
- Vincent Brown, *Tacky's Revolt: The Story of an Atlantic Slave War* (Harvard, 2020).

**India**
- Dadabhai Naoroji, *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901).
- Yasmin Khan, *The Great Partition: The Making of India and Pakistan* (Yale, 2007).
- Jon Wilson, *India Conquered: Britain's Raj and the Chaos of Empire* (Simon & Schuster, 2016).
- Shashi Tharoor, *Inglorious Empire: What the British Did to India* (Hurst, 2017).

**Africa, violence and the end**
- David Anderson, *Histories of the Hanged: Britain's Dirty War in Kenya* (Weidenfeld, 2005).
- Caroline Elkins, *Britain's Gulag: The Brutal End of Empire in Kenya* (Cape, 2005) and *Legacy of Violence: A History of the British Empire* (Bodley Head, 2022).
- Thomas Pakenham, *The Scramble for Africa* (Weidenfeld, 1991).
- Frederick Cooper, *Africa Since 1940: The Past of the Present* (CUP, 2002).

**The argument about the argument**
- Niall Ferguson, *Empire: How Britain Made the Modern World* (Allen Lane, 2003) — read alongside Tharoor and Elkins, not instead of them.
- Kwasi Kwarteng, *Ghosts of Empire* (Bloomsbury, 2011).
- Sathnam Sanghera, *Empireland* (Viking, 2021) — on the empire's afterlife in modern Britain.

**Databases and primary material**
- *Slave Voyages* (slavevoyages.org) — the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database; every voyage figure in this chapter can be checked there.
- *Legacies of British Slavery* database, University College London.
- Hansard (hansard.parliament.uk) — the full text of the debates on abolition, Amritsar, Suez and independence.
