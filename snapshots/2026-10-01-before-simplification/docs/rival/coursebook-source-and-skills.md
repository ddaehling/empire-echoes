## 1. How to use this chapter

This chapter does two jobs at once. It tells you **what happened** — which territories the British acquired, when, by what means, and how each of them ceased to be British. And it trains you to **argue about what happened**, because that is what examiners actually reward.

You will meet three kinds of box:

> **SOURCE** boxes contain real extracts from documents written at the time, with full attribution. Always read the attribution line first. Who wrote it? For whom? Why then?

> **INTERPRETATION** boxes contain the views of modern historians, named. Historians disagree. Your job is not to pick a favourite but to explain *why* they disagree — usually because they are asking different questions, using different evidence, or writing in different political weather.

> **THINK** boxes are margin questions. Do not skip them. They are where the chapter turns from reading into thinking.

A word of warning about a bad habit. Many students write about empire as though the only available question were "was it good or bad?" That question is not wrong, but on its own it is thin, and it produces essays that read like verdicts rather than history. The stronger habit is to ask: *how did this work?* Who benefited, who paid, who cooperated, who resisted, what were the alternatives available to people at the time, and what did contemporaries think they were doing? Moral judgement is best earned at the end of an analysis, not asserted at the start of one.

## 2. The problem: what was "the British Empire"?

Start with a difficulty, because the difficulty is the point.

There was never a single thing called the British Empire in the way there was a single thing called the Roman legion or the Bank of England. There was no founding document, no empire-wide constitution, no single ministry that ran it all. By 1900 the same monarch was, simultaneously: the constitutional queen of a self-governing white settler dominion in Canada; the Empress of India ruling 300 million people through a viceroy and a career bureaucracy of a few thousand British officials; the distant sovereign of Caribbean islands run by planter assemblies; the treaty-partner of several hundred Indian princes who kept their thrones and lost their foreign policy; the protector of Malay sultans; and the nominal superior of chartered companies with their own armies. Egypt from 1882 was occupied by British troops and run by a British "adviser" while remaining, on paper, a province of the Ottoman Empire. That is not a system. That is an accumulation.

A rough sense of scale, however, is essential.

| Measure | Figure | Date | Source of estimate |
|---|---|---|---|

Two sentences are worth memorising, because they frame every argument in this chapter. First: the empire was built over roughly 350 years and dismantled in roughly 35. Second: for most of its history it was governed not mainly by British soldiers and officials — of whom there were never enough — but through **local intermediaries** who found it worth their while to cooperate. Both facts should make you suspicious of any story in which a monolithic Britain simply imposes its will on passive victims, and equally suspicious of any story in which empire was a benign administrative service that happened to involve a great deal of gunfire.

## 3. Key terms: a working glossary

Learn these. Examiners notice precision, and half of all confusion about empire is vocabulary confusion.

| Term | Meaning | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Colony** | Territory under direct British sovereignty, governed from London through a governor. | "Crown colony" (governor rules with appointed council) is not the same as "colony with responsible government" (elected ministers). |
| **Protectorate** | A state that keeps its ruler and nominal sovereignty but surrenders foreign policy and often defence to Britain. | Cheaper than a colony, and legally different: protectorate inhabitants were British *protected persons*, not British subjects. |
| **Dominion** | A self-governing settler state — Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the Irish Free State, Newfoundland — with its own parliament and, after 1931, full legal autonomy. | The word implies whiteness in practice: no dominion status was granted to a non-settler colony before 1947. |
| **Mandate** | Territory taken from Germany or the Ottoman Empire after 1918 and administered under League of Nations supervision, in theory as a trust. | Critics at the time and since called it "empire with paperwork". The supervision was real but weak. |
| **Indirect rule** | Governing through existing chiefs, emirs or princes, whose authority Britain guaranteed in exchange for obedience. Associated with Frederick Lugard in Northern Nigeria. | It often *invented* or hardened "traditional" authority that had been more fluid before. |
| **Informal empire** | Countries never coloured red on the map but dominated economically and, when necessary, militarily — Argentina, Ottoman Egypt before 1882, Qing China's treaty ports. | The central concept of Gallagher and Robinson's 1953 argument. |
| **Settler colonialism** | Colonisation whose logic is the permanent replacement of an indigenous population on the land, rather than the extraction of labour or tribute from it. | Explains why Australia, New Zealand and North America look demographically different from India or Nigeria. |
| **Chartered company** | A private joint-stock company granted monopoly trading rights and, sometimes, powers of government and war: the East India Company (1600), Royal African Company (1672), British South Africa Company (1889). | Empire was frequently privatised before it was nationalised. |
| **Diwani** | The right to collect land revenue in a Mughal province. Granted to the East India Company for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765. | The moment a trading company became a tax-collecting state. |
| **Indentured labour** | Contract labour, mainly from India and China after 1834, bound for a fixed term (typically five years) on plantations. | Legally free, practically coerced; historians debate where it sits on the spectrum between wage work and slavery. |
| **Responsible government** | The principle that colonial ministers answer to an elected colonial legislature rather than to London. First granted to Nova Scotia and the Province of Canada in 1848. | The hinge between "colony" and "dominion". |
| **Decolonisation** | The transfer of sovereignty from imperial power to successor state. | A misleadingly tidy word for a process that included negotiation, riot, insurgency, partition and civil war. |

## 5. The empire described as a map

Close your eyes and build it. The old classroom maps coloured British territory pink or red; the joke was that the sun never set on the empire, which was literally true from the early nineteenth century onwards because British possessions were spread across every line of longitude.

**The Atlantic ring.** Start at Newfoundland, the cod fishery claimed in 1583, hanging off North America. Run down the eastern seaboard — thirteen colonies from Massachusetts to Georgia, lost in 1783 — then across to the sugar islands strung through the Caribbean like beads: Barbados furthest east, Jamaica largest and most valuable, with St Kitts, Nevis, Antigua, Montserrat, Grenada, Trinidad and Tobago and British Guiana on the South American mainland. Complete the triangle by crossing to West Africa: the slaving forts of the Gold Coast, the Gambia river, Sierra Leone, and eventually Lagos and the vast Nigerian interior. This ring is the empire of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and its engine was sugar and enslaved labour.

**The route to the East.** Now follow the sea road that mattered after 1783. Gibraltar (1704) guards the mouth of the Mediterranean; Malta (1800) sits in its middle; from 1869 the Suez Canal cuts through Egypt, occupied from 1882; Aden (1839) commands the exit of the Red Sea. Alternatively, before the canal, go the long way: the Cape of Good Hope (1806), then Mauritius (1810), then Ceylon (1796–1815). Either route ends in India — Bengal in the north-east, Madras in the south-east, Bombay in the west — and continues past Burma (annexed in three wars, 1826, 1852, 1885) to Penang, Singapore (1819) and Hong Kong (1842). This is the spine of the "Second Empire", and it is essentially a chain of naval bases protecting a single enormous asset.

**The settler world.** Separately, and with a different logic, there is the temperate zone where British families went to stay: Canada from the Atlantic to the Pacific after 1867; Australia's six colonies federating in 1901; New Zealand; and the Cape and Natal, later joined to the Boer republics in the Union of South Africa in 1910. These places acquired their own parliaments early and their own foreign policies eventually.

**Africa, late and fast.** Finally, the block that arrived last and left first. Before 1880 Britain held little of Africa beyond coastal enclaves and the Cape. Within twenty-five years it held Egypt, Sudan, Kenya, Uganda, Zanzibar, Nyasaland, Northern and Southern Rhodesia, Bechuanaland, Nigeria, the Gold Coast, Sierra Leone and the Gambia — Cecil Rhodes's fantasy of a red line from Cairo to the Cape almost, but never quite, complete because German East Africa sat in the way until 1918.

> **THINK 5.1** — Notice that the Atlantic empire was acquired over roughly two centuries and the African empire over roughly twenty-five years. What might explain the difference in pace? (Consider: steamships, quinine, the machine gun, the telegraph, and European rivalry.)

## 6. Part I — The First Empire, 1583–1783: Atlantic, sugar and slavery

### 6.1 The Irish laboratory

English expansion did not begin in America. It began next door. The Tudor conquest of Ireland — the Munster plantation from 1586, the defeat of Hugh O'Neill in 1603, and above all the Ulster plantation from 1609 — established a repertoire that would travel: confiscate land from a population defined as barbarous, grant it to loyal settlers, justify it by religion and civility, and garrison the result. Several of the men who promoted American colonisation, including Humphrey Gilbert and Walter Raleigh, had served in Ireland first.

This matters for two reasons. It shows that "empire" and "the British Isles" were never cleanly separable — Scotland joined England by the Union of 1707, Ireland by the Union of 1801, and Scots would be wildly over-represented among imperial administrators, soldiers, missionaries and merchants. And it means the empire's longest colonial relationship, and its most contested, ran from the sixteenth century to 1922 and arguably to the present.

### 6.2 Sugar, and the machinery of enslavement

The Caribbean, not North America, was the profitable part of the early empire. Barbados was settled in 1627, and within a generation its planters had switched from tobacco to sugar cane, a crop that demands enormous, brutal, coordinated labour at harvest. The result was a demographic revolution: by the 1680s enslaved Africans substantially outnumbered white settlers on the island. Jamaica, seized from Spain in 1655 and formally ceded in 1670, became the largest British sugar colony of all.

The trade that supplied these islands was, for a time, a state-sanctioned monopoly. The Royal African Company, chartered in 1672 with the Duke of York (later James II) as governor, held exclusive rights until deregulation in 1698 opened the trade to all English merchants — after which volumes rose sharply. Bristol, and then overwhelmingly Liverpool, became the great slaving ports.

> **SOURCE A**
> *Olaudah Equiano, an African writer and abolitionist, describing the Middle Passage in his autobiography, published in London in 1789. Equiano stated he was born in Igboland and enslaved as a child; some scholars, notably Vincent Carretta, have argued from baptismal and naval records that he may have been born in South Carolina. The book was a bestseller and a deliberate contribution to the abolition campaign.*
>
> "The shrieks of the women, and the groans of the dying, rendered the whole a scene of horror almost inconceivable."

> **THINK 6.1 — Source utility drill**
> (a) What does Source A tell us that a ship's logbook could not?
> (b) Does the scholarly dispute about Equiano's birthplace destroy the source's value, reduce it, or change *what* it is useful for? Be precise: distinguish between its value as testimony of the Middle Passage and its value as evidence about the abolition campaign.
> (c) A well-trained answer will note that a source written *to persuade* is not thereby unreliable — it is reliable evidence of what abolitionists thought would persuade.

The scale, in numbers you should be able to quote: British-flagged vessels embarked roughly 3.1–3.3 million enslaved Africans between the 1660s and 1807; roughly 2.7 million survived to be landed, meaning some 400,000–500,000 died at sea. These figures come from the *Slave Voyages* database, built from surviving ship records — which means they are minimums, and the database's compilers say so.

Two complications that separate a good answer from a sixth-form cliché. First, enslavement in West Africa was not simply something done *to* Africa by Europeans: African states and merchants — Dahomey, Asante, the Aro networks of the Niger delta — were active suppliers, for their own reasons, using their own wars. Recognising this does not diminish European responsibility for the demand, the finance, the shipping and the plantation regime; it does complicate any account in which Africans have no agency. Second, resistance was continuous — shipboard revolts, poisoning, flight, and the maroon communities of Jamaica who fought the British to a treaty in 1739 and were never conquered.

### 6.3 Company, not Crown: how Britain acquired India

While the Atlantic empire grew by settlement and conquest, the Eastern empire grew by accident of commerce. The **East India Company** was chartered on 31 December 1600 as the "Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies" — a monopoly for a group of investors, not an arm of the state. For 150 years it was a trading concern with fortified warehouses at Madras (1639), Bombay (from 1668) and Calcutta (1690), operating with the permission of Mughal authority.

What changed was the collapse of that authority. After the death of the emperor Aurangzeb in 1707 the Mughal empire fragmented into effectively independent successor states, all of them raising armies and needing money. Into this competitive market stepped European companies with access to credit, artillery and disciplined infantry. The Company did not defeat "India"; it took sides in Indian politics.

The pivot is **Plassey, 23 June 1757**, where Robert Clive's force of roughly 3,000 defeated the Nawab of Bengal's army of perhaps 50,000 — because a large part of that army, under Mir Jafar, had been bought in advance and did not fight. Seven years later at **Buxar (1764)** the Company beat a coalition of Indian rulers in a genuine battle. On **12 August 1765** the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II granted the Company the **diwani** — the right to collect land revenue — of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. A trading company had become the tax authority of a territory with more people than Britain.

The consequences were immediate and terrible. The **Bengal famine of 1770** killed a catastrophic share of the province's population; the traditional estimate of ten million, roughly one-third, derives from contemporary British reports and is regarded by modern demographers as very uncertain, but no one disputes the scale. Revenue collection continued through it.

> **SOURCE B**
> *Edmund Burke MP, speech on Fox's East India Bill, House of Commons, 1 December 1783. Burke spent years prosecuting the Company and its former Governor-General Warren Hastings.*
>
> "The Company in India does not exist as a commonwealth… but as a state in the disguise of a merchant."

> **THINK 6.2** — Burke was a British MP attacking a British institution in Parliament, using language of tyranny and corruption. Does the existence of loud, principled criticism *inside* Britain support the argument that the empire was self-correcting, or does it undermine it — given that the Company kept its Indian territories for another 75 years? Argue both sides in a paragraph each.

Parliament responded not by ending Company rule but by regulating it: the **Regulating Act (1773)** and **Pitt's India Act (1784)** created a Board of Control in London and a Governor-General in Calcutta. This is a pattern worth noticing: British reform of empire almost always meant tightening state supervision of empire, not surrendering it.

### 6.4 The empire that broke: America, 1763–1783

Britain won the Seven Years' War spectacularly. The Treaty of Paris (10 February 1763) delivered Canada, Florida, and dominance in India. Within twenty years it had lost the thirteen mainland American colonies. The reason is one of the most useful causal stories in the whole subject, because it shows that empire cost money.

Victory left an enormous war debt and a much larger territory to garrison. Parliament concluded that colonists who had benefited should contribute: the Sugar Act (1764), the Stamp Act (1765), the Townshend duties (1767), and after the Boston Tea Party of 16 December 1773, the Coercive Acts (1774). Colonists — many of them prosperous, literate, and steeped in the same English constitutional tradition — replied with the claim that taxation required representation. War began at Lexington and Concord on 19 April 1775; independence was declared on 4 July 1776; the decisive surrender came at Yorktown on 19 October 1781, made possible by French naval intervention; and the Treaty of Paris of 3 September 1783 recognised the United States.

Three points that stronger candidates make:

- **It was a world war, not a colonial rebellion.** France, Spain and the Dutch Republic all fought Britain; Minorca and Florida were lost to Spain. Britain lost America partly because it temporarily lost command of the sea.
- **Loyalism was real.** Roughly 60,000 loyalists left, many to Nova Scotia and Upper Canada, including several thousand Black loyalists who had been promised freedom for fighting for the Crown — some of whom were later resettled in Sierra Leone. Modern Canada is partly a product of American independence.
- **Losing America did not end the empire.** Trade with the independent United States grew. This is the strongest single piece of evidence for the "informal empire" thesis you will meet in section 11: formal rule and economic benefit are not the same thing.

## 7. Part II — The swing to the East, 1783–1857

### 7.1 What Britain took, and why it took it

Historians used to speak of a "swing to the East" after 1783. The phrase overstates a break — Britain never abandoned the Atlantic — but it captures a real shift. The territories acquired between 1783 and 1857 were overwhelmingly (a) strategic points on the sea road to Asia, and (b) India itself.

The Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars (1793–1815) were an acquisition engine, because Britain's method of fighting France was to take the overseas possessions of France and its satellites. The Cape of Good Hope was seized from the Dutch in 1795, returned at the Peace of Amiens in 1803, retaken in 1806 and kept. Trinidad was taken from Spain in 1797, Malta occupied in 1800, Mauritius taken from France in 1810, the Ceylon littoral taken from the Dutch in 1796. The Congress of Vienna (1814–15) confirmed most of these. Notice the logic: almost none of them were valuable for their own produce. They were valuable because of where they were.

Meanwhile, in India, the Company kept expanding — Mysore defeated with the death of Tipu Sultan at Seringapatam in 1799; Maratha power broken by 1818; Sind annexed in 1843; the Sikh kingdom of the Punjab annexed in 1849 after two hard wars. By the 1850s the Company governed, directly or through subordinate princes, essentially the whole subcontinent.

**Australia** belongs to this period and to a different logic again. The First Fleet reached Sydney Cove on 26 January 1788, carrying convicts — transportation to America having become impossible after 1783. Roughly 162,000 convicts were transported to the Australian colonies before the practice ended in 1868. The colonists' legal claim rested on the doctrine that the continent was *terra nullius*, land belonging to no one, a doctrine finally repudiated by the High Court of Australia in the *Mabo* judgment of 1992. Aboriginal Australians were dispossessed by settlement, disease and frontier violence over the following century; the historians' quarrel over how to count and characterise that violence — the so-called "history wars" of the 1990s and 2000s, associated with Henry Reynolds on one side and Keith Windschuttle on the other — is a live example of how national politics shapes historical argument.

**New Zealand** produced the single most examinable document in the whole empire.

> **SOURCE C — the Treaty of Waitangi, 6 February 1840**
> *Two texts were signed, one English, one Māori. Roughly 540 signatures were eventually gathered across copies circulated around the islands. The English text and the Māori text do not say the same thing.*
>
> **English text, Article 1:** the chiefs cede to the Queen "all the rights and powers of Sovereignty".
> **Māori text, Article 1:** the chiefs give the Queen "te Kawanatanga katoa" — complete *governance*.
>
> **English text, Article 2:** guarantees the chiefs "full exclusive and undisturbed possession of their Lands and Estates Forests Fisheries and other properties".
> **Māori text, Article 2:** guarantees "te tino rangatiratanga" — full, unqualified *chieftainship* — over their lands, dwellings and "taonga katoa" (all treasured things).

> **THINK 7.1 — The best source question in the chapter**
> "Kāwanatanga" was a missionary coinage derived from "governor". "Rangatiratanga" was the existing Māori word for chiefly authority — and it is the word the missionary translators had used for "kingdom" in the Lord's Prayer.
> (a) Which text did the chiefs sign?
> (b) On the evidence above, what did they think they were agreeing to?
> (c) Was this a mistranslation, a deliberate deception, or an ambiguity that both sides found useful at the time?
> (d) The New Zealand Wars of 1845–72 and the confiscation of over a million hectares of Māori land under the New Zealand Settlements Act 1863 followed. The Waitangi Tribunal, established in 1975, still adjudicates claims arising from this document. What does that tell you about the afterlife of imperial paperwork?

### 7.2 Abolition: conscience, economics, and the price of it

Britain abolished its slave *trade* by an Act of 25 March 1807, effective 1 May, and abolished slavery itself in most of its empire by the Slavery Abolition Act of 28 August 1833, which took effect on 1 August 1834. Roughly 800,000 people were freed — though "freed" is the wrong word for four years, because the Act imposed a system of "apprenticeship" binding former slaves to their former owners until 1 August 1838, ended early because of resistance and protest.

Here is the fact that makes students sit up. Compensation of **£20 million** was paid — not to the enslaved, but to roughly 47,000 claims by slave-owners for the loss of their property. That was around 40% of annual government expenditure. The government borrowed to pay it, and the Treasury has stated that the resulting debt was not fully discharged until 2015. The UCL *Legacies of British Slave-ownership* project, led by Catherine Hall and Nicholas Draper, has traced where that money went: into country houses, railways, banks, insurance firms, galleries and university endowments.

Why did abolition happen? This is a classic "how far do you agree" question, and there are three named positions.

> **INTERPRETATION 1 — Eric Williams**, *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944). Williams, a Trinidadian historian who became his country's first prime minister, argued that the profits of slavery financed British industrialisation, and that abolition came when the West Indian sugar economy had ceased to be economically central — that is, when it was convenient. Humanitarianism was the vocabulary, not the cause.

> **INTERPRETATION 2 — Seymour Drescher**, *Econocide* (1977). Drescher tested Williams's claim statistically and argued the opposite: the British slave system was *expanding and profitable* in 1807, so abolition was an act of economic self-harm, driven by genuine popular mobilisation — petitions, boycotts, and the largest mass campaign in British political history to that date.

> **INTERPRETATION 3 — The enslaved themselves.** Historians including Michael Craton and, more recently, Priyamvada Gopal in *Insurgent Empire* (2019), stress that abolition followed a wave of enormous slave revolts: Haiti's revolution (1791–1804), Barbados in 1816, Demerara in 1823, and the Jamaican "Baptist War" of 1831–32 involving some 60,000 people. On this view Parliament abolished slavery because the enslaved had made it unsustainable.

> **THINK 7.2** — These three interpretations are not simply incompatible. Write a single paragraph that uses all three, in a defensible order of importance, to explain 1833. (Hint: causes can be necessary without being sufficient. Which of the three explains the *timing*? Which explains the *possibility*?)

And the sting in the tail: within a year of the 1833 Act, planters in Mauritius and then British Guiana, Trinidad and Jamaica began importing **indentured labourers** from India. Between 1834 and 1917 roughly 1.3–1.5 million Indians travelled under indenture to Mauritius, the Caribbean, Natal and Fiji, on five-year contracts, in ships and conditions that abolitionists themselves compared to the slave trade. This is why Trinidad, Guyana, Fiji, Mauritius and South Africa have large Indian-descended populations today. Empire moved people as much as it moved goods.

### 7.3 Free trade and the invisible empire

From the 1840s Britain dismantled its own protectionist system — the Corn Laws repealed in 1846, the Navigation Acts in 1849. On the face of it this was anti-imperial: if trade is free, why own territory? Yet the same decades saw the Opium Wars (1839–42, 1856–60), fought to force China to accept a drug trade, producing Hong Kong Island (1842) and Kowloon (1860); the annexation of Sind and the Punjab; and gunboats deployed from Buenos Aires to Canton.

This apparent paradox produced the single most influential article in the field.

> **INTERPRETATION 4 — John Gallagher and Ronald Robinson**, "The Imperialism of Free Trade", *Economic History Review* (1953). They argued that the mid-Victorian period was not an "anti-imperial" interlude between two bursts of expansion. Britain always sought the same thing — access and security for trade — and simply used the cheapest available method: "trade with informal control if possible; trade with rule when necessary."

Their key move is to widen the definition of empire to include places that were never painted red: Argentina, Ottoman Egypt before 1882, the Chinese treaty ports. Critics — notably **D. K. Fieldhouse**, *Economics and Empire* (1973) — replied that a concept stretched to cover everything explains nothing, and that "informal empire" is very hard to falsify. You should be able to state both the thesis and that objection.

### 7.4 1857: the rebellion that ended Company rule

On 10 May 1857 sepoys of the Bengal Army mutinied at Meerut, marched on Delhi and proclaimed the aged Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar as their sovereign. What followed spread across the Gangetic plain — Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi — and took the British until 1858 to crush. The immediate trigger was the greased cartridge for the new Enfield rifle, rumoured to be lubricated with cow and pig fat, offensive to Hindu and Muslim soldiers alike. The deeper causes were the annexation of Awadh in 1856, the "doctrine of lapse" by which Dalhousie had swallowed princely states without heirs, land revenue settlements that dispossessed rural elites, and a general fear that British power intended to convert India.

Atrocity ran in both directions and was then weaponised. The killing of British women and children at Kanpur in July 1857 was reported in Britain in language of apocalyptic horror; the British reprisals — villages burned, prisoners blown from cannon, thousands hanged along the roads — were extensively documented, sometimes proudly, by the men who carried them out. What you call this event is itself a historical argument: British sources long said "the Indian Mutiny", which frames it as military indiscipline; Indian nationalists from V. D. Savarkar onwards called it the First War of Independence, which frames it as a national rising; most academic historians now say "the Rebellion of 1857" or "the Great Rebellion", because it was much more than a mutiny and much less than a nation.

The result was constitutional. The **Government of India Act of 2 August 1858** abolished the East India Company and transferred India to the Crown.

> *Queen Victoria's Proclamation to the Princes, Chiefs and People of India, read at Allahabad, 1 November 1858. It was drafted with the Queen's personal involvement and deliberately conciliatory in tone.*
>
> "We hold ourselves bound to the natives of Our Indian territories by the same obligations of duty which bind us to all Our other subjects."

> **THINK 7.3** — Compare Source D with Source E below, written twenty-three years earlier by a member of the Governor-General's Council. Do they belong to the same project or to different ones?

> **SOURCE E**
> *Thomas Babington Macaulay, "Minute on Indian Education", 2 February 1835. Macaulay was arguing that government money should fund English-language education rather than Sanskrit and Arabic learning. His view prevailed.*
>

Note the irony a strong essay would seize on: Macaulay's English-educated class did emerge — and it produced Dadabhai Naoroji, Surendranath Banerjea, Gandhi, Jinnah and Nehru, all trained in British law or English letters, all of whom turned liberal vocabulary against the empire that taught it. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, was in part a creature of Macaulay's policy.

## 8. Part III — High noon and the scramble, 1857–1914

### 8.1 The Raj, and how a few thousand people ruled 300 million

After 1858 India was governed by a Viceroy, a Secretary of State in London, and the Indian Civil Service — an elite of, at its height, roughly 1,000 covenanted officers for a population that passed 300 million. This was possible only because the overwhelming majority of soldiers, policemen, clerks, judges, tax collectors and teachers were Indian. The Indian Army, paid for by Indian taxpayers, was Britain's strategic reserve for the whole eastern hemisphere: it fought in China, Persia, Egypt, Sudan, East and West Africa and, later, in both world wars.

That last point is the most under-used fact in student essays. **India paid for its own conquest and for much of the empire's policing.** It is the core of the "drain" argument.

> **SOURCE F**
> *Dadabhai Naoroji, *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (London, 1901). Naoroji was a Parsi businessman and reformer who in 1892 became the first Indian elected to the House of Commons, sitting as a Liberal for Finsbury Central.*
>
> Naoroji argued that a continuous "drain of wealth" — the Home Charges, remitted profits, pensions and military costs paid from Indian revenue to Britain — impoverished India, and that this was contrary to Britain's own professed principles. Hence his pointed adjective: not un-British *people*, but "un-British rule".

> **THINK 8.1** — Naoroji's rhetorical strategy is to accuse the British of failing their own standards rather than of having bad standards. Why might a colonial critic in 1901 choose that strategy? What are its advantages and its limits?

The economics remain fiercely contested. Utsa Patnaik has produced a headline estimate that Britain extracted the equivalent of some $45 trillion from India between 1765 and 1938 — a figure widely quoted in public debate and widely disputed by economic historians over its assumptions about compound interest and counterfactual investment. **Tirthankar Roy** argues that India's poverty owed more to land scarcity, climate and low agricultural productivity than to transfers to Britain, and that the "drain" was small relative to national income. You do not need to resolve this. You do need to be able to say *why* the estimates differ: they rest on different counterfactuals about what India would otherwise have done with the money.

### 8.2 Famine: the hardest case

Between 1876 and 1902 India suffered a series of catastrophic famines. The Great Famine of 1876–78 is generally estimated to have killed between 5.5 and 6.6 million people at the lower end and considerably more at the upper; the famines of 1896–97 and 1899–1902 killed millions more. During the same decades, grain continued to be exported.

> **INTERPRETATION 5 — Mike Davis**, *Late Victorian Holocausts* (2001), argues that these deaths were not natural disasters but the product of the forced integration of Indian peasants into a world market, combined with a colonial administration ideologically committed to non-interference in grain prices. The El Niño droughts were real; the mortality, he argues, was policy.

> **INTERPRETATION 6 — Amartya Sen**, *Poverty and Famines* (1981), reframed the whole question. Famines, he showed, are usually not caused by an absolute absence of food but by collapses in people's *entitlement* to food — wages, prices, employment. His most quoted claim is that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press, because governments that face elections and criticism are forced to act.

> **INTERPRETATION 7 — Tirthankar Roy** and others counter that colonial India's famine mortality was largely a function of drought in a subsistence agriculture with poor transport, that the Famine Codes introduced from 1880 did reduce mortality, and that comparable famines occurred in non-colonised regions.

> **THINK 8.2** — Sen's argument is not primarily about empire at all; it is about political accountability. Explain in three sentences why his framework nonetheless amounts to a serious charge against British rule in India. Then explain what evidence would be needed to *test* Davis's stronger claim that the famines were a product of policy rather than drought.

### 8.3 The scramble for Africa

In 1880 European powers controlled perhaps a tenth of Africa, mostly coastal. By 1914 they controlled almost all of it. Britain's share included Egypt (occupied 1882), Sudan (reconquered 1896–98), British East Africa (Kenya, from 1895), Uganda (1894), Zanzibar (protectorate 1890), Nyasaland (1891), Northern and Southern Rhodesia (from the British South Africa Company's charter of 1889), Bechuanaland (1885), Nigeria (Lagos from 1861, protectorates from 1900, amalgamated 1914), the Gold Coast (1874), Sierra Leone and the Gambia.

The Berlin Conference of 15 November 1884 – 26 February 1885 is often described as the meeting where Europe "divided Africa". It did not: no African territory was allotted there. It set rules — notably that claims required "effective occupation" — which had the effect of turning paper claims into races for actual control. No African ruler was present.

Why did it happen, and why then? Four families of explanation, which you should be able to name and weigh:

1. **Economic (Hobson, Lenin).** J. A. Hobson, a radical Liberal journalist who covered the South African War, argued in *Imperialism: A Study* (1902) that inequality at home left capitalists with surplus savings and no domestic outlet, so they pushed for overseas territory to invest in — and that a small class of financiers manipulated the nation into wars that served them. Lenin adapted this in *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1917), making imperialism the necessary monopoly stage of capitalism, and quoting Rhodes to prove it.

> **SOURCE G**
> *Cecil Rhodes, in conversation with the journalist W. T. Stead, 1895, as quoted by V. I. Lenin in* Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism *(1917). Rhodes was then prime minister of the Cape Colony and the dominant figure in the diamond and gold industries.*
>
> "My cherished idea is a solution for the social problem… in order to save the forty million inhabitants of the United Kingdom from a bloody civil war, we colonial statesmen must acquire new lands… The Empire, as I have always said, is a bread and butter question."

> **THINK 8.3 — Provenance drill.** Rhodes said this to a journalist; Lenin quoted it, in a pamphlet arguing that capitalism causes imperialism, twenty-two years later. List three separate reasons for caution about using Source G as evidence of *why the scramble happened*. Then give one reason it remains genuinely valuable.

2. **Strategic (Robinson and Gallagher).** In *Africa and the Victorians* (1961) Robinson and Gallagher argued that British ministers were not chasing profits but reacting defensively to crises on the periphery — above all in Egypt, where the nationalist movement led by Colonel Ahmad Urabi threatened the Suez Canal and Britain's investments, prompting the bombardment of Alexandria in July 1882 and the invasion that culminated at Tel el-Kebir on 13 September 1882. Once in Egypt, Britain had to secure the Nile; hence Sudan and Uganda. They coined the phrase "the official mind" for the assumptions inside Whitehall that actually generated decisions.

3. **European rivalry.** Once Germany, France, Belgium and Italy were claiming territory, non-claiming became a strategic loss. The Fashoda crisis of 1898, when a British and a French expedition met on the upper Nile and nearly caused a war, shows the logic at its purest.

4. **Technology and disease.** Quinine prophylaxis, steamboats on rivers, the telegraph, breech-loading rifles and, from the 1880s, the Maxim gun radically shifted the cost of conquest. At Omdurman on 2 September 1898, Kitchener's Anglo-Egyptian force killed roughly 10,000 Sudanese Mahdist fighters for the loss of around 50 of its own. Hilaire Belloc's satirical couplet of 1898 — "Whatever happens, we have got / The Maxim gun, and they have not" — is a joke about exactly this asymmetry.

### 8.4 Collaboration: the mechanism that made it cheap

> **INTERPRETATION 8 — Ronald Robinson**, "Non-European Foundations of European Imperialism" (1972), proposed a "theory of collaboration": empire on the cheap depended on finding local elites — princes, chiefs, landlords, merchants, soldiers, clerks — whose interests were served by working with the imperial power. Imperial expansion happened where collaboration could be arranged; imperial collapse happened when collaborators defected.

This is the single most useful analytical tool in the chapter, because it explains both halves of the story with one mechanism. Indirect rule in Northern Nigeria, associated with Frederick Lugard, ran a population of millions through existing emirs. The 560-odd princely states of India covered roughly a third of the subcontinent's territory. The Indian Army was Indian. When those arrangements broke — when the sepoys mutinied in 1857, when the Indian professional classes turned to Congress, when African chiefs' authority was discredited by their association with colonial demands — the machinery seized up.

> **THINK 8.4** — "Collaboration" is a word poisoned by the Second World War. Is it a fair term for, say, an Indian clerk in the Bengal civil service in 1890, or a Fante merchant on the Gold Coast? What alternative word would you use, and does the choice change the analysis or only the tone?

### 8.5 The South African War, 1899–1902: empire's most expensive lesson

The war against the Boer republics of the Transvaal and the Orange Free State began on 11 October 1899 and was expected to be short. It lasted until the Treaty of Vereeniging on 31 May 1902, required nearly 450,000 imperial troops, and cost over £200 million. Beaten in the field, the Boers turned to guerrilla warfare; the British replied with farm-burning and the mass internment of civilians.

> **SOURCE H**
> *Emily Hobhouse, "Report of a Visit to the Camps of Women and Children in the Cape and Orange River Colonies", presented to the South African Women and Children's Distress Fund, London, June 1901. Hobhouse was a British welfare campaigner who travelled to South Africa privately and was later denounced in the Commons as hysterical and pro-Boer.*
>
> Hobhouse described overcrowded tents, inadequate rations, absent sanitation and children dying of measles and typhoid, and demanded government action. Her report prompted the appointment of the Fawcett Commission, whose findings largely confirmed hers.

Roughly **28,000 Boer civilians died in the camps, the great majority of them children**. In separate, worse-supplied camps for Black Africans, the death toll is estimated at somewhere between 14,000 and over 20,000 — a figure omitted from British public memory for most of the twentieth century, and a good illustration of how the shape of an archive shapes the shape of a national memory.

> **THINK 8.5** — Hobhouse was a private individual with no official standing, hostile to the war, writing to raise money for its victims. Construct the *strongest possible* argument that her report is unreliable. Then construct the argument that it is more valuable *because* of its provenance, not less. Which argument would you actually put in an exam answer, and why? (Model answers to this style of question appear in section 13.)

The war also produced the first serious crisis of imperial confidence. Recruitment revealed the poor physical condition of urban working-class volunteers, feeding the Edwardian "national efficiency" panic and, indirectly, early welfare legislation. And at the Colonial Conference of 1902, the Colonial Secretary Joseph Chamberlain appealed to the self-governing colonies for help with the burden, borrowing a line from Matthew Arnold: "the weary Titan staggers under the too vast orb of its fate."

## 9. Part IV — Maximum extent and hidden crisis, 1914–1945

### 9.1 The empire at war, and the empire at its largest

The First World War was fought by the empire, not just by Britain. Over a million Indian troops served overseas and roughly 74,000 died; Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa raised armies of their own and suffered casualties that reshaped their national identities — Gallipoli in 1915 became a founding story for Australia and New Zealand precisely because it was a disaster ordered from London. Nearly a million Africans served as soldiers or, in far greater numbers, as carriers in the East African campaign, where mortality among carriers was appalling and record-keeping negligible.

The peace made the empire bigger than it had ever been. At San Remo in April 1920 and under League of Nations mandates confirmed in 1922, Britain took **Palestine, Transjordan and Iraq** from the Ottoman Empire and **Tanganyika** plus parts of Cameroon and Togo from Germany. On paper, around 1921–22, roughly a quarter of the world's land surface and a similar share of its people were under British authority.

Beneath the map, the crisis was already visible. Wartime promises had been made in incompatible directions — to Sharif Hussein of Mecca, to France in the Sykes–Picot agreement of 1916, and to Zionism in the Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917, which promised "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine while stipulating that nothing should prejudice "the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities". That single sentence generated three decades of British failure in Palestine and a conflict that has not ended. Egypt exploded in revolution in 1919 and was granted nominal independence on 28 February 1922, with four "reserved points" — including the Canal and defence — kept in British hands, which is a useful definition of independence in name only. Iraq rose in revolt in 1920 and was suppressed at heavy cost, partly by aerial bombing, a technique whose enthusiasts in the RAF explicitly promoted it as cheap colonial control.

And in India, on **13 April 1919**, Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer marched troops into the enclosed Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, where a crowd had gathered in defiance of a ban, and ordered them to fire without warning until ammunition ran low. The official Hunter Committee recorded **379 dead and about 1,200 wounded**; Congress's own inquiry put the dead at over a thousand. Dyer told the Hunter Committee that his object had not merely been to disperse the crowd but "to produce a sufficient moral effect" throughout the Punjab. He was removed from command, censured by the Hunter majority — and voted a substantial sum by public subscription in Britain, a fact that tells you as much as the massacre itself about metropolitan opinion.

> **THINK 9.1** — Kim Wagner (*Amritsar 1919*, 2019) argues the massacre was not an aberration by one deranged officer but an expression of a long-standing colonial doctrine that exemplary violence was how order was maintained in an emergency. What kind of evidence would support that claim, and what kind would refute it? Where would you look?

### 9.2 The dominions walk out of the front door

While the dependent empire was being held down, the settler empire was quietly negotiating its way out — and being congratulated for it.

> **SOURCE I**
> *The "Balfour Definition", report of the Inter-Imperial Relations Committee, Imperial Conference, London, November 1926. Chaired by the former prime minister Arthur Balfour. It became the basis of the Statute of Westminster.*
>
> The dominions are "autonomous Communities within the British Empire, equal in status, in no way subordinate one to another in any aspect of their domestic or external affairs, though united by a common allegiance to the Crown, and freely associated as members of the British Commonwealth of Nations."

The **Statute of Westminster (11 December 1931)** gave that formula legal force: the Westminster Parliament could no longer legislate for a dominion without its request and consent. Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the Irish Free State and Newfoundland were now independent states that happened to share a king.

Two observations that examiners love. First, read Source I with section 3's glossary in hand: every dominion was a settler state with a white electorate. Equality of status was not on offer to India, whose Congress had been demanding dominion status since the 1920s and declared for full independence — *purna swaraj* — on 26 January 1930. Second, Newfoundland is the counter-example that proves nothing is inevitable: bankrupted by the Depression, it *surrendered* self-government in 1934, reverted to direct rule by commission, and voted narrowly in 1948 to join Canada, which it did on 31 March 1949. Not every dominion path led to independence.

**Ireland** is the sharpest case of all, and the one most often left out. After the Easter Rising of 1916, the Sinn Féin landslide of December 1918, and the War of Independence of 1919–21 — in which the Crown forces' auxiliaries, the "Black and Tans", made counter-insurgency notorious — the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 6 December 1921 created the Irish Free State as a dominion, with six north-eastern counties remaining in the United Kingdom. The Free State came into being on 6 December 1922; a bitter civil war followed among Irish nationalists over the Treaty's terms. De Valera's constitution of 1937 removed most remaining links, and the Republic of Ireland Act took effect on 18 April 1949, taking Ireland out of the Commonwealth. Ireland was the first territory to fight its way out of the twentieth-century empire, and the template — insurgency, negotiation, partition, unresolved minority question — recurs in India in 1947, Palestine in 1948 and Cyprus in 1960.

### 9.3 India between the wars: reform as a delaying tactic

British policy in India followed a rhythm: concession, repression, concession. The Morley–Minto reforms (1909) introduced limited elected representation and, fatefully, separate electorates for Muslims. The Government of India Act of 1919 (Montagu–Chelmsford) created "dyarchy", handing Indian ministers control of minor provincial departments while keeping finance, police and revenue in British hands. The Government of India Act of 1935 — the longest Act Parliament had ever passed — created elected provincial governments, which Congress won handsomely in 1937.

Against this, Gandhi built something new: mass non-violent non-cooperation, which converted an elite debating society into a movement of millions. The Non-Cooperation Movement of 1920–22; the **Salt March**, 12 March to 6 April 1930, in which Gandhi walked 240 miles to the sea to make salt in defiance of the government monopoly and was joined by tens of thousands; the **Quit India** resolution of 8 August 1942, launched with the instruction "Do or die", which brought the arrest of the entire Congress leadership for most of the war.

> **SOURCE J**
> *Winston Churchill, speech at Winchester House, Epping, 23 February 1931, opposing the government's talks with Gandhi about dominion status.*
>
> "It is alarming and also nauseating to see Mr Gandhi, a seditious Middle Temple lawyer, now posing as a fakir of a type well known in the East, striding half-naked up the steps of the Viceroy's palace."

> **THINK 9.2** — Churchill was in the political wilderness in 1931, and lost this argument: the 1935 Act passed. What does the *failure* of Source J's position tell you about British opinion on India in the 1930s that the source's own vehemence might mislead you about?

### 9.4 The Second World War: the empire's last exertion and its fatal cost

India supplied roughly 2.5 million volunteers, the largest volunteer army in history. African troops fought in Burma and East Africa. Caribbean volunteers served in the RAF. The empire's contribution was indispensable to British survival.

But the war destroyed the empire's foundations in four ways. It bankrupted Britain: by 1945 the country owed India alone over £1 billion in sterling balances, meaning the imperial power was now the debtor of its largest colony. It shattered British military prestige in Asia — the surrender of Singapore on 15 February 1942, with some 80,000 troops taken prisoner, was the largest capitulation in British military history, and it happened in front of the whole of Asia. It committed Britain rhetorically to self-determination: the Atlantic Charter of August 1941, signed with Roosevelt, declared that the signatories "respect the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live" — after which Churchill told the Commons on 9 September 1941 that he had meant it to apply to European nations under Nazi occupation, not to the British Empire. Everyone in the colonies read the Charter, and nobody accepted the footnote. And it made the United States, an anti-colonial power with its own imperial interests, the arbiter of Britain's finances.

Finally, in 1943, **famine returned to Bengal**. Between 2.1 and 3 million people died. The causes were a cyclone, the loss of Burmese rice imports after the Japanese conquest, war-driven inflation, provincial governments blocking grain movement, and a "denial policy" that removed boats and rice stocks from coastal districts to deny them to a possible invader. The London government, facing a shipping crisis, repeatedly declined to divert vessels. Historians divide sharply: Madhusree Mukerjee (*Churchill's Secret War*, 2010) presents it as a case of culpable indifference at the highest level; others, including Tirthankar Roy, emphasise wartime logistics, provincial failure and market breakdown over any single decision in London. What is not in dispute is that a famine killing millions occurred in the eighth year of a supposedly reformed system of Indian government, and that it happened while Britain was fighting a war it described as one for freedom.

> **THINK 9.3** — Set the Bengal famine of 1943 beside the Irish famine of 1845–52, in which roughly a million died and a million more emigrated while food exports continued. What features do the two share? What features make them different? Is "empire" the most useful category for comparing them, or would "nineteenth-century political economy" or "wartime logistics" explain more?

## 10. Part V — Dissolution, 1945–1997

### 10.1 The question that organises this whole section

Did Britain *choose* to decolonise, or was it *pushed*? This is the most commonly set essay question on the period, and the honest answer is: both, in different places, and the mix is what you must analyse. Three factors ran together everywhere — nationalist pressure in the colony, British economic and military weakness, and international pressure from the United States, the Soviet Union and, after 1960, the United Nations — but their relative weight varies enormously between, say, India in 1947 and Botswana in 1966.

### 10.2 India and Pakistan, 1947: the fastest and costliest transfer

Attlee's Labour government took office in July 1945 committed to Indian self-government and facing a country it could no longer hold. The 1946 naval mutiny at Bombay, the collapse of the Cabinet Mission's federal plan, and the communal killings that began on Direct Action Day, 16 August 1946, convinced London that time had run out. Lord Mountbatten arrived as the last Viceroy on 22 March 1947, and dramatically accelerated the timetable: partition was announced on 3 June, the Indian Independence Act passed on 18 July, and power transferred at midnight on **14–15 August 1947**.

The boundary itself was drawn by Cyril Radcliffe, a lawyer who had never been to India, working from maps and census returns in five weeks. His award was not published until **17 August**, two days *after* independence — so millions of people spent the first days of freedom not knowing which country they were in. Between 10 and 20 million people were displaced. Estimates of the dead range from around 200,000 to two million; nobody counted.

> **SOURCE K**
> *Jawaharlal Nehru, address to the Constituent Assembly of India, New Delhi, shortly before midnight on 14 August 1947.*
>
> "At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom."

> **THINK 10.1** — Gandhi was not in Delhi that night. He was in Calcutta, fasting, trying to stop communal killing. Historians use that absence as a symbol. Symbols are not arguments: what would you need to show to turn it into one?

Burma followed on 4 January 1948, leaving the Commonwealth. Ceylon (Sri Lanka) became independent on 4 February 1948 and stayed. Palestine ended differently: unable to reconcile Jewish and Arab claims, facing a Zionist insurgency and a hostile Arab population, Britain referred the problem to the United Nations in February 1947 and simply left on 14 May 1948, an abdication rather than a transfer.

### 10.3 The reluctant decade, 1948–1957

It is a mistake to think 1947 began a general retreat. Attlee's and Churchill's governments both hoped that a smaller, tighter empire in Africa and the Middle East could substitute for India and pay Britain's dollar debts — hence the intensified development schemes of the late 1940s, including the famously disastrous Tanganyika groundnut scheme. Historians sometimes call this the "second colonial occupation": more British officials went to Africa after 1945 than before it.

Where challenged, Britain fought. The **Malayan Emergency** (16 June 1948 – 31 July 1960) pitted the largely Chinese-recruited Malayan Communist Party against a counter-insurgency that combined political concession with the forcible resettlement of roughly 500,000 people into guarded "New Villages" under the Briggs Plan. It is often taught as the model of a successful counter-insurgency; it also included episodes such as the killing of 24 unarmed villagers at Batang Kali on 12 December 1948, never satisfactorily investigated. Malaya became independent on **31 August 1957** — on terms broadly acceptable to Britain, which is precisely why the case is used to argue that Britain "managed" decolonisation.

Then came **Suez**. When Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal Company in July 1956, Eden's government colluded secretly with France and Israel: Israel invaded Sinai on 29 October, Britain and France issued a prearranged ultimatum, bombed Egyptian airfields from 31 October and landed at Port Said on 5–6 November. A run on sterling followed, and the United States refused to support the pound unless Britain stopped. A ceasefire was accepted on 7 November and British forces withdrew in December. Eden resigned on 9 January 1957.

> **THINK 10.2** — Suez is routinely described as the moment Britain discovered it was no longer a great power. Test that claim: (a) name two things Britain did after 1956 that a non-great power could not have done; (b) explain how the *American* veto, rather than Egyptian resistance, decided the outcome; (c) decide whether Suez *caused* the acceleration of decolonisation after 1957 or merely *revealed* conditions that already existed.

### 10.4 The African rush, 1957–1968

Ghana became independent on **6 March 1957**, the first sub-Saharan African colony to do so, under Kwame Nkrumah, whose slogan — "Seek ye first the political kingdom, and all things shall be added unto it" — announced a strategy: take the state first, worry about economics after. Then the dam broke. > **THINK 10.3 — Attribution matters most here.**
> (a) Why deliver this speech in Cape Town rather than London?
> (b) Notice the verb: Macmillan says national consciousness is "a political fact" to be *accepted*, not a right to be honoured. What is the difference, and what does the choice reveal about the government's motive?
> (c) Six weeks later, on 21 March 1960, South African police killed 69 protesters at Sharpeville; South Africa left the Commonwealth on 31 May 1961. Does that sequence make Source L look prophetic, or does it show the speech failed in its immediate purpose?

Meanwhile Kenya showed what happened where white settlers had land. The Mau Mau insurgency among the Kikuyu, and the Emergency declared on 20 October 1952, produced a counter-insurgency of extraordinary severity: a system of detention camps known as "the Pipeline", forced villagisation, and the hanging of over a thousand people. Official figures record 11,503 insurgents killed; 32 European settlers died in the whole conflict. Caroline Elkins (*Britain's Gulag* / *Imperial Reckoning*, 2005) argued for very large numbers of detainees and deaths and won a Pulitzer Prize; David Anderson (*Histories of the Hanged*, 2005) reached lower but still devastating figures and criticised her demographic method. The historiographical dispute matters because it was tested in court.

> **INTERPRETATION 9 — the archive itself.** In the Mau Mau compensation case brought against the Foreign Office from 2009, the government disclosed that thousands of files removed from colonies at independence had been held at Hanslope Park. These "migrated archives", released from 2012, documented systematic destruction of records at the end of empire — Operation Legacy — as well as abuse. On 6 June 2013 the Foreign Secretary William Hague announced a settlement of £19.9 million for 5,228 Kenyan claimants and expressed regret for torture.

> **THINK 10.4** — This is the most important methodological point in the chapter. If the losing side of a conflict has its records seized or burned, and the winning side's own incriminating records are removed from the country and hidden for fifty years, what happens to the "balance" of the surviving evidence? How should a historian — and how should you, in an exam — handle a silence in the record that was *created deliberately*?

> **SOURCE M**
> *Enoch Powell MP, House of Commons, 27 July 1959, on the deaths of eleven detainees beaten by guards at Hola Camp, Kenya, on 3 March 1959. Powell was a Conservative backbencher and a former junior minister.*
>
> "We cannot say, 'We will have African standards in Africa, Asian standards in Asia and perhaps British standards here at home.'"

> **THINK 10.5** — Source M is a defence of universal standards delivered by a politician who a decade later became notorious for a speech against Commonwealth immigration. Does that later career reduce the value of this source, or is the tension itself historically revealing? Answer in three sentences.

### 10.5 Awkward endings

Not everything ended in a stadium at midnight with a flag being lowered.

- **Southern Rhodesia** refused to accept majority rule. Ian Smith's white minority government issued an illegal **Unilateral Declaration of Independence on 11 November 1965**. Britain applied sanctions but declined to use force against a settler population of British descent — a decision often cited as evidence of racial double standards in decolonisation. A guerrilla war followed. The Lancaster House agreement of December 1979 produced elections, and **Zimbabwe became independent on 18 April 1980**.
- **Aden and South Arabia** ended in defeat: after an insurgency from 1963, British forces withdrew on 30 November 1967 with no agreed successor government, and the People's Republic of South Yemen was proclaimed.
- **East of Suez**: in January 1968, facing devaluation and a financial crisis, the Wilson government announced withdrawal of British forces from the Gulf and South-East Asia by 1971. The Gulf states — Bahrain, Qatar and the seven emirates that formed the United Arab Emirates on 2 December 1971 — became independent essentially because Britain could no longer afford to protect them.
- **The West Indies Federation** (1958–62) was Britain's preferred solution and collapsed when Jamaica voted to leave in a 1961 referendum; Jamaica became independent on 6 August 1962 and Trinidad and Tobago on 31 August 1962. Britain's designs for successor federations failed in the Caribbean, in Central Africa (the Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland, dissolved on 31 December 1963) and in South Arabia.
- **The Chagos Archipelago** was detached from Mauritius on 8 November 1965 to form the British Indian Ocean Territory, and its population of roughly 1,500–2,000 people was removed between 1968 and 1973 to make way for a US base on Diego Garcia. The International Court of Justice held in an advisory opinion of 25 February 2019 that the detachment had been unlawful; a treaty transferring sovereignty to Mauritius, with a long lease on the base, was signed on 22 May 2025.
- **Hong Kong** ended by clock. The New Territories, which made the colony viable, had been leased for 99 years in 1898. The Sino-British Joint Declaration of 19 December 1984 provided for the return of the whole territory, and at midnight on **1 July 1997** sovereignty passed to the People's Republic of China. This is the one major decolonisation in which the population was not consulted and did not become independent.

Fourteen British Overseas Territories remain, from Bermuda and Gibraltar to the Falklands — over which Britain fought a war in 1982 — and the British Antarctic Territory. Empire has an ending date only if you decide which ending counts.

### 10.6 The empire comes home

Decolonisation was also a migration story. The **British Nationality Act 1948** made colonial and Commonwealth subjects British citizens with the right of entry; the *Empire Windrush* docked at Tilbury on 22 June 1948 carrying several hundred Caribbean passengers. Migration from the Caribbean, India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Cyprus, Hong Kong and East Africa followed. It was then progressively restricted — the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 and the Immigration Act 1971 — in a sequence that historians such as Kathleen Paul have argued was designed to keep the door open to white Commonwealth citizens while closing it to others. The point for your essays: the end of empire abroad and the making of multi-ethnic Britain at home are the same process, not two topics.

## 11. The great debates: an interpretations dossier

Examiners set AO3 questions on interpretations, not on your opinions. Here are the five live arguments, each with named positions. Learn the names.

### Debate 1 — Why did Britain expand?

| Position | Historian | Core claim | Strongest evidence for | Strongest objection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Absence of mind | J. R. Seeley, *The Expansion of England* (1883) | Expansion was unplanned drift: "we seem, as it were, to have conquered and peopled half the world in a fit of absence of mind" | No master plan; many acquisitions were by-products of war | Individual acquisitions were highly deliberate; Seeley was arguing *for* imperial federation, not describing dispassionately |
| Capitalist necessity | J. A. Hobson (1902); V. I. Lenin (1917) | Surplus capital needed outlets; financiers drove policy | Investment flows; Rhodes and the South African War | Most British capital went to the USA, Argentina and the dominions, not to newly conquered Africa |
| Strategic reaction | Robinson & Gallagher, *Africa and the Victorians* (1961) | Crises on the periphery, especially Egypt 1882, dragged reluctant ministers in | Cabinet papers show anxiety, not appetite | Over-reliant on the "official mind"; underweights business lobbying and men on the spot |
| Gentlemanly capitalism | P. J. Cain & A. G. Hopkins, *British Imperialism* (1993) | The driving interest was not industry but the City of London and the service/financial elite, socially fused with the political class | Explains why Manchester manufacturers were often anti-imperial while bankers were not | Hard to distinguish influence from coincidence of background |
| Collaboration | Ronald Robinson (1972) | Expansion occurred where local elites could be recruited | Indirect rule; princely states; the Indian Army | Risks making the colonised responsible for their own colonisation |

### Debate 2 — Did empire matter to Britain at home?

**John MacKenzie** (*Propaganda and Empire*, 1984, and the Manchester "Studies in Imperialism" series) argued that empire saturated British popular culture — school texts, missionary societies, music hall, biscuit tins, boys' magazines, exhibitions. **Bernard Porter** (*The Absent-Minded Imperialists*, 2004) replied that most Britons were indifferent, that imperial imagery is not the same as imperial consciousness, and that the empire was largely run by a small caste. **Catherine Hall** and **Sonya Rose** (*At Home with the Empire*, 2006) reframed the question: the issue is not whether people talked about empire but how deeply imperial assumptions structured British class, gender and racial categories even when unspoken.

> **THINK 11.1** — How would you *test* MacKenzie against Porter? What sources would count as evidence of "imperial consciousness" and what would only be evidence of imperial *imagery*? This is a genuine methodological problem, and saying so in an essay is a strength, not a dodge.

### Debate 3 — The balance sheet

**Niall Ferguson** (*Empire*, 2003) argues that the empire spread free trade, capital, law, and representative institutions, and that the relevant comparison is not with an imagined free India or Africa but with the alternative empires — Belgian, German, Japanese, Soviet — that might have ruled instead. **Shashi Tharoor** (*Inglorious Empire*, 2017) and **Caroline Elkins** (*Legacy of Violence*, 2022) argue that violence was not an aberration within liberal empire but its operating system, and that the "gifts" — railways, law — were built for extraction and paid for by the colonised. **Nigel Biggar** (*Colonialism: A Moral Reckoning*, 2023) argues for a mixed moral verdict and against what he sees as anachronistic condemnation. **Priyamvada Gopal** (*Insurgent Empire*, 2019) inverts the frame: British liberty was itself partly taught by colonial rebels, whose resistance shaped British dissent.

> **THINK 11.2 — The most important warning in this chapter.**
> The "balance sheet" framing has a hidden flaw: it assumes the costs and the benefits fell on the same people. They did not. Railways in India were real; so was the revenue system that paid for them; so were the famines. Write two sentences explaining why "the empire built railways" and "the empire caused famines" can both be true without cancelling out — and why a historian's job is to specify *for whom* each was true.

### Debate 4 — Why did the empire end?

- **The "planned withdrawal" view**, associated with older official histories and echoed in ceremonial accounts, holds that Britain always intended to lead colonies to self-government and did so in an orderly fashion.
- **John Darwin** (*The Empire Project*, 2009; *Unfinished Empire*, 2012) argues the empire was a fragile improvisation whose global system — sterling, sea power, the Indian Army, settler loyalty — was broken by the two world wars, and that ministers improvised retreat while pretending to control it.
- **Wm. Roger Louis and Ronald Robinson**, "The Imperialism of Decolonization" (1994), argue Britain traded formal empire for a junior partnership with the United States, preserving influence by shedding responsibility — informal empire again, at the end as at the beginning.
- **Ronald Hyam** (*Britain's Declining Empire*, 2006) stresses the sheer weight of economic constraint and the speed with which Whitehall's cost–benefit calculations flipped after 1957.
- **Frederick Cooper** (*Decolonization and African Society*, 1996) and **Elizabeth Buettner** (*Europe after Empire*, 2016) shift attention to African labour movements, urban politics and metropolitan society, arguing that colonial states were undone from below and that decolonisation reshaped Europe as much as Africa.

> **THINK 11.3** — Rank these five explanations for (a) India 1947, (b) Ghana 1957, (c) Aden 1967 and (d) Hong Kong 1997. If your ranking is different for each, you have discovered the reason "why did the empire end?" cannot be answered in one sentence — and you have the skeleton of a first-class essay.

### Debate 5 — Whose history is it?

**Sathnam Sanghera** (*Empireland*, 2021) argues that Britain has never properly taught its imperial history and that consequences — in law, wealth, race and self-image — are still active. Others contend that the current wave of imperial history is presentist. Note that this debate is about *memory* as well as history, and that the two are different: history is what can be evidenced; memory is what a society finds usable. A sophisticated candidate keeps them apart.

## 13. Skills workshop: writing about sources and interpretations

### 13.1 Source utility: a method that works under exam pressure

Weak answers say "this source is biased, therefore unreliable". That sentence gains almost no marks, because every source has a point of view, and a point of view is *information*. Use this four-step method instead.

**1. Nature, Origin, Purpose (NOP).** What kind of document is it? Who made it, when, and for whom? What was it trying to achieve?
**2. Content.** What does it actually say — including what it implies, assumes or omits?
**3. Own knowledge.** What do you know that corroborates or contradicts it? (This is where marks are won or lost.)
**4. Utility *for a specified question*.** Never say a source is "useful" in the abstract. Say what it is useful *for*, and what it cannot tell you.

**Worked example — Source H (Emily Hobhouse, 1901).**

> *Weak:* "Hobhouse was against the war so she was biased and might exaggerate the camps."
>
> *Strong:* "Hobhouse's report is a campaigning document produced for a relief fund, and it was written to shock: its purpose was to force intervention, so it selects the worst conditions she saw and describes them emotively. That purpose limits its use as a measure of average camp conditions across the whole system. But it is highly valuable for two other questions. First, as eyewitness testimony from someone with no official position, it records what a hostile outsider could see in camps whose administration had every incentive to conceal failure. Second, its consequences are themselves evidence: the government's response was not to disprove it but to appoint the Fawcett Commission, whose findings substantially confirmed her account, and roughly 28,000 Boer civilians, mostly children, died. The source is therefore weak evidence of typicality and strong evidence both of conditions and of the political mechanism by which imperial atrocity could occasionally be forced into British public view — while the parallel deaths of Black Africans in separate camps, which Hobhouse's report barely touches, shows the limits of even critical contemporary vision."

Notice what the strong version does: it converts "bias" into a precise statement about *what the source can and cannot establish*, it deploys specific own knowledge (the Fawcett Commission, the death tolls), and it ends by identifying a silence.

### 13.2 "How far do you agree?" — a structure for interpretation questions

Use a **three-move** paragraph, repeated:

1. **State the interpretation's strongest form.** Not a straw man — the version its author would recognise.
2. **Test it against specific evidence**, at least one example that supports and one that strains it.
3. **Judge its scope.** Most historical interpretations are not true or false; they are true *of some cases, in some periods, at some scale*. Saying where an argument works and where it stops is the single highest-value move available to you.

**Worked example — "How far do you agree that the British Empire ended because Britain could no longer afford it?"**

> *Opening judgement:* Economic weakness set the outer limits of British choice after 1945, but it was decisive only where nationalist pressure had already made the cost of staying visible; where cost was low and pressure weak, Britain stayed for decades longer.
>
> *Paragraph A (supports):* Sterling balances of over £1 billion owed to India; the 1947 convertibility crisis; the American financial veto at Suez in November 1956; the January 1968 East of Suez announcement, taken immediately after devaluation. In the Gulf, independence in 1971 was granted to rulers who did not want it and offered to pay for the garrison — the clearest possible case of withdrawal by budget.
>
> *Paragraph B (complicates):* Britain spent lavishly on holding territory when it judged the stakes high enough: Malaya from 1948, Kenya from 1952, Cyprus from 1955, Aden from 1963. It also invested *more* in colonial development after 1945 than before. Cost, then, was not an independent variable; it was the price of resistance.
>
> *Paragraph C (rival factor):* Nationalist mobilisation — Congress's mass politics, the CPP in the Gold Coast, EOKA, the NLF — changed the price. Robinson's collaboration model explains the mechanism: once educated elites and, crucially, the intermediary classes stopped cooperating, indirect rule became direct rule, and direct rule was unaffordable.
>
> *Paragraph D (scope test):* And the model fails entirely for Hong Kong in 1997, which was profitable, stable, and handed over because a lease expired and China was stronger. It also fails for Southern Rhodesia, where Britain's problem was not cost but the political impossibility of coercing white settlers.
>
> *Conclusion:* Affordability is best understood not as a cause but as an exchange rate — the mechanism by which nationalist pressure, imperial overstretch and American priorities were converted into cabinet decisions.

### 13.3 Comparing two interpretations

When a question gives you two extracts by historians, do **not** summarise them in turn. Instead:

- Identify the **question each is answering**. Ferguson and Elkins often appear to contradict each other while answering different questions — one about aggregate global economic effects, one about the character of colonial governance.
- Identify the **evidence base**. Cain and Hopkins read City records; Elkins read detention camp files and oral testimony; MacKenzie read biscuit tins. Different archives produce different empires.
- Identify the **scale**. Claims about "the empire" made from one colony, one decade or one class are vulnerable, and saying so is analysis rather than evasion.
- Identify the **politics of the moment of writing**. Gallagher and Robinson wrote in 1953, as the empire was being reappraised; Ferguson wrote in 2003, during debates about American intervention; Elkins wrote after the Hanslope files were disclosed. Context is not an accusation — it is an explanation of what a historian could see.

### 13.4 Three errors that cost marks every year

1. **The "for and against" list with no judgement.** Balance is not the absence of an argument.
2. **Anachronistic vocabulary.** Writing that Victorians "should have known better" tells the examiner nothing. Show instead that critics *at the time* — Burke, Hobhouse, Naoroji, Hobson, the Aborigines' Protection Society — said so, which proves the alternatives were available and known.
3. **Treating "the British" as a single actor.** Settlers, missionaries, the Colonial Office, the India Office, the Treasury, the Admiralty, planters and Manchester manufacturers frequently wanted opposite things. Naming *which* British is almost always an upgrade.

## 14. Chapter summary: twelve things you must be able to do

1. Distinguish colony, protectorate, dominion, mandate and informal empire, and give an example of each.
2. Explain how the Atlantic empire was built on sugar and enslaved labour, and quote the scale of the British slave trade.
3. Explain how a trading company acquired a subcontinent between 1757 and 1765, and why 1858 changed the constitutional form but not the substance of rule.
4. Explain the loss of the American colonies as a consequence of the cost of victory in 1763.
5. Explain why the territories taken between 1795 and 1842 were mostly *places on a route* rather than places of production.
6. Argue about why abolition happened in 1807 and 1833, using Williams, Drescher and the evidence of slave revolt.
7. Use the Treaty of Waitangi to demonstrate how a single document can create a two-century legal dispute.
8. Give four explanations for the scramble for Africa and say which evidence best supports each.
9. Explain Robinson's collaboration thesis and use it to account for both expansion and collapse.
10. Narrate decolonisation with dates: 1947, 1948, 1956, 1957, 1960, 1963, 1965, 1967, 1971, 1980, 1997.
11. Explain why the surviving archive of the end of empire is incomplete by design, and what that does to historical claims.
12. Write about violence, profit, famine and resistance accurately, and about contingency, disagreement and collaboration honestly — without collapsing either into the other.

**And one thing to hold on to.** The empire was not a plan. It was a series of decisions taken by people who mostly could not see more than a few years ahead, in a competitive world, under financial constraint, and who were repeatedly surprised — by American colonists in 1775, by sepoys in 1857, by Boers in 1899, by the Japanese in 1942, by Nasser in 1956. That is not an excuse for its consequences. It is a warning against reading history backwards, as though everything that happened had to happen. Contingency is not absolution; but determinism is not analysis.
