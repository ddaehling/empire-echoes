## 7.0 An object, a ship, a debt

Start with three things you could touch.

**An object.** In a case in the Jewel House at the Tower of London sits the Koh-i-Noor diamond, mounted in the crown made for Queen Elizabeth the Queen Mother in 1937. It was surrendered to the British Crown in 1849 under the Treaty of Lahore, signed by Maharaja Duleep Singh of the Punjab. He was ten years old. India, Pakistan, Iran and Afghanistan have all asked for the diamond back. Britain has said no.

**A ship.** On 22 June 1948 the troopship *HMT Empire Windrush* docked at Tilbury in Essex carrying just over a thousand passengers, most of whom had boarded in Kingston, Jamaica. They were not immigrants in any legal sense. Under the **British Nationality Act 1948**, passed a month earlier, they were *British subjects*, entitled to live and work in the United Kingdom, because Jamaica was a British colony and they had been born under the British Crown. Seventy years later, several hundred of the people who came on ships like that one — and their children — were wrongly detained, denied healthcare, sacked, or deported by the British government, because they could not produce paperwork that the government itself had destroyed.

**A debt.** When Britain abolished slavery in its colonies under the Slavery Abolition Act of 1833, it paid compensation of £20 million. Not to the enslaved. To the roughly 46,000 people who had legally owned them — a group that included bishops, MPs, widows in Bath, and the ancestors of several later Prime Ministers. The sum was around 40 per cent of the Treasury's annual spending. It was borrowed, and the loan sat on the national books until the Treasury finished paying it off in 2015. If you paid British tax before 2015, you helped compensate slave-owners.

This chapter is about the system that connects those three things. It has two halves, and both matter equally: **how the empire was assembled**, colony by colony, over roughly 350 years; and **how it came apart**, territory by territory, in a rush that took barely fifty. Most textbooks are good at the first and vague about the second. Vagueness about endings is how an empire becomes a map-colouring exercise instead of a history of people.

> **▸ Q1.** Before reading on, write down what you already think the British Empire was. Keep it. You will be asked to revise it at the end.

## 7.1 A map in words: the empire at its greatest extent, 1921

Close your eyes and build it.

Begin at the **North Atlantic**. Newfoundland, cold and fishy, the oldest claim of all. Behind it the enormous bulk of **Canada**, ocean to ocean, self-governing since 1867. Drop south past the still-British **Bermuda** into the **Caribbean**, and the map goes measles-red: Jamaica, Trinidad, Barbados, the Bahamas, the Leewards and Windwards, and on the mainland **British Honduras** (Belize) and **British Guiana** (Guyana). These are small dots that once mattered more than all of Canada, because sugar was worth more than fur.

Cross to **West Africa** and there are four coastal wedges pushed inland — the Gambia, Sierra Leone, the Gold Coast (Ghana), and the vast rectangle of Nigeria. Follow the coast round and down: **South Africa**, a Union since 1910, with the High Commission Territories of Bechuanaland (Botswana), Basutoland (Lesotho) and Swaziland (Eswatini) embedded in or beside it like unswallowed pips. Then north up the eastern side in an almost unbroken column — **Southern Rhodesia** (Zimbabwe), **Northern Rhodesia** (Zambia), **Nyasaland** (Malawi), **Tanganyika** (mainland Tanzania, taken from Germany in the First World War), **Kenya**, **Uganda**, the **Anglo-Egyptian Sudan**, and **Egypt** itself. Cecil Rhodes's fantasy of a red line from Cape Town to Cairo was, by 1921, very nearly real.

Now the **Mediterranean and Middle East**: Gibraltar (1713), Malta (1814), Cyprus (occupied 1878, annexed 1914), and the new **League of Nations mandates** of Palestine, Transjordan and Iraq — plus a ring of protected sheikhdoms down the Persian Gulf and the port of Aden.

Then the centre of gravity of the whole thing: the **Indian Empire**, comprising today's India, Pakistan and Bangladesh, plus Burma (Myanmar) until 1937 — around 300 million people, more than three-quarters of everyone the British ruled. Off its southern tip, **Ceylon** (Sri Lanka). East of it, the Straits Settlements, the Malay states, North Borneo, Sarawak, Brunei — and, on a rock at the mouth of the Pearl River, **Hong Kong**.

Finally the **Pacific**: **Australia**, federated in 1901; **New Zealand**; Fiji; and a scatter of islands whose names are mostly forgotten in Britain and unforgettable where they are — Gilbert and Ellice, Solomon, Pitcairn, Nauru.

That is roughly **35.5 million square kilometres — about a quarter of the world's land surface — and around 412 million people**, something like one human being in four or five alive at the time. It is the largest empire in recorded history. It is also, by 1921, only two generations from disappearing.

> **▸ Q2.** Look at the sequence above. Which regions were joined to the empire by *settlement*, which by *conquest*, which by *company trade*, and which by *treaty with other European powers*? You will not be able to answer fully yet. Come back to this question at the end of 7.6.

## 7.2 Part One: Acquisition

### 7.2.1 The rehearsal: Ireland

The empire did not begin in Virginia or Bengal. It began, in method if not in name, next door.

England had claimed lordship over Ireland since the Anglo-Norman invasion of 1169. But the systematic version — the version that would be exported — was Tudor and Stuart. From the 1550s onward the English Crown ran **plantations**: confiscating land from Irish (and Old English Catholic) owners and granting it to Protestant settlers from England and Scotland. The **Ulster Plantation** from 1609 was the largest. Between 1649 and 1653 Oliver Cromwell's reconquest killed a catastrophic share of the Irish population through war, famine and plague, and transferred land ownership wholesale.

Here is why this matters for a chapter about the world. The vocabulary and the personnel were the same. Humphrey Gilbert, who claimed **Newfoundland** for Elizabeth I in 1583, and Walter Raleigh, who sponsored the failed **Roanoke** colony in 1585, had both served in the Irish wars. The idea that a "civilised" people could take land from a "barbarous" one, and that this was improvement rather than theft, was worked out on Irish soil first and shipped abroad afterwards.

> **COMPLICATION.** And yet the Irish were also *agents* of empire far out of proportion to their numbers — as soldiers, administrators, missionaries and settlers in Australia, India and the Caribbean. Ireland is simultaneously Britain's first colony and a full participant in the colonisation of others. Any frame that makes people purely victims or purely villains will break on Ireland. Hold that thought; it will apply to India, to Africa, to almost everywhere.

### 7.2.2 The Atlantic beginning, 1583–1707

The first English overseas ventures were shoestring, speculative and frequently disastrous. They were also mostly **private**. The Crown did not conquer America; it issued charters to companies and courtiers and took a cut.

- **1583** — Gilbert claims Newfoundland. He drowns on the way home.
- **1607** — **Jamestown**, Virginia, founded by the Virginia Company. Of the first several hundred settlers, most die. The colony survives only because it discovers a drug crop: tobacco.
- **1609–12** — **Bermuda**, settled after the wreck of the *Sea Venture*.
- **1620** — **Plymouth**, Massachusetts.
- **1623** — **St Kitts**, the first English Caribbean colony; **1627 Barbados**; **1655 Jamaica**, seized from Spain by Cromwell's "Western Design".
- **1670** — the **Hudson's Bay Company** is chartered, granted a monopoly over the drainage basin of Hudson Bay: about 3.9 million square kilometres of other people's country, signed away in a document none of its inhabitants saw.

### 7.2.3 Sugar, and the trade in human beings

Between roughly 1640 and 1660 Barbados changed from a place growing tobacco and cotton with mostly white indentured labour into a monoculture sugar island worked by enslaved Africans. That transformation is the hinge of the first British Empire.

Sugar was extraordinarily profitable and extraordinarily lethal to produce. Cutting cane, feeding the rollers of a mill, boiling the juice in open coppers in Caribbean heat: the work destroyed people. Planters found it cheaper to buy replacements than to allow the enslaved population to reproduce. That single accounting decision is why the Atlantic slave trade lasted as long as it did.

**The numbers.** The *Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database* — built from the records of more than 36,000 individual voyages — estimates that around **12.5 million** Africans were embarked on slave ships across the whole Atlantic trade, of whom about **10.7 million** survived to be landed in the Americas. **British and British-colonial ships carried roughly 3.1 million of them, and landed about 2.7 million.** Britain was, for most of the eighteenth century, the largest slave-trading power on earth.

Take one second over the gap between those pairs of numbers. Roughly one in seven people loaded onto a slave ship died before it landed — of dysentery, of thirst, of suicide, of being thrown overboard.

> **SOURCE A — Olaudah Equiano, *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano*, published in London, 1789.** Equiano, who said he was born in Igboland in what is now Nigeria, was enslaved as a child, later bought his freedom, and became a leading abolitionist campaigner in Britain. Describing the hold of the ship:
>
> "The shrieks of the women, and the groans of the dying, rendered the whole a scene of horror almost inconceivable."
>
> *Note for historians:* some scholars have questioned whether Equiano was born in Africa or in South Carolina. The debate is unresolved and worth knowing about — but it does not touch the accuracy of what he describes, which is corroborated by ships' logs, surgeons' journals and other testimony.

> **SOURCE B — *The History of Mary Prince, a West Indian Slave, Related by Herself*, London, 1831.** The first account of slavery published in Britain by a Black woman. Prince was born enslaved in Bermuda around 1788.
>
> "I have been a slave myself—I know what slaves feel."

> **▸ Q3.** Source A was published in 1789; Source B in 1831. The slave trade was abolished in 1807 and slavery itself in 1833. What might be the relationship between publications like these and those laws? Be careful: correlation is not proof.

**The wealth.** The profits did not stay in the Caribbean. They built Georgian Bristol, Liverpool and Glasgow; they capitalised banks and insurance houses; they endowed schools and country estates. University College London's *Legacies of British Slavery* project has traced the £20 million of 1833 compensation to individual recipients, and the map of that money is a map of the British establishment. This is not a fringe claim: it is a database built from the government's own Slave Compensation Commission records, and you can search it.

**COMPLICATION.** Slavery was not a British invention, and enslavement existed in Africa, in the Islamic world, in the Americas and in Europe long before 1600. African rulers and merchants sold captives to European traders, and some states — Dahomey, Asante, the Aro networks — were transformed by that commerce. None of that dilutes British responsibility. It does mean that "Europeans went and took people" is too simple a sentence: the trade was a partnership, hideously unequal in its consequences, in which Europeans mostly stayed on the coast and the violence of capture happened inland.

### 7.2.4 The company that became a state: India, 1600–1858

On 31 December 1600 Elizabeth I granted a charter to "the Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies". It was a joint-stock company chasing pepper. It ended up governing 200 million people.

The **East India Company (EIC)** established trading posts — "factories" — at Surat (1612), Madras (1639), Bombay (1661, arriving in the dowry of Catherine of Braganza) and Calcutta (1690). For 150 years it was a supplicant at the court of the Mughal emperors, who ruled one of the richest states on earth.

Two things changed that. The first was the slow fragmentation of Mughal power after the death of Aurangzeb in 1707, which produced successor states — Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, the Marathas, Mysore — competing with each other and open to alliance. The second was the **Anglo-French global war** of the mid-eighteenth century, which taught both companies to raise armies of Indian soldiers, **sepoys**, drilled in European style.

**23 June 1757: the Battle of Plassey.** Robert Clive's force defeated Siraj-ud-Daulah, Nawab of Bengal, largely because Clive had secretly bought off the Nawab's commander, Mir Jafar. It was less a battle than a coup executed by a corporation. In **1765**, by the Treaty of Allahabad (12 August), the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II granted the Company the **diwani** — the right to collect the land revenue — of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. A London trading firm now had a tax base of perhaps 20 million people.

What followed was the most concentrated extraction in the empire's history. The Company squeezed revenue while trade collapsed. In **1770** Bengal suffered a catastrophic famine. Contemporaries claimed up to ten million dead, perhaps a third of the population; modern historians generally argue for a lower figure, but nobody disputes it was one of the great mortality events of the century, or that Company revenue demands made it worse.

> **SOURCE C — Edmund Burke, speech opening the impeachment of Warren Hastings, House of Lords, 15 February 1788.** Burke, an Irish-born MP, spent years prosecuting the former Governor-General of Bengal for corruption and cruelty. Of the Company's servants he said they were:
>
> "birds of prey and passage"
>
> — young men who came, gorged, and left. Hastings was acquitted in 1795 after a seven-year trial. The impeachment failed, but it established in British public life the idea that empire could be a moral scandal.

By 1803 the Company's private army numbered around 260,000 men — roughly twice the size of the British Army — almost all of them Indian, commanded by Britons. Read that sentence twice. **The British conquest of India was carried out overwhelmingly by Indian soldiers.** Empire ran on collaboration: on sepoys who needed pay, on bankers who financed campaigns, on princes who preferred a British alliance to a Maratha one, on clerks and translators and revenue officials without whom a few thousand Britons could not have governed a subcontinent. Understanding this is not a defence of empire. It is the only way to explain how something so small held something so large — and it is also why the collapse, when collaboration failed, was so fast.

> **SOURCE D — Thomas Babington Macaulay, "Minute on Indian Education", 2 February 1835.** Macaulay was arguing that government money should fund English-language rather than Sanskrit and Persian education. He wanted to create:
>
> "a class of persons, Indian in blood and colour, but English in tastes, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect."
>
> **▸ Q4.** Macaulay got his policy. English-medium education produced clerks — and it also produced Gandhi (trained as a barrister in London), Nehru (Harrow and Cambridge), Jinnah (Lincoln's Inn) and Ambedkar (Columbia and LSE). What does that suggest about whether colonial rulers control the consequences of their own policies?

**1857.** In May 1857 sepoys of the Bengal Army mutinied at Meerut over many grievances — pay, caste, annexations, the fear of forced conversion, and the immediate trigger of new cartridges rumoured to be greased with cow and pig fat. The revolt spread across northern India and became something much wider than a mutiny: a rebellion joined by peasants, landholders and displaced rulers, with Bahadur Shah Zafar, the last Mughal, declared its figurehead. Indians call it the First War of Independence; the British called it the Mutiny; historians argue about both labels.

The rebellion killed British civilians, including women and children, at Kanpur and elsewhere. The reprisals were far larger and deliberately theatrical: villages burned, prisoners blown from the mouths of cannon, Delhi sacked. Estimates of Indian deaths run into the hundreds of thousands.

The political result was the **Government of India Act 1858**: the East India Company was abolished as a governing power and India passed to the Crown. In 1876 Parliament made Victoria **Empress of India**; the title was proclaimed at the Delhi Durbar on 1 January 1877. The **Raj** had begun — and with it a policy of never again disturbing India's social order, which meant ruling through princes, landlords and religious authorities, and codifying "communities" into rigid administrative categories. That last decision would still be killing people in 1947.

### 7.2.5 Losing America, 1775–1783

The Seven Years' War (1756–63) made Britain the dominant power in North America: the Treaty of Paris of 1763 handed over New France (Canada) and Florida. It also left Britain with enormous debts, and a government that thought the American colonists should help pay them.

They disagreed. Between the Stamp Act of 1765 and the Declaration of Independence on 4 July 1776, thirteen colonies moved from protest to revolution, and with French, Spanish and Dutch help, they won. The **Treaty of Paris of 3 September 1783** recognised the United States.

This is the single most important fact about the British Empire that students forget: **the empire's most successful colonies left it first, and by force.** Everything Britain did afterwards — granting settler colonies self-government early, keeping non-white colonies under direct rule — was shaped by the determination not to lose another America.

> **THINK ABOUT THIS.** Around 20,000 enslaved people fled to British lines during the American War, after British commanders offered freedom to those who joined them. At the war's end, roughly 3,000 were evacuated to Nova Scotia; hundreds later sailed on to found Freetown in **Sierra Leone** in 1792. So in this war, the empire was the road to freedom and the revolution was the threat to it. How do you write that into a story about British oppression — without either ignoring it or letting it excuse the 3.1 million?

### 7.2.6 The swing east, and the settler colonies

Losing America pushed British attention east and south.

**Australia.** James Cook charted the east coast in 1770 and claimed it for Britain. On **26 January 1788** the First Fleet landed at Sydney Cove — a penal colony, because Britain could no longer transport convicts to America. About 165,000 convicts were transported to Australia before the practice ended in 1868.

The legal basis for taking the continent was the doctrine that it was **terra nullius** — nobody's land — because Aboriginal Australians did not farm or fence in ways Europeans recognised. This was false when it was invented and it was formally demolished by Australia's High Court in the **Mabo** judgment of 3 June 1992. In between, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander populations collapsed through disease, dispossession and frontier killings; historians have documented hundreds of massacres, and the University of Newcastle's *Colonial Frontier Massacres* project maps them with sources.

**New Zealand.** On **6 February 1840**, at Waitangi, representatives of the Crown and around 500 Māori rangatira (chiefs) signed a treaty. There were two texts. The English version cedes "sovereignty" to the Queen. The Māori version, which almost all the signatories signed, cedes *kāwanatanga* — governorship — while guaranteeing chiefs their *tino rangatiratanga*, their full chieftainship or authority. These are not the same promise. The New Zealand Wars of the 1840s–1870s and the confiscation of over a million hectares of Māori land followed; the Waitangi Tribunal, established in 1975, has been adjudicating the gap between the two texts ever since.

> **▸ Q5.** A treaty in two languages that say different things. Was this a translation error, a deliberate deception, or two parties each hearing what they wanted? What kind of evidence would let you decide?

**Canada.** After the 1837 rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada, Lord Durham's *Report on the Affairs of British North America* (1839) recommended **responsible government** — a local ministry answerable to a local assembly. It was granted in Nova Scotia and Canada in 1848 and became the template. On **1 July 1867** the British North America Act created the **Dominion of Canada**. This invention — self-governing dominions that stayed loyal — was Britain's answer to 1776, and it worked, for white settlers.

**COMPLICATION.** "Responsible government" was responsible to settlers. In the same decades, Canada was signing the Numbered Treaties with First Nations and building the residential school system, which ran into the 1990s and which Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission described in 2015 as **cultural genocide**. Self-government for one population was intensified rule over another. The dominions were not the empire's exit; for indigenous peoples they were its most permanent form.

### 7.2.7 Informal empire: the parts of the map that were never coloured red

Not all imperial power required a flag. Britain's nineteenth-century dominance rested at least as much on **informal empire**: gunboats, unequal treaties, loans, and the simple fact of owning most of the world's shipping and half its industrial capacity. Argentina was never British-ruled, but by 1914 British capital owned its railways. China was never British-ruled, but Britain forced open its ports.

**CASE STUDY: The Opium Wars, 1839–1860 — the drug war Britain fought to keep selling drugs.**

Britain wanted Chinese tea. China wanted very little Britain made, and demanded silver. The East India Company solved its balance-of-payments problem by growing opium in Bengal and Bihar and selling it, through intermediaries, into China, where it was illegal. By the 1830s this was one of the largest commodity trades on earth.

In 1839 the Qing commissioner **Lin Zexu** seized and destroyed some 1,200 tonnes of opium at Canton and wrote to Queen Victoria asking how she could permit a trade forbidden in her own country. Britain sent a fleet.

> **SOURCE E — William Ewart Gladstone, House of Commons, 8 April 1840**, opposing the war (he was then a young Tory MP; he would later be Prime Minister four times):
>
> "a war more unjust in its origin, a war more calculated in its progress to cover this country with permanent disgrace, I do not know and I have not read of."

Britain won anyway. The **Treaty of Nanking (29 August 1842)** opened five ports, imposed an indemnity, and ceded **Hong Kong Island** in perpetuity. A second war brought the Convention of Peking (1860) and **Kowloon**; in 1898 Britain took the **New Territories** on a 99-year lease. That lease is why Hong Kong went back to China in 1997 — a war over narcotics in 1839 set a clock that ran out 158 years later.

> **▸ Q6.** Gladstone opposed the Opium War and later, as Prime Minister, ordered the occupation of Egypt (1882). What does that tell you about how far individual conscience could restrain imperial expansion?

### 7.2.8 The Scramble for Africa, 1870–1914

In 1870 European powers controlled around a tenth of Africa, mostly coastline. By 1914 they controlled all of it except Ethiopia and Liberia. It is the fastest partition of a continent in history, and Britain took the largest share.

Why then? Several pressures converged: quinine made the interior survivable for Europeans; the Maxim gun (1884) made resistance suicidal in open battle; steamships and the **Suez Canal** (opened 1869) made Egypt strategically essential to the route to India; and rivalry with a newly unified Germany and a resurgent France turned African territory into a counter in a European game.

The **Berlin Conference** (15 November 1884 – 26 February 1885), convened by Bismarck, did not carve up Africa on a map — that legend is too tidy — but it set the rules by which the powers would recognise each other's claims, above all the principle of "effective occupation". No African was present.

> **SOURCE F — Lord Salisbury, Prime Minister, speaking in 1890 about the Anglo-French agreement on West Africa:**
>
> "We have been engaged in drawing lines upon maps where no white man's foot ever trod; we have been giving away mountains and rivers and lakes to each other, only hindered by the small impediment that we never knew exactly where the mountains and rivers and lakes were."

Those lines are still there. Roughly 44 per cent of Africa's international borders are straight lines drawn in European chancelleries; they cut through the territory of hundreds of language groups and have shaped every border conflict since.

> **SOURCE G — Cecil Rhodes, from his "Confession of Faith", 1877.** Rhodes made a fortune from De Beers diamonds, became Prime Minister of the Cape Colony, and had two countries named after him:
>
> "I contend that we are the finest race in the world and that the more of the world we inhabit the better it is for the human race."

The British acquisitions came fast: Egypt occupied in 1882 after the bombardment of Alexandria and the defeat of Ahmed Urabi's nationalist movement at Tel el-Kebir (13 September 1882) — an occupation announced as temporary that lasted 74 years. Bechuanaland protectorate 1885. The British South Africa Company chartered 1889, which took what became the Rhodesias. Zanzibar 1890. Uganda 1894. The East Africa Protectorate (Kenya) 1895. Northern and Southern Nigeria 1900, amalgamated 1914. Sudan reconquered 1896–98 — at Omdurman on 2 September 1898, roughly 10,000 Sudanese were killed against fewer than 50 on the Anglo-Egyptian side. That ratio is what the Maxim gun meant.

**CASE STUDY: the South African War, 1899–1902, and the invention of the concentration camp.**

Britain fought the two Boer republics — the Transvaal and the Orange Free State — over political rights for British settlers and, not incidentally, over the world's richest goldfield on the Witwatersrand. Britain expected a short war. Instead the Boers fought a long guerrilla campaign, and Britain responded by burning farms and interning civilians.

Around **28,000 Boer civilians died in the camps, some 22,000 of them children under sixteen**, mostly of measles, typhoid and dysentery in overcrowded tents on short rations. In separate, worse-supplied camps, **at least 14,000 Black Africans died** — the true figure is probably higher, because nobody counted carefully.

The camps became public in Britain because of **Emily Hobhouse**, a Cornish welfare campaigner who visited them in 1901 and published what she saw. The Liberal leader **Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman** asked, in a speech on 14 June 1901: "When is a war not a war? When it is carried on by methods of barbarism in South Africa."

> **THINK ABOUT THIS.** Hobhouse and Campbell-Bannerman prove that people at the time knew, and objected, in the language we would use now. That destroys the excuse "you cannot judge them by our standards." But it also complicates the picture: British public opinion contained abolitionists and slave-traders, Hobhouse and Kitchener, all at once. "The British" is not one thing. Whose Britain are you talking about?

The war ended in 1902 with British victory. In **1910** the Union of South Africa was created, joining British colonies and Boer republics — and the price of that reconciliation between whites was paid by Black South Africans, whose limited Cape franchise was frozen and then destroyed. The Natives Land Act of 1913 restricted African land ownership to about 7 per cent of the country. Apartheid, formalised after 1948, was built on foundations laid by the British Empire.

### 7.2.9 The last enlargement: mandates, 1919–1922

The First World War is when the empire is at its most visibly global. Around **1.3 million Indians** served, some 74,000 dying; roughly a million Africans served as soldiers and carriers, with carrier deaths in East Africa running into the hundreds of thousands; Canadians died at Vimy, Australians and New Zealanders at Gallipoli, West Indians in Palestine and France.

Colonial subjects were promised things. Indians were promised progress toward self-government (the Montagu Declaration, 20 August 1917). Arabs were promised independence in correspondence between Sharif Hussein of Mecca and Sir Henry McMahon (1915–16). Zionists were promised "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine by the **Balfour Declaration** of 2 November 1917. Meanwhile Britain and France had secretly agreed to divide the Ottoman Middle East between themselves in the **Sykes–Picot Agreement** of May 1916.

These promises were not all compatible. After the war the League of Nations awarded Britain **mandates** over Palestine, Transjordan and Iraq, and over Tanganyika and part of Togoland and Cameroon. A mandate was supposed to be a trusteeship leading to independence. In practice it looked a great deal like a colony with a euphemism.

**Amritsar.** On **13 April 1919**, in Jallianwala Bagh, an enclosed garden in Amritsar, Punjab, Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer ordered troops to fire without warning on a large crowd gathered in defiance of a ban on public meetings. The firing continued for about ten minutes; 1,650 rounds were expended. The official Hunter Committee put the dead at **379**, with over 1,200 wounded; the Indian National Congress's own inquiry estimated over 1,000 killed. Dyer told the Hunter Committee he had fired not merely to disperse the crowd but to produce "a sufficient moral effect" throughout the Punjab. He was relieved of command — and a public subscription in Britain raised over £26,000 for him.

> **SOURCE H — Rabindranath Tagore, letter to the Viceroy, Lord Chelmsford, 31 May 1919**, renouncing the knighthood he had been given in 1915. Tagore had won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913:
>
> "The time has come when badges of honour make our shame glaring in their incongruous context of humiliation, and I for my part wish to stand, shorn of all special distinctions, by the side of those of my countrymen who, for their so-called insignificance, are liable to suffer degradation not fit for human beings."

Amritsar is conventionally the moment when a mass of Indian opinion stopped believing that the Raj could be reformed. Within a year Gandhi had launched the Non-Cooperation Movement. **The empire reached its greatest size and lost its moral licence in the same three years.**

## 7.3 The machinery: how so few ruled so many

Before the second half of the story, understand the mechanisms. Four of them do most of the work.

**1. Indirect rule.** Britain almost never had enough administrators. In the 1930s the Indian Civil Service — the elite cadre governing some 350 million people — numbered barely a thousand men. So Britain ruled *through* existing authorities: 565 princely states in India, emirs in northern Nigeria, chiefs in Uganda and the Gold Coast. **Sir Frederick Lugard** codified this in *The Dual Mandate in British Tropical Africa* (1922). Where suitable authorities did not exist, Britain invented them — the "warrant chiefs" of southeastern Nigeria, imposed on societies that had governed themselves without chiefs, and violently rejected in the Women's War (Ogu Umunwaanyi) of 1929.

**2. Classification.** Colonial states are counting machines. Censuses, land settlements and law codes required people to belong to fixed categories: this caste, that tribe, this religious community. Categories that had been fluid became legal facts with consequences for taxation, army recruitment (the "martial races" doctrine) and, from the Morley–Minto reforms of 1909, for who could vote for whom. **Separate electorates** made religion the basic unit of Indian politics. You cannot understand 1947 without 1909.

**3. Racial hierarchy, formal and informal.** Colour bars in clubs, hotels, railway carriages and promotion ladders were rarely written into imperial statute but were nearly universal in practice. In Kenya's "White Highlands", African ownership of the best farmland was prohibited outright.

**4. Force, held in reserve and used.** The empire's normal condition was not war but the credible threat of it. When the threat failed, the response was punitive: expeditions, collective fines, burned villages, and — after 1919 — aerial bombing of villages in Iraq, Waziristan and Somaliland, a technique Britain pioneered as cheap "air policing".

> **▸ Q7.** Of these four mechanisms, which do you think left the deepest mark after independence? Argue for one.

## 7.4 Part Two: Dissolution

### 7.4.1 Why empires end

There is no single cause, but four forces recur, and in most territories at least three are present:

1. **Cost.** Empire was affordable while Britain was the world's workshop and banker. Two world wars ended that. Britain finished 1945 with debts of over £3 billion (much of it owed, awkwardly, to India), dependent on an American loan negotiated by Keynes.
2. **Nationalism with mass organisation behind it.** Educated elites had demanded reform since the 1880s. What made the twentieth century different was that they learned to mobilise peasants, workers and women — Gandhi's Salt March of 1930, Nkrumah's "Positive Action" strikes of 1950.
3. **A changed world order.** After 1945 the two superpowers were, for different reasons, anti-colonial. The UN Charter (1945) spoke of self-determination; UN General Assembly Resolution 1514 (14 December 1960) declared colonialism must end. The moral vocabulary of international politics had turned against empire.
4. **Violence that failed and violence that succeeded.** Sometimes armed resistance forced the pace; sometimes British repression "won" militarily and lost politically. Both routes ended in the same place.

> **COMPLICATION.** Decolonisation was not simply a gift, and it was not simply a defeat. British governments frequently tried to *manage* withdrawal: to leave behind friendly regimes, military bases, sterling balances, and constitutions of their own design. Many of those constitutions collapsed within a decade. Judge the empire not only by how it ruled but by what it left standing.

### 7.4.2 Ireland leaves first (1922)

Ireland had been merged into the United Kingdom by the **Act of Union of 1801**. Between 1845 and 1852 the **Great Famine** killed around one million people and drove between one and one and a half million to emigrate, while food continued to be exported under armed guard and the government insisted the market must not be interfered with. Ireland's population has never recovered its pre-famine level.

The **Easter Rising** of 24–29 April 1916 was militarily hopeless and initially unpopular; the execution of fifteen leaders in May transformed opinion. Sinn Féin swept the 1918 election and set up its own parliament; the War of Independence ran from 1919 to 1921. The **Anglo-Irish Treaty of 6 December 1921** created the **Irish Free State** (formally established 6 December 1922) as a dominion, with six northern counties remaining in the UK. A civil war followed among Irish nationalists over the Treaty's terms. Ireland became a republic under the Republic of Ireland Act 1948, effective **18 April 1949**, and left the Commonwealth. Partition remains.

**Why it matters:** Ireland gave every later nationalist movement a working template — boycott, an alternative parliament, guerrilla war, negotiation, partition as the price. Indian, Israeli, Egyptian and Cypriot activists all studied it.

### 7.4.3 CASE STUDY: India and Pakistan, 1947 — freedom and catastrophe together

**The build-up.** The Indian National Congress was founded in 1885 as a loyal debating body; by the 1930s it was a mass party. Gandhi's genius was to make constitutional argument into physical, symbolic, participatory action: the Salt March of March–April 1930, in which he walked 240 miles to the sea to make salt in defiance of a state monopoly, drew tens of thousands and made the tax look absurd worldwide. The All-India Muslim League, led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, moved from demanding safeguards to demanding, in the **Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940**, a separate state.

**The war that broke the Raj.** In 1939 the Viceroy declared India at war with Germany without consulting a single Indian politician; Congress ministries resigned. Around **2.5 million Indians volunteered** for the armed forces — the largest volunteer army in history — while Congress launched **Quit India** in August 1942 and its leadership spent most of the war in prison.

Then, in 1943, **between two and three million people died of famine in Bengal**. There was no absolute shortage of food in India. There was war-panic hoarding, the loss of Burmese rice imports, a "denial policy" that removed boats and rice from coastal districts to deny them to a Japanese invasion, price inflation, and a refusal by London to divert shipping for relief. The economist **Amartya Sen**, who was a nine-year-old in Bengal at the time, later built his Nobel-recognised theory of famine on it: famines, he argued in *Poverty and Famines* (1981), are usually failures of **entitlement** — of who can command food — not of food supply. Britain's wartime leadership does not emerge well. Churchill's own Secretary of State for India, **Leo Amery**, recorded in his diary in 1942 that Churchill said "I hate Indians. They are a beastly people with a beastly religion," and wrote that he did not see much difference between Churchill's outlook and Hitler's.

**The end.** A Labour government elected in 1945 faced a bankrupt treasury, a mutiny in the Royal Indian Navy (February 1946), and communal killing after the Muslim League's Direct Action Day in Calcutta (16 August 1946). Lord Mountbatten, appointed Viceroy in March 1947, brought the deadline forward by ten months.

**The line.** **Sir Cyril Radcliffe**, a British barrister who had never been to India, arrived on 8 July 1947 and was given about five weeks to draw the borders of Punjab and Bengal, using out-of-date maps and census returns. Independence came at midnight on 14/15 August 1947. The boundary award was not published until **17 August** — two days *after* independence. Millions did not know which country they were in.

> **SOURCE I — Jawaharlal Nehru, address to the Constituent Assembly, New Delhi, midnight, 14–15 August 1947:**
>
> "At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom."

> **SOURCE J — W. H. Auden, "Partition" (1966), on Radcliffe's task:** the poet notes drily that "the maps at his disposal were out of date."

**The cost.** Estimates of the dead in the Partition massacres range from around 200,000 to two million; the range itself tells you how little anyone was counting. Between 10 and 15 million people were displaced — the largest forced migration in recorded history. Tens of thousands of women were abducted and raped. Gandhi was assassinated on 30 January 1948 by a Hindu nationalist who thought him too conciliatory toward Pakistan. Kashmir was left disputed and has been fought over in 1947–48, 1965 and 1999.

> **THINK ABOUT THIS.** Who is responsible for Partition? Candidates: the British, for a century of governing through religious categories and then leaving at speed; the Muslim League, for demanding a state; Congress, for refusing federal compromise; local politicians and militias who organised the killing. Historians distribute the blame differently — compare Yasmin Khan's *The Great Partition* (2007) with Ayesha Jalal's *The Sole Spokesman* (1985). **Rank the candidates and defend your ranking in six sentences.**

### 7.4.4 Palestine, and the mandate Britain walked out of

Britain had promised the same land twice. Jewish immigration to Palestine rose sharply in the 1930s as Nazi persecution intensified; the **Arab Revolt of 1936–39** was suppressed with over 100 executions, house demolitions and thousands of deaths. The 1939 White Paper then restricted Jewish immigration at the worst possible moment, and after 1945 Zionist paramilitaries turned on the British. Unable to satisfy anyone, Britain handed the problem to the United Nations, which voted for partition on 29 November 1947, and withdrew on **14 May 1948**. War began the same day. The consequences are today's news.

### 7.4.5 Suez, 1956: the year Britain found out

On 26 July 1956 Egypt's President **Gamal Abdel Nasser** nationalised the Suez Canal Company. Britain, France and Israel secretly agreed a plan: Israel would invade Sinai, Britain and France would "intervene to separate the combatants" and seize the canal. It worked militarily and collapsed politically. The United States, furious at not being told, refused to support sterling in a run on the pound unless Britain withdrew. Britain withdrew in December 1956. Prime Minister Anthony Eden resigned in January 1957.

Suez is the hinge of the second half of this chapter. It demonstrated that Britain could no longer act as a great power against American wishes; it made Nasser a hero across the colonised world; and it convinced a generation of British officials that holding colonies by force was no longer viable.

### 7.4.6 Africa: from Ghana to Zimbabwe, 1957–1980

On **6 March 1957**, the Gold Coast became **Ghana** — the first sub-Saharan African colony to gain independence. Its leader, **Kwame Nkrumah**, had been imprisoned by the British in 1950 and won an election from his cell.

> **SOURCE K — Kwame Nkrumah, Accra, midnight, 6 March 1957:**
>
> "At long last, the battle has ended! And thus, Ghana, your beloved country, is free forever."

Three years later a Conservative Prime Minister said the quiet part aloud.

> **SOURCE L — Harold Macmillan to the Parliament of South Africa, Cape Town, 3 February 1960:**
>
> "The wind of change is blowing through this continent, and, whether we like it or not, this growth of national consciousness is a political fact."

What followed was extraordinarily fast. In 1960 alone, Nigeria, Somaliland and Cyprus became independent. Between 1957 and 1968 Britain gave up almost all of Africa.

**CASE STUDY: Kenya and the Emergency, 1952–1960 — the war Britain tried to delete.**

Kenya had white settlers, and settlers made decolonisation violent. Around 30,000 Europeans held the fertile "White Highlands"; Kikuyu families who had lost land there became squatters or slum-dwellers. From 1952 an oath-bound insurgency — the British called it **Mau Mau**, its fighters called themselves the Land and Freedom Army — attacked settlers and, far more often, Africans who cooperated with the government. A State of Emergency was declared on **20 October 1952** and lasted until 1960.

The counter-insurgency involved mass detention without trial, forced "villagisation" of over a million Kikuyu into guarded settlements, and a screening system in which torture — beatings, burning, sexual violence — was systematic. Official figures record more than 11,000 insurgents killed and **1,090 hanged**, the largest use of capital punishment in any British counter-insurgency. Thirty-two white settlers were killed in the entire Emergency.

**How many were detained is disputed, and the dispute is instructive.** Official records give around 80,000. **Caroline Elkins**, in *Britain's Gulag* (US title *Imperial Reckoning*, 2005), argued from oral testimony and demographic analysis that the real figure passing through the camp system was far higher — up to 320,000 — and that tens of thousands died. **David Anderson** (*Histories of the Hanged*, 2005) and others accept the brutality but regard Elkins's highest numbers as unsupported. Both books won major prizes. Both are worth reading against each other.

What is no longer disputed is the cover-up. In 2011, during a case brought by five elderly Kenyan claimants, the Foreign Office admitted it held a secret archive at **Hanslope Park** containing thousands of files removed from colonies at independence — with other files deliberately destroyed. On **6 June 2013**, Foreign Secretary William Hague told the House of Commons that the British government "sincerely regrets" the abuses, and announced a settlement of **£19.9 million** shared among **5,228** Kenyan claimants.

> **▸ Q8.** The Hanslope Park files existed for fifty years before anyone outside government saw them. What does that tell you about the relationship between archives, historians and power? What might still be missing from the record you are being taught from?

**Where settlers were strongest, decolonisation was worst.** In Southern Rhodesia, the white minority government of Ian Smith issued a **Unilateral Declaration of Independence on 11 November 1965** rather than accept majority rule. A fifteen-year guerrilla war followed; the Lancaster House Agreement of December 1979 ended it, and **Zimbabwe** became independent on **18 April 1980**. South Africa, already independent in white hands, became a republic and left the Commonwealth on 31 May 1961; apartheid ended in 1994.

### 7.4.7 Asia, the Caribbean, the Gulf, the Pacific

**Southeast Asia.** Britain fought the **Malayan Emergency** (1948–60) against a mostly Chinese-Malayan communist insurgency, resettling some 500,000 people into fortified "New Villages" — a model later exported to Vietnam — and committing atrocities of its own, including the killing of 24 unarmed villagers at Batang Kali in December 1948. Malaya became independent on **31 August 1957**; Malaysia was formed on 16 September 1963; Singapore was expelled from it on **9 August 1965** and became a state by accident.

**The Caribbean.** Britain's plan was a single federation. The **West Indies Federation** (1958–62) collapsed when Jamaican voters rejected it in a 1961 referendum, and the islands went separately: Jamaica and Trinidad and Tobago in 1962, Barbados and Guyana in 1966, and the smaller states through the 1970s and 1980s, ending with St Kitts and Nevis in 1983. Barbados removed the Queen as head of state and became a republic on **30 November 2021**.

**The Gulf and "east of Suez".** In January 1968 the Wilson government announced that Britain could no longer afford military commitments east of Suez. Aden had already been abandoned in November 1967 after a bitter insurgency; Bahrain, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates all became independent in 1971 — several of their rulers reportedly offering to pay for the British garrisons to stay.

### 7.4.8 The last acts: Hong Kong, and what is left

Hong Kong's fate was fixed by the 99-year lease of 1898. The **Sino-British Joint Declaration** of 19 December 1984 committed Britain to hand the whole territory to China on **1 July 1997** under a "one country, two systems" formula. Britain had never allowed Hong Kong's people to elect their own government; it introduced significant democratic reform only in the 1990s, on the way out. Roughly 3.4 million Hong Kong residents held a British nationality that carried no right to live in Britain.

Fourteen **British Overseas Territories** remain, from the Falklands (over which Britain fought Argentina in 1982) to Gibraltar, Bermuda, the Pitcairn Islands and the British Antarctic Territory.

**And one unfinished expulsion.** In 1965 Britain detached the Chagos Archipelago from Mauritius to create the **British Indian Ocean Territory**, then between 1968 and 1973 removed the entire population — somewhere between 1,500 and 2,000 Chagossians — so that the United States could build an airbase on **Diego Garcia**. Their dogs were gassed; the people were dumped in Mauritius and the Seychelles. In February 2019 the **International Court of Justice** advised that the detachment was unlawful and that Britain should end its administration; in October 2024 Britain agreed to transfer sovereignty to Mauritius, with a treaty signed in May 2025 retaining the base on a 99-year lease.

> **▸ Q9.** Chagos happened in the 1960s and 1970s — after the wind of change, after most of Africa was free, within the lifetime of people you know. What does it do to the idea that empire is "the past"?

## 7.5 The balance sheet: did empire pay, and who paid?

This is where the argument is fiercest. Three positions worth knowing:

**Position 1: extraction.** **Dadabhai Naoroji**, an Indian MP in the British Parliament, argued in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901) that India suffered a continuous **"drain of wealth"** — revenue raised in India spent on British administration, pensions, and wars, and paid for by Indian exports that returned no equivalent. Angus Maddison's estimates put India at roughly a quarter of world GDP around 1700 and about 4 per cent by 1950. Paul Bairoch estimated India's share of world manufacturing output falling from around 25 per cent in 1750 to about 2 per cent by 1900. Britain deindustrialised Bengali textiles with tariffs and machine competition. The economist **Utsa Patnaik** has put a figure of nearly $45 trillion on the drain from India between 1765 and 1938; the number is widely quoted (notably by **Shashi Tharoor**) and widely contested, because it depends on compound-interest assumptions that other economists reject. *Cite it, but cite the argument about it too.*

**Position 2: empire was expensive for Britain too.** **Patrick O'Brien** argued (1988) that the empire was not necessary to British industrial growth, and **Lance Davis and Robert Huttenback** (*Mammon and the Pursuit of Empire*, 1986) calculated that imperial defence was subsidised by ordinary British taxpayers for the benefit of a narrow investor elite. On this reading empire was less a national profit machine than a transfer from British workers and colonised subjects alike to a small class of shareholders and officials.

**Position 3: the ledger of goods.** **Niall Ferguson** (*Empire*, 2003) argues that Britain spread free trade, law, property rights, English and parliamentary institutions, and that the counterfactual — Belgian, German or Japanese rule — was worse. **Shashi Tharoor** (*Inglorious Empire*, 2017) replies that railways were built for extraction and paid for by Indian taxpayers at guaranteed returns to British investors; that the rule of law coexisted with the Rowlatt Acts and Amritsar; and that Britain does not get credit for institutions Indians built and defended themselves.

> **THINK ABOUT THIS.** Notice that Positions 1 and 2 can both be true: an empire can impoverish its subjects *and* fail to enrich the average citizen of the metropole. "Was it worth it?" is the wrong question, because it assumes a single "it" and a single set of books. Better questions: **worth it to whom? measured how? compared with what?**

**One more statistic that belongs here.** After the abolition of slavery, plantation economies needed labour. Between 1834 and 1917 roughly **1.3 million Indians** went as **indentured** workers under long contracts to Mauritius, British Guiana, Trinidad, Fiji, Natal and elsewhere. That single policy is why there are Indo-Trinidadians, Indo-Fijians and Indo-Guyanese today — and it is a reminder that the empire did not only extract wealth. It rearranged where humanity lives.

## 7.6 The empire in the present tense

**Migration.** "We are here because you were there," the Sri Lankan-British writer **Ambalavaner Sivanandan** said, and it is the single most useful sentence about modern Britain. The 1948 British Nationality Act made hundreds of millions of colonial subjects British citizens; Britain then spent thirty years legislating that right away — the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968, the Immigration Act 1971 — while Enoch Powell's speech of 20 April 1968 made hostility respectable. In 2018 the **Windrush scandal** revealed that people who had arrived legally as children before 1973 had been denied NHS care, sacked, detained, and in dozens of confirmed cases wrongly removed or deported (the Home Office identified 164 such cases for review), partly because the Home Office had destroyed the landing cards that proved their arrival. The 2020 *Windrush Lessons Learned Review* by Wendy Williams found "institutional ignorance and thoughtlessness towards the issue of race" in the department.

**Museums.** In February 1897 a British punitive expedition sacked Benin City, in what is now Nigeria, and removed several thousand brass and ivory objects — the **Benin Bronzes** — many auctioned to defray the expedition's cost. They are in about 160 museums worldwide, roughly 900 in the British Museum. Since 2021 some have gone back: Jesus College, Cambridge returned a bronze cockerel; the Horniman Museum in London agreed in 2022 to transfer ownership of 72 objects to Nigeria. The British Museum has not, citing the British Museum Act 1963.

**Borders and states.** Kashmir, the India–Pakistan border, the Israel–Palestine conflict, the Nigeria of three unequal regions, the Iraq of three provinces welded together in 1920, the Ireland of six counties: these are not accidents of local history. They are British administrative decisions still under load.

**Money and apology.** The **CARICOM Ten-Point Plan for Reparatory Justice** (2014) asks European states for a formal apology, debt cancellation, and investment in health and education, on the argument that the underdevelopment of the Caribbean was manufactured. Britain has expressed "regret" but not apologised for slavery, and has refused reparations.

**And the Commonwealth**, 56 states and around 2.7 billion people, which now includes countries Britain never ruled — Mozambique, Rwanda, Gabon, Togo. Its existence is the strongest evidence that decolonisation produced something other than pure rupture, and that former colonies made their own choices about what to keep.

## 7.7 Summary: the chapter in twelve sentences

1. The British Empire was assembled over roughly 350 years, mostly by private companies and settlers before the state caught up, and it was dismantled in about 50.
2. Ireland was the rehearsal: plantation, dispossession and a language of civilising others, exported outward from the 1550s.
3. The first empire was Atlantic and built on sugar and slavery: British ships carried around 3.1 million enslaved Africans, and British taxpayers compensated the enslavers, not the enslaved.
4. India was conquered by a corporation using Indian soldiers and Indian money, and passed to the Crown only after the rebellion of 1857.
5. Losing the American colonies in 1783 taught Britain to give white settler colonies self-government early — a policy that produced Canada, Australia and New Zealand as dominions, and intensified rule over indigenous peoples inside them.
6. Much British power was **informal** — debt, gunboats and unequal treaties — which is why maps understate it.
7. The Scramble for Africa (1884–1914) drew borders that still shape conflicts, and was made possible by medicine, machine guns and European rivalry rather than by African weakness.
8. The empire reached its maximum extent around 1921, and in the same years — Amritsar 1919, the Irish war, Egyptian and Iraqi revolts — lost the consent it had always partly relied on.
9. Two world wars bankrupted Britain, mass nationalist movements made colonies ungovernable, and a post-1945 international order delegitimised colonial rule.
10. Where settlers were entrenched — Kenya, Rhodesia, South Africa, Ireland — decolonisation was violent; where they were not, it was usually negotiated but not therefore peaceful, as Partition's million dead attest.
11. Britain concealed the worst of its late-colonial record, and much of what we now know came out only after the Hanslope Park archive surfaced in 2011.
12. The empire is not over as a subject: it is present in Britain's population, its museums, its wealth, its borders abroad, and its unresolved arguments about apology and repair.

## 7.9 Glossary

**Asiento** — the contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America, won by Britain in 1713.
**Crown colony** — a colony governed directly from London, without an elected legislature controlling the executive.
**Diwani** — the Mughal right to collect land revenue, granted to the East India Company in Bengal in 1765.
**Dominion** — a self-governing settler state within the empire (Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Irish Free State, Newfoundland).
**Entitlement (Sen)** — the bundle of resources and rights that lets a person command food; famines are often failures of entitlement, not of supply.
**Indentured labour** — long-contract migrant labour, largely Indian and Chinese, used after the abolition of slavery.
**Indirect rule** — governing through existing (or invented) local authorities.
**Informal empire** — economic and military dominance without formal sovereignty.
**Mandate** — a territory taken from Germany or the Ottoman Empire after 1918 and administered under League of Nations supervision.
**Protectorate** — a nominally sovereign state whose foreign policy and much else Britain controlled.
**Raj** — British Crown rule in India, 1858–1947.
**Responsible government** — a colonial executive answerable to an elected colonial legislature.
**Sepoy** — an Indian soldier in European-officered service.
**Terra nullius** — "nobody's land"; the legal fiction used to claim Australia, overturned by *Mabo* in 1992.
