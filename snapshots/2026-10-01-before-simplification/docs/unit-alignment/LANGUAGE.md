## Decision

Keep an English argument as the final product. Reduce each station to one named operation and one short response, with a specific source detail. Make language help optional and small enough to use while writing. Build one or two close-reading moments around actual supplied wording; historical paraphrases alone do not practise analysis of an author's language. Do not increase workload by making students complete a glossary, planning grid, oral presentation and paragraph at every stop.

The most useful alignment is a repeated move students can later transfer to the Fletcher task: **identify exact wording → explain what it presents or implies → support or qualify that interpretation**. This should not prescribe a political judgement or treat an author, government and all British people as one speaker.

## Operators and output: exact student-facing definitions

Show only the relevant operation with a concrete product near the top of a stage. These are activity instructions, not purported verbatim official examination definitions.

| Label           | Short meaning                                                                                                   |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Outline         | Give the main points relevant to the question, in your own words.                                               |
| Explain         | Show how or why the events are connected. Use a source detail and explain the connection.                       |
| Analyse wording | Select a word or short phrase. Explain what it presents or suggests, and how this serves the speaker's purpose. |
| Compare         | Use the same point of comparison for both examples. Explain a relevant similarity or difference.                |
| Evaluate        | Reach a judgement. Weigh relevant evidence and explain a limitation or counterargument.                         |
| Rewrite         | Produce a new version for the stated audience. Use the evidence to decide what to retain or change.             |
| Comment         | Give and support your view. Consider a relevant complication or opposing argument.                              |

Avoid using “analyse” as a generic synonym for “write about.” A task that only asks what happened is not language analysis. Students need the exact excerpt visible if the task asks them to examine a word. Appropriate existing anchors include Cotton's threat and Victoria's equal-protection promise; only assign a Mau Mau wording analysis if the actual words are added and clearly distinguished from the editor's paraphrase.

The current stops ask for a mechanism plus a modern omission; a panel revision plus resistance plus chronology plus significance; a political comparison plus official-source limitation; a constitutional change plus human consequence plus hypothesis plus a research plan; and similarly dense later products. Retain a primary operation and move repeated qualifications to the final synthesis or optional challenge. The task author owns the final prompt wording.

## Exact optional language-help copy

Disclosure label: **Language support · optional**

One-line introduction: **Use a phrase if it helps. Complete it with your own evidence and reasoning.**

Use no more than two stems and three lexical chunks at a station. The stems below contain no worked historical answer. Terms combine essential reading vocabulary with carefully chosen transfer from the Britishness sheet. A short gloss may be English only; a German translation is not necessary for every term. The task remains in English.

### Stop 1: explanation (`profit-and-power`)

Stems:

- “The evidence links … to … because …”
- “This helps explain …, although …”

Terms:

- **revenue** — money received, for example through taxes
- **to exert influence on** — to affect a person's decisions or a situation
- **a consequence of** — a result of something

“Exert influence on” transfers vocabulary sheet A5, retaining its preposition. Do not provide a completed tax/army/control chain in this help block.

### Stop 2: wording and agency (`freedom-and-memory`)

Stems:

- “The word or phrase ‘…’ presents … as …”
- “For its audience, this could suggest … because …”

Terms:

- **to imply** — to suggest without stating directly
- **a grievance** — a reason for feeling unfairly treated
- **to voice concerns about** — to express worries about something

The latter two transfer B15–16. Keep the quotation slot empty and do not identify the effect, tone or missing actor for students.

If the task author's proposed **museum-label rewrite** replaces the wording-analysis task, use only these audience-oriented stems instead: “This panel explains how …” / “The evidence from … shows …”. Optional support line: “Use clear sentences for visitors your age. Name the people or institutions you mean.” Do not require a separate analytical explanation as well as the revised label.

### Stop 3: comparison and claim (`rule-and-resistance`)

Stems:

- “Whereas …, the other source …”
- “The phrase ‘…’ suggests …, but does not establish …”

Terms:

- **impartial** — treating different sides fairly
- **a disconnect between … and …** — a lack of agreement between two things
- **to establish** — to show with evidence

“Disconnect between … and …” transfers A11; students must decide whether evidence actually supports a disconnect. A promise alone does not establish either successful implementation or universal failure.

### Stop 4: evidence and inference (`departure-and-division`)

Stems:

- “The map records …; the source adds …”
- “This may help explain …; to test this, I would need …”

Terms:

- **displacement** — being forced to leave one's home
- **testimony** — a person's account of an experience
- **an inference** — a conclusion drawn from evidence

Use the second stem only if the final prompt still asks for a hypothesis or further evidence; do not create that requirement through the scaffold.

For the proposed **two-sentence outline**, omit the second stem. The first already supports the constitutional/human distinction without giving its content.

### Stop 5: distinction and belonging (`migration-and-belonging`)

Stems:

- “The source distinguishes … from … by showing …”
- “This example suggests …, but does not represent …”

Terms:

- **legal status** — a person's position under the law
- **belonging** — being or feeling part of a community
- **regardless of** — without being affected by

“Regardless of” transfers B26. Do not supply a complete national-status/social-treatment contrast in the help text.

### Stop 6: acknowledgement and source limits (`remembering-empire`)

Stems:

- “By using ‘…’, the speaker presents … as …”
- “This supports the claim that …, but cannot tell us …”

Terms:

- **to acknowledge** — to recognise or accept that something is true
- **liability** — legal responsibility
- **to grapple with** — to work hard to understand or deal with something

“Grapple with” transfers B27. Use the first stem only with a displayed direct quotation; a summary's word choice belongs to the summary's author.

### Final argument (`whose-britain`)

Stems:

- “To a … extent, I agree because …”
- “Although …, the evidence from … suggests …”
- “This source helps establish …; its limitation is …”

Terms:

- **a judgement** — a conclusion reached after considering evidence
- **a distorted view of** — an account that gives a misleading impression
- **a legacy** — an effect of the past that continues

“Distorted view of” transfers A3. It is an optional evaluative expression, not the preferred conclusion. The final is the only block with three stems. Keep its help under about 90 words overall. Do not require use of a specific phrase, term, viewpoint or paragraph template for marks.

## Optional transfer to the Britishness reading

Teacher-facing follow-up after the timed activity, using the students' existing copy: **Choose one claim about Britishness in the class extract. Use one rallye source to support, qualify or challenge it. Explain one word or image in the extract that shapes the claim.** This is a later bridge, not an eighth task inside 45 minutes.

The vocabulary sheet's “whitewash,” “self-righteous” and “in decline” are useful for discussing how authors present Britain, but are poor compulsory keywords: they can predetermine tone or judgement. Do not treat satirical stereotypes as factual descriptions. Students should attribute an attitude to the writer and distinguish it from the nation's opinions.

## UI, differentiation and marking contract

- Put the task, operator, expected product and time above or alongside the reading. Keep the exact question visible when students reach the response field. Current lengthy source cards precede the response, making it easy to lose the purpose.
- Render optional help as native `<details>` below the short response instructions and before the textarea. Suggested classes agreed with UX: `.ry-language-support`, `.ry-language-stems`, `.ry-language-terms`. Do not prefill or append stems to answers. Keyboard opening must work; no animation or completion badge is needed.
- Agreed and engine-confirmed data shape: `investigation.support = { stems: string[], vocabulary: [{ term: string, meaning: string }] }`, alongside `investigation.operator` and `investigation.responsePurpose`. Engine also tolerates plain vocabulary strings. The object form preserves a clear term/gloss distinction. Keep the shared intro in the renderer; do not create competing task models.
- Weakest needed support is a selective glossary and two phrase frames. A learner can use one frame and then write independently. No separate “easy” track that substitutes factual recall for analysis is required.
- Optional challenge: **Test your interpretation against a second detail. Revise one sentence if it is too broad.** This deepens precision without adding a second essay or compulsory source search.
- During checking, invite a single purposeful revision: **Check one sentence: have you identified whose claim it is, given evidence and explained what it shows?** Keep the final two-minute check inside the existing 12 minutes.
- Teacher feedback should separate historical/source reasoning from language clarity. A useful small feedback record is one effective phrase and one concrete revision target, such as distinguishing past event tense from present source-analysis tense, or correcting a phrase's preposition. Do not invent exam-style language weighting without the assessment owner's evidence and decision.
- Source citations need attribution in prose (“The proclamation promises …”; “Williams's review records …”), not raw URLs inside every sentence. The notebook stores the complete source reference. A cited link alone is not explanation; perfect citation format should not consume the lesson.
- Preserve learner drafts when changing prompts. The UI must not silently treat an earlier answer as an answer to a newly changed question. This is an engine/content-revision concern, separate from language support.

- Every implemented support block contains **one or two stems and three terms**. All are optional. They leave historical actors, evidence, interpretations and the judgement to the learner. No vocabulary quota, automatic English mark, extra planning field or completed answer is inserted.

- A formal evaluation of grammatical sophistication is neither added nor automated. The local rubric rewards intelligible causal, contrastive and argumentative communication. This suits a practice enquiry; it does not claim an official exam language weighting.
