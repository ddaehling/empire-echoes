## Recommended prompt changes

Coordination note: the task author subsequently proposed analysis of original Victoria wording at stop 3 and official acknowledgement/liability wording at stop 6. Those tasks can close the English-analysis gap without a further compulsory satire analysis. Under that route, retain Fletcher/Hawkins as an **optional transfer from already-studied class copies**, not a dependency of the timed rallye. Suggested introductory bridge: “If you have studied Adam Fletcher or Paul Hawkins, use this evidence trail to test how their humorous representations select, exaggerate or question ideas of Britishness.” Suggested optional final sentence: “You may connect a case to Fletcher's criticism of an idealised national story or to Hawkins's closing image of ‘an empire that died’; explain what the historical evidence adds or complicates.” This fits the task author's shorter school-magazine comment with two cases, a concession and a source limit. The more substantial prompt variants below are alternatives, not cumulative requirements.

### Preferred replacement within “When does an empire end?”

Class-copy locator: Fletcher, the paragraph about school, media and history books, particularly “whitewashed empire”. Evidence: the existing 2013 Kenya statement. Keep the 5-minute stop and 45–65-word guide; remove the additional Hong Kong comparison from this response if this option is selected.

> Analyse how Fletcher's phrase “whitewashed empire” challenges an idealised image of Britain. Explain its likely effect on the reader, then use one detail from the 2013 Kenya statement to develop or qualify his criticism. Explain why neither text tells us what every person in Britain thinks.

This uses one small language choice, one historical detail and one warranted limit. Successful responses explain that the expression presents memory as selective concealment or beautification, rather than merely translating the word. They can connect the official acknowledgement of colonial mistreatment to the limits of celebratory narratives. They must distinguish Fletcher's representation from survey evidence, and the official statement's scope from all British opinion. An answer may qualify Fletcher's generalisation; agreement is not a marking condition.

Suggested five-mark emphasis: (1) meaningful explanation of the wording; (1) plausible reader effect connected to the critique; (1) accurate historical detail; (1) explanation linking the two; (1) appropriate limit. A student who only says “it is a metaphor and makes the reader think” has not explained the effect.

### Alternative: Hawkins and the evidence against a sealed island

Class-copy locator: Hawkins, section 1, the extended image of the island as a protected cultural garden. Evidence: an already completed trade or migration stop. This can replace one identity-transfer subquestion at the relevant stop, not be added to every station.

> Analyse how Hawkins's garden image presents Britain's relationship with the wider world. Use one precise example from the trade or Windrush evidence to test this comic picture. Explain how the contrast changes your reading of the satire.

Expected distinction: the metaphor constructs privacy or separation; trade, empire and migration supply evidence of connections. Students should not conclude that historical connections make the joke meaningless or that geography by itself explains national personality. This is a comparison between a rhetorical representation and historical evidence, not a scientific test of humour.

### Alternative: agency and grammatical framing

The existing Jamaica prompt already offers a strong English-language task. Make its focus visible without adding a second text:

> Analyse how the verb “gave” presents Britain and enslaved people in this museum sentence: “Britain gave enslaved people their freedom.” Rewrite the sentence using evidence of resistance and abolition, then explain how your wording changes the reader's view of who acted.

Use this as a model of language → representation → effect. A later teacher-led link can connect selective agency to Fletcher's criticism of idealised history. This option works if the supplementary class copies are unavailable during the activity or students have not read Fletcher/Hawkins. It does **not** count as analysis of those authors.

### Final assessment bridge

The current final asks for 140–200 words, three stops, two past/present links, a counterargument and a source limit. Do not append a new full satire analysis to all of those demands. Keep the historical evidence and judgement, and integrate the classroom transfer into an existing explanatory sentence.

Alternative final wording, if the supplementary class readings are familiar:

> How far can knowledge of empire challenge an idealised picture of Britishness? Write a reasoned comment using evidence from at least three rallye stops. Explain two links to belonging or public memory today, and connect one example to Fletcher's critique of national mythology or Hawkins's comic picture of island life. Include a qualification and explain one limit of your evidence.

Retain the existing practical instructions on a 140–200-word target, at least two named historical sources, and plural identities. The classroom text is an additional representation to interpret; it should not replace both historical sources. Let students choose either author, and do not require personal disclosure of nationality, migration experience or family history. Analysis of a word/image is assessed at the selected station; the final assesses transfer and judgement. Avoid a compulsory conclusion that empire is the single or greatest cause of contemporary identity.

If preserving the current final claim about present map size is important, replace only its second explanatory instruction with: “Connect one historical example to Fletcher's critique of national mythology or Hawkins's comic picture of island life.” The teacher key must accept claims that qualify or challenge those representations.

## Misconceptions to anticipate in teacher support

- **Satire is representation, not a population description.** Fletcher's and Hawkins's broad national characterisations invite analysis of voice, exaggeration and criticism. Neither establishes that all British people are polite, reserved, nostalgic or isolated; neither licenses students to present the texts' stereotypes of other nationalities as facts.
- **Reader effect is argued, not universal.** “May invite readers to question …” is stronger than asserting that the language makes every reader feel the same thing. Students should identify the phrasing and explain the connection.
- **The pronoun “we” constructs a collective voice.** It can invite identification or spread a generalisation. It does not establish consent from every nation, class, racialised group or individual represented by the label.
- **Island geography is not a complete historical explanation.** Hawkins's protected-island metaphor is a comic proposition. Empire, trade and migration provide counterpressure to a story of cultural isolation. Do not turn the joke into geographical determinism.
- **Comic brevity is not historical accuracy.** Hawkins's remarks about imported food, monarchy and “an empire that died” are rhetorical material. Do not use them as standalone proof of origins, constitutional history or a single ending of empire.
- **Geographical labels, citizenship and self-identification differ.** Hawkins deliberately piles up labels; his aside about Northern Ireland cannot settle any individual's Irish or British identity. Use the existing plural-identity framing and verified factual cards, not the joke, to teach the distinctions. The date and perspective of the 2019 text matter.
- **“Successful empire” needs criteria and a standpoint.** Fletcher's superlative can be analysed as part of a national myth. Territorial scale alone does not define moral success, prosperity for whom, consent or lived experience. Do not reproduce the class-copy speaking thesis as an unqualified fact.
- **Neither satire nor an official acknowledgement measures current public opinion.** An official statement establishes a dated institutional position within its scope. A classroom satire establishes one published representation. Claims about prevalence of present attitudes need other evidence.
