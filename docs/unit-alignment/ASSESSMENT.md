# Assessment and English-task alignment audit

## Status and scope

This is an assessment review of the original six-stop rallye in `app/journey/js/rallye-content.js`, its teacher criteria and `rallye.js` student workflow, before the coordinated content revision, updated after the primary source set was resolved. No application files were edited for this review. The three primary planning sources are `Q2_Englisch_UK_5_Verlaufsplanungen_AUSFUEHRLICH_2026_27.pdf` (213 pages), `Lehrerhandbuch_Q2_Englisch_UK_2026_27.pdf` (129 pages), and `UK_Q2_Lehrwerksrecherche_SH_2026_27.pdf` (22 pages). The shorter plans and three classroom DOCX files are supplementary materials, not substitutes for the primary planning documents. All PDF page numbers below are physical PDF pages, matching the numbered extracts in `/tmp/empire-unit-alignment/`.

**Recommendation:** retain six stops and a 45-minute total, but make the stops a short evidence bank with a visible comprehension → language/source analysis → evaluation progression. End with one purposeful comment for a named audience answering how imperial history helps explain contemporary British identities. Historical mechanisms remain the substance of the enquiry; English skills make those explanations accurate, bounded and communicative. Treat the existing 40-point system as local formative feedback, not an Abitur mark scheme.

## Baseline strengths and gaps

The existing route is unusually careful about unequal forms of rule, agency, legal status, maps' limits and plural identities. It invites supported disagreement, gives source provenance, separates summaries from originals and correctly reserves interpretation for a teacher. These strengths should remain.

The assessment design nevertheless pulls toward a history worksheet written in English. Every stop requests roughly 45–65 words of explanation or judgement, frequently plus a modern-identity connection. Stop 1 already asks for evaluation of a modern national description. Students never get a small, successful outline before making that jump. The final revisits the same mode at greater length rather than making a distinct communicative product.

The largest English gap is **language doing something**: no required response consistently connects a precise authentic phrase to a textual choice, intended audience/purpose and possible reader effect. Identifying an official statement's institutional origin is valuable source criticism, but it does not substitute for language analysis. A source summary can support historical comprehension; its wording cannot be attributed to the original speaker. Similarly, analysing the invented museum panel analyses a teacher-made stimulus, not an authentic historical text. Label it as such.

The baseline final has an argumentative question but no audience, publication context, purpose or text type. The claim about imperial history mattering “more” than map size is evocative but needs its comparison explained: territorial size cannot measure the continuing relationships shaped by empire. Preserve that historical-causal enquiry. A school-magazine audience can make it communicative without replacing it with the different question of whether empire ought to be taught. Require two developed historical-to-contemporary connections, then qualify the strength and scope of the explanation.

All 40 baseline marks reward historical knowledge, causal reasoning, source use or qualification. Those are worthwhile, but they omit clear communication, source-language analysis, audience/register and English coherence. Do not quietly declare this an Abitur English rubric. Add these dimensions as formative criteria, while distinguishing language difficulty from mistaken historical reasoning.

The baseline recommended output is 410–590 words (six times 45–65 plus 140–200), before optional notebook notes. Students must also read contexts and multiple source cards, manipulate dates, inspect territory pages, open three original sources, save citations, plan and hand in. This may be achievable by fast, prepared writers; it is an unsafe general 45-minute gN/B2 assumption. Higher hard ceilings are not additional time. Open-ended live research or a first reading of the full Fletcher/Hawkins excerpts cannot also fit.

Finally, the engine's word-count and citation-count gates measure formal completeness, not learning. A concise valid answer can be blocked while padded prose passes. A source saved in a notebook need not have been used. Keep substantive source use in the teacher criteria, make word targets guidance, and allow concise notes/short sentences in early stages. Do not add more required fields merely to make analysis visible.

## A coherent 45-minute route

The following is an implementable recommendation, not a competing edit to the task author's content. Preserve station IDs to protect draft migration. All times include reading, map changes and short navigation; stop 1 includes the brief orientation. Use a small prepared source selection. Original websites are enrichment outside the timed core; an offline student must be able to analyse authentic wording in a clearly attributed short quotation or use a teacher-issued class copy. If the application cannot provide the authentic wording, make that exact analysis task optional and supply an equivalent accessible source.

Final timing: two minutes select and order evidence; nine minutes write; three minutes revise one weak evidence link and one language/clarity issue; one minute export/hand in. This is 45 minutes in total, including delivery. It is about 265–400 words overall rather than seven uniform essays. A classroom without quick individual typing may use the same note products on paper; slower writers can use shorter sentences with the same intellectual requirements.

A teacher may retain the original final quotation as the magazine's stimulus. Define its comparison as a claim about the limits of territorial size and require two developed cases rather than three compressed ones. Judge the explanatory force, evidence and scope of each connection: an official response can demonstrate an institutional legacy without establishing the views of everyone in Britain. Do not replace this enquiry with a generic curriculum debate. Such a debate is a possible later transfer task in the sources, not the purpose of this route.

## Connecting classroom readings without overloading the route

After prior reading of Fletcher, stop 6 can instead test how a loaded phrase such as “whitewashed empire” frames history, using a rallye example to support or qualify the implication. Students must separate Fletcher's satire and generalising voice from their historical evidence. Use the existing class copy and a paragraph locator; do not ship the whole copyrighted reading. The baseline teacher must know whether the reading has happened, so this is an explicit alternative rather than a hidden prerequisite.

Hawkins belongs in a later transfer: compare a comic islandness claim with one demonstrable imperial connection and explain why a humorous generalisation is limited evidence. Do not add a fresh full reading plus comparison within the 45-minute route. The vocabulary sheet can supply optional collocations; reward apt use, not a mandatory count of ambitious words.

## Formative criteria and feedback

If retaining 40 points is necessary for the existing interface, label them **local practice criteria; teacher feedback required**. A workable five-point allocation per stop is:

- Stop 1: accurate historical change 2; revenue/power connection 2; concise own wording 1.
- Stop 2: historical correction and agency 3; specific evidence 1; audience clarity 1.
- Stop 3: precise wording 1; analytical feature/effect/purpose link 2; relevant context 1; scope limit 1.
- Stop 4: political change 1; human consequence and evidence 2; clear distinction 1; precise concise English 1.
- Stop 5: legal/social distinction 2; specific attributed evidence 1; contextual connection 1; coherent contrast 1.
- Stop 6: precise wording 1; language and communicative-purpose analysis 2; historical use 1; source boundary 1.

For the final ten points: reasoned judgement with two developed historical-to-contemporary mechanisms 3; accurate purposeful evidence 2; source qualification 1; audience/text-type effectiveness 2; coherence and intelligible precise English 2. The three reasoning points can be one for a justified overall position and one per developed mechanism. Use explanatory force, evidence and scope as explicit criteria. This is a proposed classroom rubric, not an official weighting or a conversion to German/Abitur grades. A judgement that empire explains only some identities or matters less than another influence can earn full marks if it engages with the historical connections; false historical statements do not become acceptable because the judgement is permitted to differ.

Show no more than two task-specific success checks at the point of writing, with the full rubric available on demand. Give feedback as observation → consequence → next step. For example: “You name the promise, but your paragraph does not explain its audience. Add who is meant to be reassured and how the phrase helps.” That is more usable than “more analysis”.

A minimum formative loop within the final three-minute revision is: underline one evidence-to-claim link; check whether its wording overstates the source; revise one sentence for scope or clarity. The teacher's follow-up selects one strong/revisable paragraph and asks students to reperform one small skill. Do not publish model answers as a substitute for this feedback, and do not give a grade merely because all boxes are complete.

## Acceptance checks for the coordinated revision

1. A student can identify which actions are outline, analyse and comment, and can see why they differ.
2. At least two required notes analyse exact authentic wording; source cards distinguish quotation, editorial summary and invented stimulus.
3. The final has a genuine purpose and named audience, with two developed historical-to-contemporary mechanisms within a feasible short product; it does not drift into a generic debate about studying empire.
4. Any classroom-text connection states the prerequisite/class-copy route and has a workable core alternative.
5. Reading, navigation, planning, revision and export fit inside the 45-minute budget; the teacher does not silently add a full preliminary lesson to make a fresh task appear feasible.
6. Student drafts remain recoverable when prompts change; old wording is not silently reinterpreted under a new task.
7. A student can hand in a concise meaningful response; neither word count nor the presence of a source URL is described as a quality grade.
8. Teacher materials explicitly identify the activity as formative English practice and distinguish it from a secure unseen-text assessment.

### Actionable findings and verified resolution

1. **Partition temporal fit:** the prompt asks for a 1947 change and a human consequence, while the model's named detail is Iqbal's aunt's displacement _before partition_. The source card accurately labels that timing, so the problem is the task/model relationship. Either provide a verified detail about a 1947 consequence, or explicitly ask for the political change alongside a wider human experience surrounding partition; the rubric/model must not reward a misleading causal inference.
2. **Abolition model chronology:** the museum-label model says Parliament abolished slavery in 1834. The surrounding context and source correctly distinguish the 1833 Act and 1834 commencement. Model the distinction with “abolition took effect in 1834” or “the 1833 Act took effect in 1834”.
3. **Liability model precision:** the authentic statement denies legal liability for the claims. The stage context's “legal limit on liability” and model's “limits the legal responsibility it accepts” can imply that some legal liability was accepted. Use the precise distinction: recognition/regret and a settlement alongside denial of legal liability. This follows the statement's own wording, not a separate legal conclusion.

All three corrections were independently re-read in the actual content file after the task author applied them. The partition prompt now asks for a human experience surrounding partition and its rubric explicitly distinguishes earlier events from consequences of 1947. The abolition model now says abolition took effect in 1834. The Kenya context/model now recognises suffering while denying legal liability for the claims. The shorter proclamation context supplies chronology without pre-answering the wording analysis. These changes preserve the time budget, task types and marks. **Final content assessment: accepted; no unresolved actionable prompt/model/rubric/source-dependency alignment issue found.** Classroom timing remains an implementation estimate rather than an observed learner trial.
