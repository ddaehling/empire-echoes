# Independent review 2: does the evidence warrant claims about identities today?

Reviewed 1 October 2026. Reviewer remit: inferential validity, temporal reach and source scope. This review read the current `rallye-content.js`, `rallye-resources.js` and teacher content independently, without assuming the desired outcome. It is an expert-style agent review, not a trial with real pupils.

## Initial verdict: not adequately prepared for the choice offered

The final assignment offers any two of six cases, but only the Windrush and Kenya cases contain an explicit, documented connection between imperial events and postcolonial institutional action or belonging. The other four tasks practise valuable historical interpretation but do not give pupils the evidence necessary to explain contemporary British identities. A strong student choosing Company rule and Victoria could responsibly explain why the provided sources cannot answer the question, but could not supply the two positive explanatory cases the final prompt expects. This is a curriculum alignment problem rather than a need for more difficult wording.

The final prompt's three routes—belonging, public memory, ideas about Britain—are useful. They permit bounded arguments about specific people and institutions rather than demanding a survey of national psychology. The introduction already distinguishes plural identities and warns against universal claims. These are strengths, but a definition and a warning cannot replace missing contemporary evidence.

A publication date alone does not establish a claim about identity.

## Hidden dependencies in the teacher material

The teacher guide supplies an explanatory framework of legal membership, felt belonging and official national stories; tells teachers to require a mechanism; discusses UCL research tracing slavery into British institutions; and includes a teacher-only final model paragraph. Students do not receive equivalent evidence for the first four cases. The teacher model's Jamaica passage says accurate remembrance *can* change recognition, which is a defensible pedagogical possibility. It does not document that a named contemporary public account actually does so. That distinction matters if pupils are expected to support claims about identities today.

The optional final ONS reading usefully establishes plural national labels, England-and-Wales scope and measurement caveats. It explicitly did not ask whether empire caused those labels, so it cannot fill any historical-to-contemporary gap. The Commonwealth Charter supplies institutional values in 2013, not popular identities or proof of compliance. Neither optional reading makes every pair of historical cases viable.

## Concrete remedies

1. Preserve the final question's intellectual ambition. Supply a short, essential, readable contemporary bridge for every case that remains freely selectable. Each bridge needs a named person/institution, an event or publication date, an attributable present-day claim/action, and one precise limit. Keep original external links optional and render the needed evidence in the assignment.
2. For Company rule, add a documented British museum/heritage decision about Clive or Company wealth and ask how the evidence challenges or complicates the institution's public story. Do not jump directly from Bengal revenue to “British prosperity” or all Britons' identities.
3. For Jamaica, replace or pair the hypothetical label with a real dated British institution's account or commemorative choice about enslavement, resistance and abolition. Pupils can then justify an interpretation of actual public memory rather than unknowingly using a made-up label as data.
4. For the proclamation, provide a genuinely documented present institutional treatment of imperial authority or royal history. Alternatively make this stop preparatory background for a later belonging case and explicitly say it is not a standalone final case. Merely placing a recent equality statement beside Victoria's words demonstrates comparison, not causal continuity.
5. For partition, include a dated British interview, oral-history exhibition or family-memory project that establishes who remembers, where in Britain, and how family memory is publicly narrated. A diaspora connection must be evidenced, not inferred from an Asian name.
6. For Windrush and Kenya, revise the analytical task's endpoint to produce a reusable bounded identity argument. Ask what historical connection explains this named person's/institution's recent response, and what cannot be inferred from it. Preserve the existing close reading and qualified judgements.
7. Before the final response, offer a compact planning aid: historical event → documented later connection → specific aspect of belonging/public memory → limit. It should require pupils to construct the relationship, not hand them six ready-made conclusions. Distinguish “this helps explain why this institution acts this way” from “this proves all British people believe this”.
8. Clarify time. “Today” can be addressed through recent evidence, but students must date it. Do not label evidence from 2013 as an undated current government position. An updated source can strengthen a case without requiring a constantly updated news feed.
9. Make “how far” demand a judgement about the reach of the explanation, not an arbitrary extra sentence saying class/religion also matter. A source limitation is a legitimate alternative, already permitted: explain which claim it restricts and whether that weakens the overall conclusion.

If six bridges overburden the route, an honest alternative is to identify two or three final-ready cases and make the other stops foundational historical study. Since the user's focus is student preparation and meaningful choice, concise source bridges are preferable to several new full assignments.

## Acceptance conditions for re-review

- Every case freely offered in task 7 has enough essential on-page evidence for a defensible historical-to-contemporary argument; any unsupported standalone case is explicitly excluded or paired.
- At least two substantially different contemporary mechanisms can be chosen (for example migration/status and public commemoration), rather than repeating one general claim.
- Each bridge provides attributable actors, dates and a source-supported connection. Modern publication, historical likeness and geographic origin do not masquerade as causation.
- No fictional teaching text is presented as an authentic museum's public position.
- Core tasks make students practise the inferential move required in the final, while leaving the conclusion open.
- Pupils can distinguish state position, museum interpretation, personal belonging and population opinion. Source scope makes generalisation limits usable.
- No essential answer depends on teacher-only material, blocked links, giant PDFs or unexplained supplementary knowledge.
- The final “how far” judgement can be earned through the available evidence and a meaningful limitation, without requiring an unsupported claim about another cause.

### Task 7 and remaining boundaries

The final instructions now require historical and contemporary evidence for each case, a comparison and an explanation of how a limitation changes the overall judgement. This is materially stronger than appending an unrelated caveat. The introduction also identifies contemporary evidence as dated examples rather than an assertion about everyone in 2026. On-page source cards keep attribution, date, paraphrase labels and scope visible; the comparison aid retrieves the essential evidence with prior responses.

The final still cannot support an estimate of what percentage of British identity is caused by empire, or an explanation of the entire population's beliefs. It does not need to: its explicit options allow bounded reasoning about belonging, public memory and ideas of Britain. The scope limits are now part of the reasoning students practise rather than an obstacle they must discover alone.

One optional enrichment, not a condition of acceptance: add a short paraphrase of Javed's explicit British Punjabi self-description to the existing card. This would broaden its use from the already sufficient public-memory route to an especially direct account of plural identity. No new source or additional assignment is necessary.
