# Reviewer 4: explanatory judgement and genuine choice

Reviewed 1 October 2026. Independent review of the existing student route in `app/journey/js/rallye-content.js`, `rallye-resources.js` and its rendering in `rallye.js`. This is an expert simulation, not observed student research. No application files changed by this reviewer.

## Before verdict: revision needed

The route teaches useful historical interpretation, but it does not yet reliably prepare a student to judge **how far** imperial history explains **British identities today**. The task is not ideologically closed: it expressly permits another influence or an evidential limitation, and many source cards caution against generalisation. The problem is preparation and the distribution of evidence. The easiest successful-looking answer is two retrospective examples plus an unsupported sentence that family or religion matters too. A careful student choosing the first and fourth stops cannot establish the required modern British connection from the supplied evidence.

The distinction matters: criticising empire is not itself evidence about how present-day people identify, and identifying a present-day connection does not establish its relative explanatory importance. Students need to practise that distinction before their final comment.

## The final task and its optional census evidence

The final prompt (`rallye-content.js:379`) names two cases and requires students to explain each modern connection. That is a valuable demand, but it asks for a skill and evidence connection not explicitly developed across most of the route. “Another influence ... or something your sources cannot tell you” allows a generic limitation to stand in for an actual judgement of explanatory reach. The prompt should ask the student to explain how the chosen limitation or rival influence changes their conclusion, not merely mention it.

The ONS census card is sound about its boundaries: identity labels differ from citizenship and ethnicity; multiple labels are possible; England and Wales are not the entire UK; the data do not ask whether empire caused those choices. It is **not evidence that national or regional identity is independent of imperial history**, nor evidence that empire is unimportant. Used properly, it helps students avoid treating “British identities” as one measurable imperial attitude and limits the reach of claims based on two cases.

At present this card is marked `optional: true`, and the final stage has `essentialSourceIds: []` (`:410`). `rallye.js` consequently renders no core evidence section there. The census appears inside a closed “Explore sources further (optional)” disclosure **after the writing area** (`rallye.js:297`, `:333`). Students following the default route need never see the one prepared source most directly about present-day self-identification. The optional Commonwealth Charter supplies institutional aspirations, not an alternative measure of people's identities.

## Smallest credible revision

1. Make a concise, directly readable present-day identity source part of the final core evidence. The existing ONS card is enough for an explicitly bounded evidence-limit route; do not pretend it establishes a rival causal explanation. If a rival-influence route is required instead of optional, supply evidence for it.
2. Give every case that the final prompt invites students to choose a defensible modern connection, or clearly explain which earlier stops provide background for the later cases. Do not tell students that all six are equally sufficient if they are not. A short modern public-memory artifact can bridge an early case; a historical case may also be explicitly paired with a later one. New standalone stops are not intrinsically necessary.
3. In the earlier task wording, make students test a connection rather than merely announce a legacy. For example, require a bounded claim about what a dated public act or a person's testimony can establish, and identify where a stronger identity claim would need different evidence. Integrate that reasoning into the existing argument, without adding a repeated worksheet checklist to every stop.
4. Exploit the best existing cases. Windrush can distinguish colonial relationships from later policy choices and individual attachment. Kenya can examine government self-presentation while reserving judgement about national consensus. Jamaica can ask how the allocation of historical agency affects the image of Britain a visitor receives, with the label's classroom status clear through its framing.
5. In task 7, make the final judgement respond to the strength and reach of the selected cases. Ask what imperial history explains well and where that explanation becomes less secure, and require the limitation to affect the conclusion. Avoid supplying a template argument or preferred position.

## Acceptance conditions for re-review

- A student can construct a historical event → documented modern connection → bounded identity/memory claim from the prepared route without independent research or invented continuity.
- The final choice offers more than one viable pair, with clear support for the cases advertised as selectable.
- Students have practised judging explanatory reach, not only recalling history or condemning colonial harm.
- The standard visible route includes evidence that complicates a general claim about British identities. If the census is used, no causal inference from identity labels is asserted.
- At least two materially different, evidence-supported final positions remain possible. Both stronger and more limited claims about imperial influence can be defended; no “correct” degree of influence or compulsory personal identification is supplied.
- A sentence such as “family also matters” or “not everyone agrees” is insufficient unless the writer explains its consequence for the argument.
- The identity of each speaker, date, place, and evidential limit stay clear. Evidence of public policy, government rhetoric, individual identity and population identity is not conflated.
- Challenge increases through evaluation and transfer, not additional trivia, word counts, prescribed conclusions, or inaccessible sources.

### Evidence for the decision

- **All six choices now have a usable bridge.** Company rule is connected to the dated Clive statue decision; Jamaica to the 2024 M Shed display; Crown rule to the 1949 constitutional change and a 2024 royal statement; partition to Javed’s 2022 London display; Windrush to the 2020/2022 review and 2022 monument; Kenya to official recognition in 2013 and 2023. These are selected public acts or accounts, not claims about a whole population. Jamaica explicitly separates Colston’s earlier history from the later Jamaican rebellion, and partition distinguishes the three families and dates.
- **“How far” is rehearsed.** Stop 1 asks how far imperial history explains a particular dispute and limits its geographical reach. Stop 3 weighs an institutional image against what it can establish about identities. Stop 4 asks how widely an interpretation of family memory applies. Stop 6 tests public recognition and its limits. These differ enough to develop transfer without turning every response into the same checklist.
- **The Windrush task now offers actual competing explanatory material.** The prepared review card explains later documentary requirements, missing documents and absent Home Office records. Students can distinguish the imperial relationship that helps explain the migration and legal connection from more immediate policy decisions that enabled wrongful exclusion. This is a concrete explanatory mechanism, rather than an empty instruction to mention “other factors”. The monument adds agency and public recognition without implying that recognition resolved injustice.
- **The final comparison requires the limitation to do argumentative work.** It asks students to compare what imperial history explains across two cases and explain how a relevant limit or evidenced influence changes the overall judgement. The preparation text differentiates historical fact from a connection, and the optional discussion question usefully separates “empire explains less” from “these sources cannot establish more”. A generic sentence about family, religion or disagreement would not fulfil the revised task.
- **ONS is visible and accurately bounded in the learning design.** `final-identities` is now a core source, displayed before the comparison and writing area. The prepared text distinguishes overlapping identity labels from the causes of those labels, bounds the population to England and Wales in 2021, and explicitly refuses a measurement of imperial causation. It cannot legitimately be used as proof that other identities displace imperial influence. It can make a national generalisation less defensible.
- **Choice remains substantive.** A student may judge imperial history highly explanatory for selected public debates across distinct institutions, while still limiting population-wide claims. Another may place more weight on later policy decisions, curatorial agency or constitutional change, and defend a narrower explanation. The material allows both; neither a pro-imperial nor an anti-imperial political thesis is prescribed. The difference concerns explanatory reach, not denial of the historical evidence.
- **The default route supports the advertised choice.** Every modern card is in its stop’s `essentialSourceIds`, and the renderer displays historical and contemporary evidence separately without requiring an external visit. The final “Build your comparison” section visibly offers all six named cases and brings each student's response, notes and both evidence groups together. Students must choose and explain connections; the interface does not supply a completed argument.

All eight acceptance conditions above are met in the reviewed student content. No further application change is required from this review. The total is now a 60-minute planning estimate (40 minutes across the six stops and 20 for the final comment), not a demonstrated completion time; actual classroom pacing remains to be observed.
