## Before verdict

**Not yet adequately prepared for the full choice offered by task 7.** A learner who selects Windrush and the Kenya settlement can produce a defensible, qualified argument. A learner who selects the East India Company and partition cannot substantiate the demanded links to British identities today using only the provided cases. The sequence does practise evidence use, wording analysis and judgement well. It does not systematically practise the crucial move from a historical case through a documented continuing connection to an aspect of present identity, nor weighing how much that connection explains. Difficulty is partly an evidence gap rather than productive intellectual challenge.

Task 7 says to choose any two cases and discuss either another influence or a source limitation. The evidence makes the choices highly unequal. A fair challenging task should let students work out connections from sufficient material, not guess facts that happen to be absent.

## A limited outline using the strongest pair

**Cases: 5 Windrush and 6 Kenya.** This is an outline achievable from the materials, not a model essay.

- Tentative answer: empire remains useful for understanding some disputes about belonging and public recognition, but these cases do not explain everyone's identity today.
- Windrush historical connection: some Caribbean people were British subjects while their countries were colonies. Migration connected their lives with Britain. The 2020 Williams review documents wrongful treatment of lawful residents and reports one person's reference to “my beloved England”.
- Interpretation: that wording suggests attachment and a sense of betrayal. Imperial connections help explain why exclusion by British institutions could conflict with a person's felt relationship to England.
- Limit: the anonymous speaker is not Ena Sullivan. The form records Sullivan's work, not her feelings. Neither establishes what all Caribbean migrants felt; work is not a condition of belonging. A 2020 report also does not establish whether the position remains the same in 2026.
- Kenya historical connection: colonial detention and mistreatment preceded independence in 1963, but claims led to a British government statement and settlement in 2013.
- Interpretation: a British government still had to account publicly for that past. Regret, settlement and the denial of liability show a particular official way of recognising and limiting responsibility. This is evidence of an official idea about Britain's relationship to its past, not a survey of British identity.
- Limit: the memorial is in Nairobi. The source does not tell me how British residents identify today or how survivors assessed it. I cannot infer that everyone in Britain now regrets empire.
- Extent judgement: the two cases support an explanation of particular institutional conflicts and official public memory; the record does not support an explanation of all identities. I can use the final context's mention of family, region and religion as possibilities, but cannot evaluate their causal importance from supplied evidence.

**Where I get stuck:** I can show continuing consequences into 2013/2020, but I have to invent the analytical connection between those consequences and “identities today” myself. That inference is reasonable when bounded, but there is no earlier explicit practice comparing why one case explains belonging while another primarily shows state self-presentation. “How far” may become “empire is important, but not everyone is the same”, which sounds balanced while doing little explanatory weighing.

## A limited outline using a weak pair

**Cases: 1 East India Company and 4 partition.** The task permits this pair.

- Company: the 1757 intervention and 1765 taxation show political power growing from commerce. I can justify which gives stronger evidence of governing authority.
- Partition: the 1946 Singh letter and remembered pre-partition displacement show concerns and experiences a boundary map cannot capture. The timing stops me attributing every experience to the 1947 boundary decision.
- Possible connection to today: I could say these cases complicate a simple story of British progress or imperial generosity. However, I have no current British statement, museum framing, community account or other evidence showing those ideas being used or disputed today.
- Unsupported leaps I must **not** make: “colonial wealth made modern Britain rich”; “partition caused present British Asian identities”; “all British people are proud of empire”; “British Sikhs now feel excluded because Singh feared exclusion in 1946”. None is established by these materials.
- Actual conclusion available: these cases change my historical understanding, but I cannot demonstrate the requested present-day connection. Saying “there is no evidence about today” twice would be an honest limitation, not completion of two explanatory cases.

A student should not be penalised for that outcome after following “choose two cases”.

## Minimal remedies and acceptance conditions

1. Supply at least one concrete, dated, accessible modern British bridge for any historical case that remains an unrestricted final choice. It can be a short attributed museum framing, a community or descendant's account in Britain, or a public institution's engagement with that case. Its scope must identify what it shows, without implying it speaks for everyone. Embed enough evidence to work offline in the assignment; a link alone is not preparation.
2. If adding bridges to all six would overload the lesson, define a supported final route honestly: e.g. selected cases with documented modern bridges, while other historical stops supply background and comparison. Do not still advertise every pair as equally evidence-ready.
3. Build a small number of explicit transfer moves into earlier responses: historical relationship → continuing mechanism or later event → whose belonging/memory/idea is illuminated → what remains unexplained. Students should construct these links, not copy a completed answer. Stops 2, 4, 5 and 6 are natural places.
4. Explain “How far” briefly in the final's main flow: judge what imperial history helps explain and where its explanatory reach stops. Distinguish “my source is limited” from “another cause mattered”: weak evidence alone does not prove a small historical influence. Ask students to weigh their two cases, not append a ritual “not everyone”.
5. Keep claim boundaries clear: an official 2013 statement is evidence of that government's position; 2020 testimony records that speaker; neither establishes all British identities in 2026. Present dates as dates rather than calling every recent source “today”.
6. On revision, I should be able to outline a strong final argument with two different supported pairs from the materials, each with a case-specific present connection, a defensible extent judgement and a real limitation. I should also be able to identify the earlier prompt that practised each move. No extra facts from the teacher guide should be needed.

No need for extra grading, word counts, mandatory citation administration or many more pages. The strongest repair is better connected evidence and a few carefully designed reasoning prompts.

## Stress test: the formerly weakest pair, Company + partition

A plausible learner outline, using required material alone:

- My view: imperial history helps explain why particular histories remain visible or contested in British public places. It cannot on its own tell me how all British people identify.
- Company evidence: Clive secured tax-collecting rights in Bengal in 1765. That gives substance to the idea that the statue honours someone associated with governing power, rather than only trade. Shropshire Council's 2020–2021 record describes petitions, retention and an interpretation panel. Knowing the earlier power helps explain why the later statue becomes controversial and why more explanation was proposed.
- Company inference: this is a choice about public memory in one British town. The history matters, but it does not force one response: keeping a statue with explanation is itself a later institutional choice. The record is not a count of all residents' views.
- Partition evidence: Singh's 1946 worry or Iqbal's aunt's memory shows experiences beyond the 1947 border line. Separately, Javed's ancestors lost a village home during Partition. His commissioned bracelet and 2022 London museum display show a later decision to keep that place present in family and public memory.
- Partition inference: the past explains what the bracelet commemorates and why the lost home matters in this account. Javed's choice to commission and display it explains how a family connection becomes visible within British public heritage. I must not say that he is from the same family as the archive speakers or that the early letter proves why his ancestors moved.
- Comparison: Company remembrance concerns how an imperial figure is publicly honoured and contextualised; Javed's display gives public space to a family's remembered loss. Both connect Britain with histories outside its current borders, but through different people and decisions.
- Overall judgement: empire explains the historical subject of these memories strongly; later curators, families and councils shape how that past is presented. My cases support a claim about particular public memories, not a general claim about every British identity. ONS shows overlapping self-described labels in England and Wales but cannot establish their causes. That limits how far I can extend the conclusion rather than proving that empire has little influence everywhere.

The earlier version made this outline impossible without invented modern links. It is now feasible. Task 1 practises selecting historical evidence and separating local public memory from national opinion; task 4 practises an account's later use, chronology and representativeness. Task 7 asks me to compare their results and make a broader bounded judgement. This is meaningful transfer.

## Mixed-pair test A: Jamaica + Windrush

A feasible different outline:

- Cotton's threat, resistance, compensation to owners and apprenticeship complicate an account of freedom as a simple British gift. The 2024 M Shed display retains protest graffiti and offers voices on different sides. This gives a real contemporary example of how an account of slavery can become contested public memory.
- I must keep Colston's earlier slave trading separate from the later Jamaican rebellion. The sources support a comparison of remembrance, not a claim that Colston caused those later Jamaican events.
- British subjecthood supplies a historical relationship for Caribbean migration. The Windrush witness's attachment to “my beloved England” contrasts with documented institutional harm. Later demands for paperwork help explain how the wrong occurred; imperial history alone is not a complete account of the later policy mechanism.
- The 2022 Windrush monument shows an artist, committee and public contributors creating recognition. It is evidence of remembrance, not evidence that injustice was repaired or that all migrants feel welcome.
- Comparison and judgement: empire helps explain which relationships are disputed or recognised in both cases. Later protests, museum decisions, documentation policies and commemoration also matter to their present form. Personal attachment is more directly evidenced in the Windrush words than in museum curatorial choices. I can therefore make different, appropriately bounded claims from the two cases.

Task 2 now rehearses how historical selection shapes an idea about Britain; task 5 asks explicitly what later policy and remembrance add to an explanation. This prepares comparison instead of merely adding a disconnected final caveat.

## Mixed-pair test B: monarchy + Kenya

A feasible institutional-focus outline:

- Victoria's “Our Subjects” presents authority and duties from a ruler to people she governs. The 1949 change and Charles's 2024 description of an equal voluntary association allow me to explain a changed official relationship rather than calling the Commonwealth a renamed empire.
- Hague's 2013 regret and denial of liability can be compared with Charles's 2023 account of colonial harm, British Kenyans and equal partnership. I can argue that public acknowledgement forms part of how these institutions present Britain's relationship with others.
- The cases show the monarchy and government responding to inherited relationships, but their modern purposes and choices shape their statements. Public statements do not establish equal influence in practice, survivors' acceptance or citizens' own identities.
- Overall: these two cases are especially useful for official ideas of Britain in the world, less direct evidence of personal belonging. Their shared institutional perspective narrows the conclusion I can responsibly reach.

This is a valid alternative to a migration-centred answer and does not require importing a personal opinion survey.

## Practical learner experience and timing

The final now gives me the ONS card and six named case disclosures containing my earlier response, notes and required evidence. Source inspection confirms that the material is available directly in the final workspace, even if I wrote nothing at an earlier stop. This materially reduces memory and navigation demands: I can choose cases by content, inspect both and build my comparison without opening PDFs or relying on a teacher to restate facts.

The 60-minute plan is **plausible as a brisk planning estimate for this simulated learner**, with focused responses at the six stops and a 20-minute final draft. It has not been measured with students. The individual allocations total 60 minutes and leave little extra time for orientation, discussion, dictionary use or substantial redrafting; some B2 learners will need longer, particularly on the long proclamation. The self-paced design and editable saved work accommodate that. Wording such as “about 60 minutes” or “plan around 60 minutes” should be used consistently rather than presenting a universal completion promise. This is a pacing caveat, not a blocker to task alignment.

The revised demands are more coherent, not simply more numerous: earlier responses now become usable evidence-linked arguments for task 7. I would retain the seven-task route and the concise, optional support rather than add another assignment.
