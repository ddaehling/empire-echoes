# Task 7 research: belonging, public memory and explanatory limits

Verified 1 October 2026. Research only; no app files edited.

## Recommended additions

Add one short Windrush Monument card to task 5, and make the existing ONS card visible before task 7's writing area. The monument supplies a documented act of public memory and agency alongside institutional exclusion. The census guards against treating British identities as a single viewpoint. Neither warrants a claim about what everyone in Britain thinks.

Avoid an additional full assignment. Students can already weigh a historical connection against later institutional and commemorative choices inside the Windrush case. Asking for a wholly separate non-imperial cause would demand new evidence and risks producing an unsupported list of influences.

## 1. Windrush Monument: contemporary remembrance and agency

**Primary HTML:** [National Windrush monument unveiled at London Waterloo Station](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/national-windrush-monument-unveiled-at-london-waterloo-station).

**Attribution:** Department for Levelling Up, Housing and Communities and Windrush Commemoration Committee, press release, **22 June 2022**. Use that date for the event rather than the 2025 date of a later guidance page.

**Proposed student-facing paraphrase (85 words):**

> A national monument unveiled at London Waterloo in June 2022 presents a Caribbean family standing together on suitcases. Jamaican sculptor Basil Watson connected the work to his parents' migration and to the cultural influence of Caribbean people in Britain. The Windrush Commemoration Committee commissioned the sculpture after public consultation. The government funded it as a permanent place for reflection and recognition. This is evidence of people choosing how to remember migration publicly; the monument alone cannot show how welcome every person feels or whether injustices have been resolved.

**Scope label:** A government announcement includes the artist's account and describes the commemoration's intentions. It does not measure visitors' responses or show that official celebration removes exclusion.

**Locator:** Opening description; Basil Watson's statement; paragraphs about commissioning and public engagement. All accessible as HTML without an account.

**Corroborating primary HTML:** [Journey of the National Windrush Monument](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/journey-of-the-national-windrush-monument), Ministry of Housing, Communities and Local Government, **11 March 2025**, sections “The search for an artist” and “Basil Watson”. It describes an online questionnaire and five intergenerational focus groups of British Caribbean people in 2021. This supports the specific claim of consultation, not a claim that the whole community endorsed the monument. Watson links the work to his parents' 1952 journey. The page is a retrospective account, not a new 2025 unveiling.

**Curriculum value (interpretation):** A learner can argue that empire helps explain why this migration is part of British public memory, while decisions by artists, communities and public bodies determine how that history is represented. This makes migrants and descendants participants in Britain's culture and public remembrance. Their entitlement to belong does not depend on their jobs or economic contribution.

## 2. ONS: identities are self-described and can overlap

**Primary HTML:** [National identity, England and Wales: Census 2021](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/culturalidentity/ethnicity/bulletins/nationalidentityenglandandwales/census2021).

**Attribution:** Office for National Statistics, census data from **21 March 2021**, statistical bulletin published **29 November 2022**. Scope: usual residents of **England and Wales**, not the entire UK and not a survey of views in 2026.

**Proposed student-facing paraphrase (81 words):**

> In the 2021 census, people in England and Wales could select more than one national identity. For example, 13.6% selected English and British together. ONS describes national identity as people's own sense of where they belong or consider home, rather than something determined by citizenship or ethnic group. These answers show that identities can overlap. They do not explain why someone chose a label, whether empire influenced that choice, or what that person thinks about Britain's imperial past.

**Locator:** Section 2, “Changes in other UK identities”, and section 8, “National identity”. The existing app locator's section 10 is a useful optional methodological note: changed question order complicates 2011–2021 comparisons. Avoid making historical-change claims from these percentages.

**Curriculum use (interpretation):** Display this as a boundary for task 7's claims. It cannot by itself establish that empire is unimportant, that English/Welsh identities have no imperial connection, or that contemporary British identities are caused by a different named factor. Census labels are not causal evidence. Two chosen cases can illuminate particular experiences, disputes or public narratives without explaining everybody's self-understanding.

## 3. A supported additional influence within the existing case

**Primary accessible HTML:** [Windrush Lessons Learned Review: progress update](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/windrush-lessons-learned-review-progress-update/windrush-lessons-learned-review-progress-update-accessible), **Wendy Williams, independent reviewer, 31 March 2022**.

**Proposed student-facing paraphrase (80 words), if a more explicit policy mechanism is needed:**

> In her 2022 follow-up, Wendy Williams explains how later government decisions harmed lawful residents. Immigration controls increasingly required documentary proof of status to obtain work, housing and services. Some members of the Windrush generation had a right to remain but lacked documents, while the Home Office also lacked records. Some then faced enforcement action. This gives students a specific sequence of policy decisions and administrative failures to investigate alongside the earlier imperial relationship; it does not make those later decisions inevitable.

**Locator:** Introduction, “Background”, especially the paragraphs on documentation and access to services; Part 3, section 3.1, summarises the earlier review's findings on policy-making and historical knowledge.

**Scope:** Williams explains documented governmental failures. This passage does not establish present outcomes for all affected people in 2026. Date a new card 2022; do not silently attribute this text to the existing 2020 source. The existing 2020 quotation remains that of an unnamed affected person, not Ena Sullivan.

**Curriculum use (interpretation):** This is a nearer cause of exclusion, connected to but not mechanically determined by imperial history. It gives “how far” analytical substance: explain the historical relationship, assess the later mechanism, and acknowledge whose experience the evidence can represent. It should not be labelled wholly unrelated to empire.

## Suggested task adaptation

Preserve close reading, then add a bounded argument rather than a basic retrieval question:

> What tension between belonging and recognition emerges when you compare “my beloved England” with the Windrush Monument? Explain how the imperial relationship helps you understand that tension, and assess what later decisions or actions add to the explanation.

The existing Sullivan form can remain accessible as an individual historical record. It should not be made evidence of her private feelings or used to imply that employment earns belonging. Task 7 should ask students to identify whose identity, memory or idea about Britain their evidence actually supports and to distinguish a connection from a complete explanation.

No modern-source quotations are proposed; the existing short witness quotation is retained only in the suggested task wording. Student summaries above are paraphrases, not primary-source transcripts.
