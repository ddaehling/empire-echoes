# Context revision for the ungraded enquiry

Prepared 1 October 2026. These are student-facing drafts for the content author, followed by implementation and source notes. They introduce the historical situation and unfamiliar terms before the evidence and task. The two paragraphs in each draft can become `contextParagraphs`; a joined `context` string may remain for compatibility. They contain no assessment or response-length requirements.

## 1. When trade becomes rule — `profit-and-power`

The East India Company began as an English trading business run by merchants who wanted to make money from trade with Asia. In 1600, Queen Elizabeth I gave it a charter: an official document allowing the Company to operate. That permission did not make it the ruler of India. Over time, the Company became involved in wars and agreements with Indian rulers, as well as buying and selling goods.

This stop takes you to Bengal, in the eastern part of the Indian subcontinent. By 1765, the Company had gained the right to collect taxes there. Tax income is called revenue. You will compare the Company's position in 1600 and 1765, then use the evidence to explain how control of money could change what a trading business was able to do.

## 2. Who made freedom happen? — `freedom-and-memory`

Jamaica was a British colony in the Caribbean. On plantations, enslaved people were forced to work, often producing sugar for sale overseas. Enslavement meant that people were treated as property and denied control over their own lives. They resisted in different ways, while campaigners also demanded an end to slavery. Abolition means ending a practice through law; emancipation means being freed from slavery.

In the 1830s, resistance in Jamaica and decisions in Britain's Parliament formed part of the struggle over slavery and freedom. At this stop, you are helping to improve a museum label for visitors your age. A label tells readers who acted and what changed, so its choice of words matters. Read the evidence about resistance and the changes between 1833 and 1838 before deciding how to tell this story.

## 3. One colour, unequal power — `rule-and-resistance`

By the mid-nineteenth century, the East India Company governed large parts of India. In 1857, soldiers and other groups rose against its rule. Their reasons and aims differed; people in India did not all take the same side. After the uprising, Britain's Parliament transferred the Company's governing powers to the Crown in 1858. Crown rule meant government under the authority of the British monarch, carried out through British ministers and officials.

Queen Victoria then issued a proclamation: a public announcement explaining the new government's position. It addressed people living under British rule in India at a time of conflict and distrust. You will examine a short passage from that announcement. Pay attention to the actual words Victoria used, their possible purpose in this situation, and the difference between an official promise and evidence about people's lives.

## 4. Independence is more than a border — `departure-and-division`

In 1947, British rule in India ended after decades of political campaigning, resistance and negotiations. British India was divided into two independent countries, India and Pakistan. This division is called partition. New borders cut through Punjab and Bengal, regions where people from different religious communities lived. Millions of people fled or were forced to leave their homes, and violence affected many communities around the time of independence.

A map can show the new countries and their borders. To understand what this period meant to people, you will also read evidence of an individual's concerns or a family's remembered experience. The sources come from different moments: one letter was written before partition, while later testimony looks back on an earlier displacement. Keep those dates in mind when explaining what the evidence shows.

## 5. Who gets to belong? — `migration-and-belonging`

Empire Windrush was the name of a ship that brought passengers from the Caribbean and elsewhere to Britain in June 1948. At the time, Jamaica and several other Caribbean islands were British colonies, and their people were British subjects: they had a legal relationship with Britain through its empire. The name Windrush has also come to describe a wider generation of people who moved from the Caribbean to Britain after the Second World War.

Decades later, the Windrush scandal revealed that people who were lawfully living in Britain had been wrongly treated as having no right to be there. Some lost jobs or access to services. You will read about Ena Sullivan's life after her arrival and a separate review of the scandal. These sources raise different questions: what rights did a person have, how did institutions treat them, and where did they feel they belonged?

## 6. When does an empire end? — `remembering-empire`

Kenya, in East Africa, was under British colonial rule. In the 1950s, the Mau Mau movement fought against that rule. The colonial government declared an emergency and detained many people, meaning it held them in camps or prisons, often without a trial. People suffered torture and other ill-treatment at the hands of the colonial authorities. Kenya became independent in 1963, but some survivors later brought claims against the British government over their treatment.

In 2013, Britain's Foreign Secretary, William Hague, announced a settlement of those claims in Parliament. A settlement is an agreement that resolves a legal dispute. You will read two short extracts from his statement. One expresses regret; the other concerns liability, meaning legal responsibility. Examine how these words present the government's position and what the event reveals about the relationship between independence and later demands for recognition.

## 7. The past inside the present — `whose-britain`

Your school magazine is preparing an issue about British identities: how people describe themselves, where they feel they belong, and how others see them. These identities can overlap. Someone may feel British as well as Scottish, Welsh, English or Northern Irish, or connect their identity to a region, religion, family history or community. There is no single experience or opinion shared by everyone in the UK.

You have now encountered trade, resistance, government, independence, migration and public memory. Your comment will use selected evidence from these stops to explore how much imperial history helps explain identities today. Readers may know little about the events, so make each connection understandable. You can reach your own judgement, while considering other influences on identity and what your chosen sources cannot tell you. A comment presents and supports a point of view for its readers.

## Task clarifications for the author

- **Trade:** Keep the causal explanation in the learner's response. The introduction supplies the difference between commerce and governing authority, but leaves how revenue supports power to the evidence and reasoning. Avoid restoring the complete money–troops–administration answer inside the introduction.
- **Jamaica:** Put the explicitly invented label inside the one task panel, immediately before the replacement-label instruction. The learner writes the new label itself. Explain apprenticeship in the evidence as compulsory labour after legal emancipation; do not rely on the modern meaning of paid vocational training.
- **Victoria:** The visible authentic passage must immediately precede the task. “Analyse” means choose precise wording, explain what it presents, and connect that choice to a possible purpose or effect. “Effect” must remain a reasoned possibility, not a claim about every reader's actual reaction.
- **Partition:** Ask for a human experience **surrounding partition**, rather than automatically caused by the final 1947 border. Santokh Singh's concern is from 1946; Iqbal's aunt recalls displacement before partition. Let either provide the specific human detail, with its timing intact. If “Sikh” remains in the evidence, identify it there as a religious community; no prior knowledge of South Asian religions should be assumed.
- **Windrush:** The 1948 ship, the broader generation and the later scandal are related but distinct. Ena Sullivan's nationality form records her life and work; the review's unnamed quoted person supplies a separate experience. Do not give Sullivan the unnamed speaker's words or claim the form tells us how she felt. Legal status, institutional treatment and felt or accepted belonging should be distinguishable in plain language.
- **Kenya:** Explain both liability and settlement before analysis. Keep the meaning of the contrast open: the context should not announce the government's rhetorical strategy, decide whether the settlement was adequate, or imply that payment necessarily admitted liability. The claimants must not all be described as proven Mau Mau fighters: Hague states that many detainees' links with Mau Mau were never proved.
- **Final:** The school-magazine comment is the one product. Explain “complication” as another influence, a different experience or evidence that qualifies the writer's claim. Define “source limit” in terms of what this evidence cannot establish; an old source's date alone does not make it useless. Naming a source naturally in the writing is sufficient attribution; do not add a manual citation-administration task.

## Suggested reading order in the workspace

1. Title and place/date.
2. **The situation:** the two connected paragraphs above, visible by default.
3. **Evidence to use:** the essential local reading and, for the analysis tasks, the authentic quotation. Separate quotation from editorial paraphrase.
4. **Your task:** one concrete instruction naming the response product; include the invented museum label here at stop 2.
5. **Your response:** one editable writing area with a quiet saved-state message.
6. Optional disclosures for writing help, source details and further research. Map access remains nearby, with a clear return to the same stop.

The introduction should carry necessary knowledge even if a learner opens a stop directly. Optional disclosures can carry extra history; essential meanings such as partition, Windrush, Crown rule and liability should not be hidden behind them. No glossary quiz or extra written answer is needed.
