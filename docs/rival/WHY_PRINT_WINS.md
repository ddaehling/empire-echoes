# WHY PRINT WINS

## Fifteen things the printed chapter does that an interactive map-based web app will probably do worse

*An adversarial brief. Written against our own product. Every item below is grounded in something CHAMPION.md actually does; if the app cannot answer these, it should not ship as a replacement for the chapter.*

---

**1. A sustained causal argument survives on a page and dies in a tooltip.**
The chapter's thesis is a single 60-word proposition — improvisation, collaborators, the profit-to-cost ratio inverting — that is stated in section 1, tested at the Stamp Act, re-triggered at Bengal, cashed in at 1946, and settled in section 17. That is an argument carried across roughly 18,000 words and eleven deliberate callbacks. A map app's atomic unit is the territory-plus-date. An argument is not a territory. Nothing in a click-target can carry a claim that only becomes true across forty pages, so the app will end up asserting the thesis in a splash screen and then failing to demonstrate it anywhere.

**2. A chapter has a beginning, middle and end — an explorable map has no ending, so nothing lands.**
"The argument in twelve sentences" only works because the reader has come through the preceding sixteen sections in order; it is a *conclusion*, and conclusions are earned by sequence. An explorable interface cannot guarantee an ordering, so it cannot guarantee an arrival. Every user's last screen is different and arbitrary — they close the tab when they get bored, not when the argument completes. The app has no final paragraph, therefore no closure, therefore no moment at which the student knows what they were supposed to have learned.

**3. A reader's eye can hold three paragraphs in tension; a click-driven UI serialises everything.**
The abolition passage requires four facts to be *simultaneously present on the page*: the £20 million paid to owners, the forced apprenticeship, the real cost of the West Africa Squadron, and the substitution of Indian indenture. The instruction is literally "hold all of the following at once", and the pedagogy is the refusal to let either the redemption story or the cynicism story win. In an interface these become four panels, tabs or pins. Whichever is on screen wins; the others are memory. The single most sophisticated move in the chapter is destroyed by the act of navigation.

**4. A map makes territory look like the unit of analysis — which is the precise error the chapter exists to correct.**
Section 1 spends its whole length arguing that a quarter of the world was *claimed*, rather less *administered*, and less again *controlled*, and section 4 lands the punch: the empire was a system of communications before it was a territory. A choropleth cannot render claimed/administered/controlled without becoming unreadable, so it renders everything as flat colour — which is exactly the red-map lie the chapter opens by attacking. Worse, a map's visual weight is *area*, and the chapter's argument is that historical weight often ran the other way: Barbados at 166 square miles was worth more to eighteenth-century statesmen than the whole of North America, and the load-bearing points of the whole system — Gibraltar, Malta, Aden, Singapore, Hong Kong, Ascension — are dots. **The grammar of the medium contradicts the thesis of the chapter, twice.**

**5. Informal empire is unmappable, so the app will silently reproduce the lie it was built to expose.**
Gallagher and Robinson's "trade with informal control if possible; trade with rule when necessary" is one of the five named interpretations a student must be able to deploy, and its entire content is that British power operated in places that were never coloured red: Argentina, Persia, Siam, the Chinese treaty ports, Ottoman Egypt before 1882. An app built on polygons has no polygon for that. It will show 1860 as a modest scatter of red and thereby teach, structurally and without saying so, that mid-Victorian Britain was barely imperial — the exact misconception the chapter is written to kill.

**6. The mechanism column is the pedagogy; a time-slider teaches chronology and unteaches causation.**
The added-how / left-how table works because the student reads *down* a column and counts: conquest, treaty, settlement, chartered company, mandate termination, lease expiry, unilateral withdrawal. THINK 13.1 then converts that count into the chapter's central analytical question — is "the British Empire" one phenomenon or several? An animated map answers only *when*, which is the least interesting variable in the whole subject, and hides *how*, which is the entire lesson. A red flood spreading across Africa in the 1890s is memorable and teaches nothing except that it happened fast.

**7. Print can render a silence; software must always display something.**
The chapter repeatedly refuses to give a number, and the refusal is the teaching: Partition deaths "from around 200,000 to two million; the range itself tells you how little anyone was counting"; Black African camp deaths "probably higher, because nobody counted carefully"; the Bengal famine of 1770 where "modern demographers regard that figure as very uncertain". Above all THINK 11.3 — Hanslope Park, Operation Legacy, an archive whose gaps were *manufactured* — asks what happens to the balance of evidence when one side's records were burned and the other's hidden for fifty years. A map must pick a value, pick a shade, pick a border. **A deliberately created silence is not a data type.** The best methodological lesson in the chapter is the one an app is structurally least able to deliver.

**8. A quotation on a page carries its provenance; a quotation in an interface becomes decoration.**
Source G is teachable only because of the sentence attached to it: Rhodes said it to a journalist, and Lenin quoted it twenty-two years later in a pamphlet arguing that capitalism causes imperialism. The exercise — list three reasons for caution, then one reason it is still valuable — requires the attribution line to be as visually prominent as the quotation. Interfaces compress attribution into a byline, a hover, or an "i" icon, because screen real estate is scarce and designers protect the punchy line. The result is that the app will train precisely the habit the chapter's skills workshop exists to break: treating a quotation as a fact rather than as an act by someone with a purpose.

**9. Two texts side by side: Waitangi is invisible at map scale.**
The strongest source exercise in the chapter is four short parallel passages — "sovereignty" against *kāwanatanga*, "possession" against *tino rangatiratanga* — plus the detail that the same missionary translators had used *rangatiratanga* for "kingdom" in the Lord's Prayer, plus the 1863 confiscations, plus the Waitangi Tribunal still sitting. That is a full page of vertically aligned comparison. On a map, 6 February 1840 is New Zealand turning red. The typographic device that does the teaching — two columns the eye can cross in one movement — has no map equivalent, and putting it in a modal simply means the app has become a book with extra steps.

**10. Disproportion is a prose fact, not a spatial one.**
Thirty-two European settlers killed; over a million Kikuyu villagised; 11,503 insurgents recorded killed; 1,090 hanged; £19.9 million to 5,228 claimants in 2013. The Kenya case study works because those numbers sit in consecutive sentences and the reader does the division themselves. A map shows Kenya as one shape, the same shape in 1952 and 1963, no larger for the camps. The chapter's most morally serious paragraph has no cartographic footprint at all, and anything without a footprint on a map-first product gets demoted to a caption.

**11. A book can ambush the reader; an app's users route around whatever they don't click.**
The COMPLICATION boxes exist to damage the argument the chapter has just made — the Irish as agents of empire as well as its first victims, African states as active suppliers to the slave trade, the 20,000 enslaved people for whom the British lines were the road to freedom and the American Revolution the threat. Print puts self-refutation directly in the reader's path with no way round it. Interfaces are navigated, and anything that undercuts the main flow is, by definition, a thing users can decline to open. **Deliberate self-undermining is a print affordance.** Optional complications are not complications.

**12. Engagement metrics will quietly select against the material that matters most.**
Interaction data rewards the visually dramatic: the 1922 maximum, the scramble animation, the handover clock. It will not reward Naoroji's drain, the collaboration thesis, the causal line from the 1909 separate electorates to 1947, or THINK 15.1's warning that the balance-sheet framing assumes costs and benefits fell on the same people. Those are the load-bearing passages and they produce no clicks. No honest product process survives that pressure for long: within three iterations the app will be optimised toward the parts of imperial history that look good in motion, which are almost exactly the parts a serious chapter treats with the most suspicion.

**13. A page carries its own evidence base; an app buries it in a build.**
The chapter tells students to check every slave-trade figure against *Slave Voyages* and every quoted speech against *Hansard*, and it names where each contested figure comes from — Maddison, Bairoch, the Hunter Committee, the Fawcett Commission, Patnaik-and-the-argument-about-Patnaik. A head of department can audit all of it in one armchair sitting. In an app, the provenance of 11,503, of 379, of 3.1 million, of 28,000, lives in a dataset no teacher will ever open. A single wrong row is invisible, undetectable, and propagates to every student at once — and the app's polish makes it look *more* authoritative than the book that showed its working.

**14. Print has one version; software has a deploy — and curriculum is built on permanence.**
A chapter is stable, citable by page, photocopiable, annotatable in pencil, markable by a supply teacher, and it works in a room with no Wi-Fi, no devices, a trust filtering policy, and a supplier that has gone out of business. Everything a teacher plans a Tuesday lesson around has to be in the same place next Tuesday and the same place in three years when the cohort is re-taught. Interactive resources have a half-life measured in browser upgrades. **A head of history is buying a five-year scheme of work, not a demo.**

**15. Print has the standing to tell the reader they are wrong — and an interface does not.**
"Contingency is not absolution; but determinism is not analysis." "'Was it worth it?' is the wrong question, because it assumes a single 'it' and a single set of books." "Balance is not the absence of an argument." "Naming *which* British is almost always an upgrade." These are corrective, unsolicited and faintly rude, and the chapter earns the right to say them by taking a position and defending it for the length of a book. An exploratory interface has no voice, only affordances; it flatters the user by construction, because the user is the one driving. A UI that tells you your framing is wrong does not read as teaching. It reads as a bug.

---

### The summary charge

The chapter's subject is **causation, contested evidence, and things that are true of some cases and not others**. The app's medium is **place, extent and time**. Where those coincide the app will do better — it will show the stitching of coaling stations, the pace of the scramble, the simultaneity of taking Iraq while losing Ireland, better than any static page can. Everywhere else it is fighting its own grammar, and a serious teacher will notice within one lesson.

**The app does not lose because it is less good. It loses because it is answering a different question.** If it is to be defended, it must either (a) prove it can carry an argument to a conclusion the user cannot skip, (b) prove it can render uncertainty and deliberate archival silence without flattening them into a colour, and (c) prove a head of department can audit its numbers as fast as they can audit a page. Until then, the honest positioning is *companion to the chapter*, not *replacement for it*.
