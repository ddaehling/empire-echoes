## 1. Three objects, and the problem with the red map

Start with three things you could touch.

**An object.** In the Tower of London sits the Koh-i-Noor diamond, surrendered to the British Crown in 1849 under the Treaty of Lahore, signed by Maharaja Duleep Singh of the Punjab. He was ten years old. India, Pakistan, Iran and Afghanistan have all asked for it back. Britain has said no.

**A ship.** On 22 June 1948 the troopship *HMT Empire Windrush* docked at Tilbury with 1,027 passengers, most boarded at Kingston, Jamaica. They were not immigrants in any legal sense: they were British subjects, born under the Crown in a British colony. Seventy years later, several hundred people who arrived on ships like that one were wrongly detained, denied healthcare, sacked or deported, partly because the Home Office had destroyed the landing cards proving they had ever arrived.

**A debt.** When Parliament abolished slavery in 1833 it paid £20 million in compensation — not to the enslaved, but on some 46,000 claims by the people who had legally owned them. That was around 40 per cent of annual government expenditure. It was borrowed, folded into the national debt, and the Treasury has stated the last of it was not redeemed until 2015.

Every schoolroom in Britain once had the map: a Mercator projection with a quarter of the world washed in the same confident pink-red, so that the sun genuinely never set on it. The map was a lie — not because the territories were not British, but because one colour concealed the thing you most need to understand. **The British Empire was never one thing.** By 1900 the same monarch was simultaneously the constitutional queen of a self-governing settler dominion in Canada; the Empress of India, ruling some 300 million people through a viceroy and a few thousand British officials; the distant sovereign of Caribbean islands run by planter assemblies; the treaty-partner of several hundred Indian princes who kept their thrones and lost their foreign policy; the protector of Malay sultans; and the nominal superior of chartered companies with their own armies. Egypt from 1882 was occupied by British troops and run by a British "adviser" while remaining, on paper, Ottoman. **That is not a system. That is an accumulation.**

### The thesis

> **The British Empire was not planned. It was improvised — a rolling series of solutions to problems of debt, security and trade, executed largely by men on the spot whom London could neither fully control nor afford to disown. It expanded when the ratio of profit to cost looked favourable and when local collaborators could be found; it collapsed when that ratio inverted and those intermediaries withdrew their cooperation. Acquisition and dissolution are not two stories. They are one story, run forwards and then backwards.**

That argument has consequences. Be suspicious of the two most popular explanations, which are mirror images of each other: the *heroic* account of a civilising project deliberately built, and the *conspiratorial* account of a single grand plot to loot the world. Both credit London with far more intention, and far more control, than it ever had.

> **SOURCE A — J. R. Seeley, *The Expansion of England*, Cambridge lectures published 1883.** Seeley was Regius Professor of Modern History and an advocate of closer imperial federation; the book was a bestseller and a call to take the empire *more* seriously.
>
> "We seem, as it were, to have conquered and peopled half the world in a fit of absence of mind."

> **▸ THINK 1.1** — Seeley meant this as criticism. Taken too literally it becomes an alibi, as though nobody chose anything: people chose constantly, but locally, in the dark, for short-term reasons. Write one sentence on what is *useful* about the phrase and one on what is *misleading*. Keep your answer; you will revise it at the end.

**How to read the numbers.** At its maximum in the early 1920s the empire covered roughly 13.7 million square miles — about 35.5 million km², a quarter of the world's land surface. Angus Maddison's estimate puts the population under British rule at about 412 million in 1913, roughly 23 per cent of humanity. Treat both with care: they rest on colonial censuses of variable quality, and they lump together a Canadian farmer with full voting rights, an Indian peasant with none, and a nomad in the Sudan whom no British official had ever met. **A quarter of the world was *claimed*. Rather less was *administered*. Less again was actually *controlled*.**

Two sentences frame everything that follows. The empire was built over roughly 350 years and dismantled in roughly 35. And for most of its history it was governed not mainly by British soldiers and officials — of whom there were never enough — but through **local intermediaries who found it worth their while to cooperate**.

## 2. Key terms

| Term | Meaning | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Colony of settlement** | Territory where Britons settled permanently, displacing or dominating existing populations. | The logic is *replacement* of a population on the land, not extraction of labour from it — which is why Australia looks demographically nothing like Nigeria. |
| **Crown colony** | Governed from London through an appointed governor. | Not the same as a colony with *responsible government*, where elected local ministers rule. |
| **Protectorate** | A state that kept its ruler but surrendered foreign policy and usually much else. | Cheaper than a colony, and legally different: inhabitants were British *protected persons*, not British subjects. |
| **Indirect rule** | Governing through existing emirs, chiefs and princes. Associated with Frederick Lugard in Northern Nigeria. | It frequently *invented* authority: the "warrant chiefs" of south-eastern Nigeria were imposed on societies that had had no chiefs. |
| **Dominion** | A self-governing settler state sharing the Crown: Canada (1867), Australia (1901), New Zealand (1907), South Africa (1910), Newfoundland, the Irish Free State (1922). | The word implies whiteness in practice. No non-settler colony was given dominion status before 1947. |
| **Mandate** | Territory taken from Germany or the Ottomans after 1918, held under League supervision with a duty to prepare it for self-government. | Empire with a conscience clause and an expiry date. Critics called it "empire with paperwork". |
| **Informal empire** | Countries never coloured red but shaped by British capital, navies and treaties — Argentina, Persia, the Chinese treaty ports. | Central to Gallagher and Robinson (1953). Its weakness: stretched far enough, it becomes unfalsifiable. |
| **Chartered company** | A private company granted monopoly rights and sometimes powers of government and war: EIC (1600), Royal African Company (1672), Hudson's Bay Company (1670), British South Africa Company (1889). | Empire was frequently privatised before it was nationalised. |
| **Diwani** | The Mughal right to collect land revenue. Granted to the EIC for Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in 1765. | The moment a trading company became a tax-collecting state. |
| **Indentured labour** | Contract labour, mainly Indian, after 1834, bound for a fixed term on plantations. | Legally free, practically coerced. |
| **Responsible government** | Colonial ministers answerable to an elected colonial legislature. First granted 1848. | The hinge between "colony" and "dominion" — and it transferred power over indigenous peoples to settlers. |
| **Decolonisation** | Transfer of sovereignty from imperial power to successor state. | A misleadingly tidy word for a process including negotiation, riot, insurgency, partition and civil war. |

## 4. The empire as a body: a map in words, 1922

Close your eyes and build it. The empire at its maximum had five limbs and a set of stitching.

**The Atlantic and American limb.** Newfoundland, claimed in 1583, a separate Dominion until 1949; behind it Canada, ocean to ocean, self-governing since 1867. South past Bermuda into the Caribbean, where the map goes measles-red: Jamaica, the Bahamas, Trinidad, Barbados, the Leewards and Windwards, with British Honduras and British Guiana on the mainland, and far south the Falklands. Small dots that once mattered more than all of Canada, because sugar was worth more than fur.

**The African limb** — the Cape-to-Cairo band, complete after 1918 when German East Africa became the mandate of Tanganyika: Egypt, the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, Uganda, Kenya, Tanganyika, the two Rhodesias, Bechuanaland, into the Union of South Africa. On the west coast four separate blocks: the Gambia, Sierra Leone, the Gold Coast, and Nigeria — the largest, with a Muslim north ruled indirectly through emirs and a south ruled more directly, a fault line that would matter greatly after 1960.

**The Middle Eastern limb**, new and unstable: mandates for Palestine, Transjordan and Iraq; protectorates from Aden round the Gulf to Kuwait; Cyprus, occupied since 1878, annexed 1914.

**The Indian limb**, the heart of it: British India plus 565 princely states under paramountcy — around 300 million people, more than three-quarters of everyone Britain ruled. Burma was governed as part of India until 1937; Ceylon separately.

**The Southeast Asian and Pacific limb.** The Straits Settlements, the Malay states, Sarawak, North Borneo, Brunei, Hong Kong; then Australia, federated in 1901, and New Zealand, a Dominion from 1907, each with a small Pacific sub-empire of its own.

**And the stitching**: a chain of coaling stations, cable relays and naval bases — Gibraltar, Malta, Cyprus, Aden, Ceylon, Singapore, Hong Kong, the Falklands, St Helena, Ascension. This is the real skeleton of the thing. **The empire was a system of communications before it was a territory. When the communications failed, the territory followed.**

## 5. The Atlantic machine, 1583–1783

### 5.1 The rehearsal: Ireland

English expansion did not begin in Virginia. It began next door. The Tudor and Stuart **plantations** — Munster from 1586, Ulster from 1609 — established a repertoire that travelled: confiscate land from a population defined as barbarous, grant it to loyal settlers, justify it by religion and civility, garrison the result. Cromwell's reconquest of 1649–53 killed a catastrophic share of the Irish population and transferred landownership wholesale. The personnel were the same men: Humphrey Gilbert, who claimed Newfoundland in 1583, and Walter Raleigh, who sponsored Roanoke in 1585, had both served in the Irish wars.

> **COMPLICATION.** And yet the Irish were also *agents* of empire far out of proportion to their numbers — as soldiers, administrators, missionaries and settlers in Australia, India and the Caribbean. Ireland is simultaneously Britain's first colony and a full participant in colonising others. **Any frame that makes people purely victims or purely villains will break on Ireland.** It will break the same way on India, on Africa, on almost everywhere.

### 5.2 Sugar, and the machinery of enslavement

England came late and badly to overseas expansion. Gilbert drowned on the way home in 1583; Roanoke vanished. Not until **Jamestown in 1607** did an English settlement in the Americas survive, and it very nearly did not.

What changed everything was not gold or God but **a crop**. Tobacco in Virginia from the 1610s, and above all **sugar in the Caribbean** — St Kitts 1624, Barbados 1627, Jamaica seized from Spain in 1655 — created plantations: large agricultural factories requiring enormous, disciplined, replaceable labour forces to produce a single export commodity for a distant market. Sugar was extraordinarily profitable and extraordinarily lethal to produce; cutting cane and boiling juice in open coppers in Caribbean heat destroyed people. Planters found it cheaper to buy replacements than to let the enslaved population reproduce. **That single accounting decision is why the Atlantic slave trade lasted as long as it did.**

Indentured servants from Britain and Ireland were tried first and were insufficient. From the 1640s the Caribbean colonies turned decisively to enslaved Africans. England entered the trade seriously with the **Royal African Company (1672)**, with the Duke of York — the future James II — as governor; when its monopoly was effectively broken in 1698, volumes exploded as private merchants of Bristol, London and above all Liverpool poured in.

**The numbers, which you should be able to quote.** The *Slave Voyages* database, built from the records of over 36,000 voyages, estimates around **12.5 million** Africans embarked across the whole Atlantic trade, of whom about **10.7 million** were landed alive. British and British-colonial ships carried roughly **3.1–3.4 million** and landed about **2.7 million**. Take one second over the gap between those last two figures: roughly one in seven people loaded onto a British slave ship died before it landed. Britain was, for most of the eighteenth century, the largest slave-trading power on earth. This is not a footnote to the first empire. It *was* the first empire's economic engine.

> **SOURCE B — Olaudah Equiano, *The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano*, London, 1789.** Equiano stated he was born in Igboland, was enslaved as a child, bought his freedom, and became a leading abolitionist. The book was a bestseller and a deliberate contribution to the abolition campaign. Some scholars, notably Vincent Carretta, have argued from baptismal and naval records that he may have been born in South Carolina; the debate is unresolved.
>
> "The shrieks of the women, and the groans of the dying, rendered the whole a scene of horror almost inconceivable."

> **▸ THINK 5.1 — source utility drill.**
> (a) What does Source B tell us that a ship's logbook could not?
> (b) Does the dispute about Equiano's birthplace destroy the source's value, reduce it, or change *what* it is useful for? Distinguish its value as testimony about the Middle Passage from its value as evidence about the abolition campaign.
> (c) A well-trained answer notes that a source written *to persuade* is not thereby unreliable — it is reliable evidence of what abolitionists believed would persuade.

> **COMPLICATION.** Slavery was not a British invention, and African states and merchants — Dahomey, Asante, the Aro networks of the Niger delta — were active suppliers, using their own wars, for their own reasons. None of this dilutes British responsibility for the demand, the finance, the shipping and the plantation regime. It does mean "Europeans went and took people" is too simple: Europeans mostly stayed on the coast, and the violence of capture happened inland. And resistance was continuous — shipboard revolts, poisoning, flight, and the maroons of Jamaica, who fought the British to a treaty in 1739 and were never conquered.

> ### CASE STUDY: Barbados, or how an island was rebuilt around a plant
>
> Barbados is 166 square miles — smaller than the Isle of Wight. In 1640 it grew tobacco and cotton on smallholdings worked by white indentured servants, and its population was mostly European. By 1680 it grew almost nothing but sugar, on consolidated estates, and the majority of its people were enslaved Africans. In four decades the island's ecology, demography, law and social order had been entirely reconstructed around one commodity. The Barbados Slave Code of 1661 — which classified the enslaved as property and offered almost no protection — was then exported to Jamaica, Antigua and South Carolina. Small islands can be historically enormous: in the 1770s the tiny British Caribbean was worth far more in trade to Britain than the whole of North America.

### 5.3 The chartered-company cycle, and the wars that moved the map

The first empire was governed by an idea: **mercantilism**. The Navigation Acts of 1651 and 1660 laid down that colonial goods must travel in British or colonial ships, that "enumerated" commodities must be shipped to Britain first, and that colonists should buy British manufactures. The empire was conceived as a closed circuit designed to run a trade surplus for the metropolis.

Alongside it ran the **chartered company**: a device by which the Crown granted merchants a monopoly and quasi-governmental powers, so that expansion could happen without the Crown paying for it. The Hudson's Bay Company's charter of 1670 handed investors monopoly rights over the entire drainage basin of Hudson Bay — about 3.9 million square kilometres of other people's country, signed away in a document none of its inhabitants saw.

**Remember this device.** It is the single clearest evidence for this chapter's thesis, and the pattern is always the same: **Crown outsources empire because it will not pay for it → company overreaches → scandal → state takeover.** You will see it at the East India Company in 1773–84, at the Imperial British East Africa Company in 1895, at the Royal Niger Company in 1900, and at the British South Africa Company after the Jameson Raid.

War did the rest. The eighteenth century was a long duel with France. The **Treaty of Utrecht (1713)** brought Newfoundland, Acadia (renamed Nova Scotia), the Hudson Bay territories, Gibraltar (taken 1704), Minorca (1708) and the *asiento* — the contract to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America. The **Treaty of Paris (10 February 1763)** was the greatest single haul in British history: New France, Florida, all French claims east of the Mississippi, Senegal, and Grenada, Dominica, St Vincent and Tobago.

**In 1763 Britain had the largest empire in the world and no idea how to pay for it. Twenty years later it had lost the most valuable part.**

### 5.4 The first amputation: America, 1763–1783

Here is the first great dissolution, and it teaches a lesson that recurs in 1947 and 1960: **empires most often break at the point where the imperial power tries to make the colony pay its own costs.**

Victory in 1763 nearly doubled the national debt, to around £130 million, and left Britain garrisoning a vast new frontier. Successive governments concluded, not unreasonably, that colonists defended at British expense should contribute: the Sugar Act (1764), the **Stamp Act (1765)**, the Townshend duties (1767), the Tea Act (1773). Every measure collided with a principle the colonists had absorbed from English constitutional history itself. They had their own elected assemblies, long practice in self-government, and a political vocabulary borrowed from the Civil War and 1688. **They were not rebelling against English liberty; they were claiming it.**

The sequence ran fast: the Boston Massacre (1770); the Tea Party (16 December 1773); the Coercive Acts (1774); Lexington and Concord (19 April 1775); the **Declaration of Independence (4 July 1776)**. Britain might still have won a colonial war. It could not win a world war: France entered in 1778, Spain in 1779, the Dutch in 1780, and the Royal Navy was stretched from the Caribbean to the Channel to India. At **Yorktown (19 October 1781)** Cornwallis surrendered to a Franco-American force whose escape route was closed by a French fleet. The **Treaty of Paris (3 September 1783)** recognised the United States.

Three points that stronger candidates make:

- **It was a world war, not a colonial rebellion.** Britain also lost Minorca and Florida to Spain, partly because it temporarily lost command of the sea.
- **Loyalism was real.** Roughly 60,000 loyalists left, many to Nova Scotia and Upper Canada, including several thousand Black loyalists promised freedom for fighting for the Crown, some of whom later helped found Freetown in Sierra Leone in 1792. Modern Canada is partly a product of American independence.
- **Losing America did not end the empire.** Trade with the independent United States grew rapidly — evidence that political rule was not necessary for commercial profit, an idea that would take fifty years to mature into free-trade orthodoxy.

> **THINK ABOUT THIS.** Around 20,000 enslaved people fled to British lines during that war, after British commanders offered freedom to those who joined them. In *that* war, the empire was a road to freedom and the revolution was a threat to it. How do you write that into a story about British oppression — without ignoring it, and without letting it excuse the three million?

## 6. The swing to the East, 1757–1857

### 6.1 CASE STUDY: Bengal, or how a trading company became a government

The East India Company was chartered on 31 December 1600 — a monopoly for investors chasing pepper, not an arm of the state. For 150 years it was a supplicant at the court of the Mughal emperors, trading from fortified "factories" at Surat, Madras (1639), Bombay (arriving in 1661 in Catherine of Braganza's dowry) and Calcutta (1690).

Two things changed that. The fragmentation of Mughal power after Aurangzeb's death in 1707 produced successor states — Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, the Marathas, Mysore — competing with one another and open to alliance. And the Anglo-French global war of the mid-eighteenth century taught both companies to raise armies of Indian soldiers, **sepoys**, drilled in European style. In such a system a well-drilled force with reliable artillery and European credit was an extremely valuable ally. **The Company did not defeat "India". It took sides in Indian politics.**

At **Plassey on 23 June 1757**, Robert Clive's force of roughly 3,000 defeated the Nawab of Bengal, Siraj-ud-Daula, whose army numbered perhaps 50,000 — because a large part of that army, under Mir Jafar, had been bought in advance and did not fight. Note this: **the founding victory of British India was won by conspiracy, not conquest.** Seven years later at **Buxar (22 October 1764)** the Company beat a genuine coalition in a real battle, and in the **Treaty of Allahabad (12 August 1765)** the emperor Shah Alam II granted it the **diwani** of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.

That grant is the true beginning of the British Empire in Asia. A commercial company now had a tax base of perhaps twenty million people. It could raise armies from Indian revenue and recruit Indian soldiers to fight for it. By the early nineteenth century the Company's armies numbered around 200,000 men — larger than the British Army — and were overwhelmingly Indian, commanded by Britons. **The British conquered India largely with Indian soldiers paid out of Indian taxes.** No proposition about the empire is more important, or more often forgotten. Understanding it is not a defence of empire; it is the only way to explain how something so small held something so large — and it is why the collapse, when collaboration failed, was so fast.

What followed was not benevolent. In **1770** a catastrophic famine struck Bengal. Contemporary British reports claimed up to ten million dead, perhaps a third of the population; modern demographers regard that figure as very uncertain and generally argue lower, but nobody disputes it was one of the great mortality events of the century, or that Company revenue demands and its servants' grain speculation made it worse. Revenue collection continued through it.

> **SOURCE C — Edmund Burke MP, speech on Fox's East India Bill, House of Commons, 1 December 1783.** Burke, Irish-born, later prosecuted the former Governor-General Warren Hastings in an impeachment that ran from 1788 to Hastings's acquittal in 1795.
>
> "The Company in India does not exist as a commonwealth… but as a state in the disguise of a merchant."
>
> Of the Company's young servants, who came, enriched themselves and left, he said they were "birds of prey and passage".

> **▸ THINK 6.1** — Burke was a British MP attacking a British institution in Parliament, in the language of tyranny. Does loud, principled criticism *inside* Britain support the argument that the empire was self-correcting, or undermine it — given that Hastings was acquitted and the Company kept its Indian territories for another 75 years? Argue both sides in a paragraph each.

Parliament responded not by ending Company rule but by regulating it: the **Regulating Act (1773)** and **Pitt's India Act (1784)** created a Board of Control in London and a Governor-General in Calcutta. Notice the pattern: British reform of empire almost always meant tightening state supervision of empire, not surrendering it.

Expansion then proceeded by war and treaty over sixty years: Mysore defeated (Tipu Sultan killed at Seringapatam, 4 May 1799); the Marathas broken by 1818; Sind annexed 1843; **the Punjab in 1849**. Around India an Asian ring accumulated: Penang (1786), **Singapore (1819)**, Malacca (1824), Burma in three wars (1826, 1852, 1885), and **Hong Kong Island, ceded by the Treaty of Nanking on 29 August 1842** — after a war fought to force China to accept the import of a narcotic grown in British India. Gladstone, then a young Tory MP, told the Commons on 8 April 1840 that "a war more unjust in its origin… I do not know and I have not read of". Britain won anyway; Kowloon followed in 1860 and the **New Territories on a 99-year lease in 1898**. *A war over narcotics in 1839 set a clock that ran out 158 years later.*

> **▸ THINK 6.2** — "The British conquered India with Indian soldiers paid from Indian taxes." Suggest three different reasons why an Indian man in 1800 might have chosen to enlist. Does calling him a "collaborator" help or hinder understanding?

### 6.2 Settlement, and the invention of the dominion

**Australia.** On **26 January 1788** the First Fleet landed at Sydney Cove, founding New South Wales as a penal colony — Britain having lost the American dumping ground for convicts. About 162,000 convicts were transported before the practice ended in 1868. The legal basis was **terra nullius**, "nobody's land", because Aboriginal Australians did not farm or fence in ways Europeans recognised. It was false when invented and was formally demolished by Australia's High Court in the *Mabo* judgment of 3 June 1992. In between, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander populations collapsed through disease, dispossession and frontier killings, of which the University of Newcastle's *Colonial Frontier Massacres* project maps hundreds with sources.

**Strategic harvest.** The Napoleonic Wars were an acquisition engine, because Britain's method of fighting France was to take the overseas possessions of France and its satellites: the Cape (1795, retaken 1806), Ceylon's coast (1796), Trinidad (1797), Malta (1800), Mauritius (1810) — confirmed at Vienna. Notice what these have in common. Almost none were valuable for their own produce. They were **naval stations and route-nodes**. The empire's logic in this period is maritime and strategic before it is territorial.

**Canada and responsible government.** After rebellions in Upper and Lower Canada in 1837–38, Lord Durham's *Report* (1839) proposed **responsible government** — ministries answerable to elected local assemblies — while London kept foreign policy, defence and trade. Nova Scotia and the Province of Canada got it in 1848; the British North America Act created the **Dominion of Canada on 1 July 1867**. The Durham formula was Britain's answer to 1776, and it worked — for white settlers.

> **COMPLICATION.** "Responsible government" was responsible to settlers. In the same decades Canada was signing the Numbered Treaties with First Nations and building the residential school system, which ran into the 1990s and which Canada's Truth and Reconciliation Commission in 2015 described as **cultural genocide**. Self-government for one population was intensified rule over another. **For indigenous peoples the dominions were not the empire's exit; they were its most permanent form.**

> ### CASE STUDY: The Treaty of Waitangi, or the empire in two languages
>

> **SOURCE D — The Treaty of Waitangi, 6 February 1840, in both texts.**
>
> **English, Article 1:** the chiefs cede to the Queen "all the rights and powers of Sovereignty".
> **Māori, Article 1:** the chiefs give the Queen "te Kawanatanga katoa" — complete *governance*.
>

### 6.3 Abolition: conscience, economics, and the price of it

And then, astonishingly, Britain turned against slavery. The abolition movement — founded in 1787, driven by Quakers, evangelicals, formerly enslaved writers such as Equiano, Cugoano and Mary Prince, mass petitioning, a consumer sugar boycott, and above all the resistance of the enslaved themselves — achieved the **abolition of the British slave trade by an Act of 25 March 1807** and the **Slavery Abolition Act of 28 August 1833**, freeing some 800,000 people from 1 August 1834.

Do not let this become a simple redemption story, and do not let it become a simple cynicism story either. Both are available and both are wrong. Hold all of the following at once:

- Emancipation came with **£20 million in compensation** paid on roughly 46,000 claims by owners — about 40 per cent of annual government expenditure, borrowed and, the Treasury has stated, not fully redeemed until 2015. The UCL *Legacies of British Slave-ownership* project, built from the government's own Slave Compensation Commission records, has traced where that money went: country houses, railways, banks, insurance firms, galleries, university endowments. **The map of that money is a map of the British establishment.**
- The freed were bound into an "apprenticeship" that kept them working for former owners until it was abandoned early, on 1 August 1838, under pressure.
- The Royal Navy's West Africa Squadron then spent six decades suppressing the slave trade of *other* nations, at real cost in ships and lives — and that anti-slavery mission became, in west and east Africa, a standard justification for intervention and annexation. **Abolition ended one form of coercion and licensed another.**
- Slavery in Company territories was excluded from the 1833 Act and addressed separately, imperfectly, in 1843. And **indentured labour** replaced enslaved labour: between 1834 and 1917 roughly 1.3–1.5 million Indians were shipped under five-year contracts to Mauritius, Trinidad, British Guiana, Natal and Fiji, in conditions abolitionists themselves compared to the slave trade. That policy is why Trinidad, Guyana, Fiji, Mauritius and South Africa have large Indian-descended populations today. **Empire did not only extract wealth; it rearranged where humanity lives.**

Why did abolition happen when it did? A classic "how far do you agree" question, with three named positions.

> **INTERPRETATION 1 — Eric Williams**, *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944). Williams, a Trinidadian historian who became his country's first prime minister, argued that slave profits financed British industrialisation and that abolition came when the West Indian sugar economy had ceased to be central — that is, when it was convenient. Humanitarianism was the vocabulary, not the cause.

> **INTERPRETATION 2 — Seymour Drescher**, *Econocide* (1977). Drescher tested Williams statistically and argued the opposite: the British slave system was expanding and profitable in 1807, so abolition was economic self-harm, driven by genuine popular mobilisation — petitions, boycotts, the largest mass campaign in British political history to that date.

> **INTERPRETATION 3 — the enslaved themselves.** Michael Craton and, more recently, Priyamvada Gopal (*Insurgent Empire*, 2019) stress that abolition followed a wave of enormous revolts: Tacky's War (1760), the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), Barbados 1816, Demerara 1823, and the Jamaican "Baptist War" of 1831–32 involving some 60,000 people. On this view Parliament abolished slavery because the enslaved had made it unsustainable.

> **▸ THINK 6.4** — These three are not simply incompatible. Write one paragraph using all three, in a defensible order of importance, to explain 1833. Hint: causes can be necessary without being sufficient. Which explains the *timing*? Which explains the *possibility*?

Meanwhile, in the empire's oldest and nearest colony, catastrophe. The **Great Irish Famine (1845–52)** killed around one million people and drove between one and one and a half million to emigrate, in a United Kingdom that was the richest state in the world, while food continued to be exported under guard. Relief was constrained by ideological commitment to markets and by official belief that Irish poverty was a moral failing. Ireland's population has never recovered its pre-famine level. **Ireland is the standing rebuke to any account of the empire that places it safely overseas.**

## 7. The machinery: how so few ruled so many

Before the second half of the story, understand the mechanisms. Four of them do most of the work, and they are why the empire was cheap enough to be worth having.

**1. Indirect rule.** Britain almost never had enough administrators: in the 1930s the Indian Civil Service, governing some 350 million people, numbered barely a thousand men. So Britain ruled *through* existing authorities — 565 princely states covering roughly a third of India, emirs in northern Nigeria, chiefs in Uganda and the Gold Coast. Lugard codified it in *The Dual Mandate* (1922). Where suitable authorities did not exist, Britain invented them: the "warrant chiefs" of south-eastern Nigeria, imposed on societies that had governed themselves without chiefs, and violently rejected in the Women's War of 1929.

**2. Classification.** Colonial states are counting machines. Censuses, land settlements and law codes required people to belong to fixed categories: this caste, that tribe, this religious community. Categories that had been fluid became legal facts with consequences for taxation, army recruitment (the "martial races" doctrine) and, from the Morley–Minto reforms of 1909, for who could vote for whom. **Separate electorates made religion the basic unit of Indian politics. You cannot understand 1947 without 1909.**

**3. Racial hierarchy, formal and informal.** Colour bars in clubs, hotels, railway carriages and promotion ladders were rarely in imperial statute but nearly universal in practice. In Kenya's "White Highlands" African ownership of the best farmland was prohibited outright; in South Africa the Natives Land Act of 1913 restricted African landownership to about 7 per cent of the country.

**4. Force, held in reserve and used.** The empire's normal condition was not war but the credible threat of it. When the threat failed, the response was punitive: expeditions, collective fines, burned villages, and — after 1919 — the aerial bombing of villages in Iraq, Waziristan and Somaliland, a technique the RAF explicitly promoted as cheap "air policing".

> **INTERPRETATION 4 — Ronald Robinson**, "Non-European Foundations of European Imperialism" (1972), proposed a **theory of collaboration**: empire on the cheap depended on finding local elites — princes, chiefs, landlords, merchants, soldiers, clerks — whose interests were served by working with the imperial power. Expansion happened where collaboration could be arranged; collapse happened when collaborators defected. **This is the single most useful analytical tool in the chapter, because it explains both halves of the story with one mechanism.**

## 8. The Victorian zenith, 1857–1902

### 8.1 1857: the year the mask slipped

On 10 May 1857 sepoys of the Bengal Army mutinied at Meerut, marched on Delhi and proclaimed the aged Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar as their sovereign. The rising spread across the Gangetic plain, gathering dispossessed princes, peasants crushed by revenue demands, artisans ruined by machine-made cloth, and religious leaders alarmed by missionary activity. The greased-cartridge trigger is remembered because it is vivid, but it lit a fire already laid: the annexation of Awadh in 1856, Dalhousie's "doctrine of lapse", revenue settlements that dispossessed rural elites.

Atrocity ran in both directions and was then weaponised. The killing of British women and children at Kanpur was reported in Britain in apocalyptic language; the reprisals — villages burned, prisoners blown from the mouths of cannon, mass hangings along the Grand Trunk Road — were extensively documented, sometimes proudly, by the men who carried them out. Indian deaths ran into the hundreds of thousands.

**What you call this event is itself a historical argument.** British sources long said "the Indian Mutiny", which frames it as military indiscipline; Indian nationalists from V. D. Savarkar onward called it the First War of Independence, which frames it as a national rising; most academic historians now say "the Rebellion of 1857", because it was much more than a mutiny and much less than a nation.

The constitutional consequences were decisive. The **Government of India Act (2 August 1858)** abolished Company rule and transferred India to the Crown; the Royal Titles Act (1876) made Victoria Empress of India, proclaimed at the Delhi Durbar on 1 January 1877. And a new caution set in: no more annexations of princely states, and a firm alliance with conservative Indian elites. **The Raj after 1858 was more powerful and more nervous than the Company had ever been.**

> **SOURCE E — Thomas Babington Macaulay, "Minute on Indian Education", 2 February 1835.** Macaulay was arguing that government money should fund English-language education rather than Sanskrit and Arabic learning. His view prevailed.
>
> "We must at present do our best to form a class who may be interpreters between us and the millions whom we govern; a class of persons Indian in blood and colour, but English in taste, in opinions, in morals, and in intellect."

> **▸ THINK 8.1** — Macaulay got his policy, and the class did emerge: Naoroji, Banerjea, Gandhi (a barrister of the Inner Temple), Nehru (Harrow and Cambridge), Jinnah (Lincoln's Inn), Ambedkar (Columbia and the LSE) — all of whom turned liberal vocabulary against the empire that taught it. The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, was in part a creature of Macaulay's policy. What does that suggest about whether colonial rulers control the consequences of their own policies?

### 8.2 Free trade and the empire nobody mapped

For most of the mid-Victorian period Britain did not want more colonies. It wanted markets. Having repealed the Corn Laws in 1846 and the Navigation Acts in 1849, Britain became the world's workshop, banker and shipper, and where it could get trade without rule it took trade without rule: treaty ports in China, gunboats in Latin America, railways in Argentina, a de facto veto in Persia.

> **INTERPRETATION 5 — John Gallagher and Ronald Robinson**, "The Imperialism of Free Trade" (1953). The mid-Victorian decades were not an anti-imperial interlude between two bursts of expansion. Britain always sought the same thing — access and security for trade — and used the cheapest available method: **"trade with informal control if possible; trade with rule when necessary."** Expansion was often driven not from London but by crises on the periphery, to which London responded reluctantly and expensively.
>
> *Objection to know:* D. K. Fieldhouse (*Economics and Empire*, 1973) replied that a concept stretched to cover everything explains nothing, and that "informal empire" is very hard to falsify.

Egypt is the textbook case. The Suez Canal opened in 1869 and instantly became the artery to India; in 1875 Disraeli bought the bankrupt Khedive's 44 per cent shareholding for £4 million. When nationalist officers under Ahmed 'Urabi revolted against foreign financial control in 1881–82, Gladstone's Liberal government — elected on anti-imperial rhetoric — bombarded Alexandria and invaded, winning at **Tel el-Kebir on 13 September 1882**. Britain announced the occupation would be temporary. It lasted 74 years.

> **▸ THINK 8.2** — Egypt was occupied by a government that disapproved of imperialism, for reasons it called temporary, to protect an investment made by its predecessor. What does this suggest about the usefulness of "British policy" as an explanatory category?

### 8.3 The Scramble for Africa, 1880–1902

In 1870 Europeans controlled perhaps a tenth of Africa, mostly coastal. By 1914 they controlled all of it except Ethiopia and Liberia. It is the fastest partition of a continent in history, and Britain took the largest share: Egypt (1882), the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan (reconquered 1896–98), Bechuanaland (1885), the Rhodesias (from the British South Africa Company's charter of 1889), Zanzibar (1890), Nyasaland (1891), Uganda (1894), Kenya (1895), Nigeria (Lagos 1861; protectorates 1900; amalgamated 1914), the Gold Coast (1874, Asante annexed 1901–02), Sierra Leone and the Gambia.

**Why then? Four families of explanation, which you should be able to name and weigh.**

1. **Economic (Hobson, Lenin).** J. A. Hobson, a radical Liberal journalist who covered the South African War, argued in *Imperialism: A Study* (1902) that inequality at home left capitalists with surplus savings and no domestic outlet, and that a small class of financiers manipulated the nation into wars that served them. Lenin adapted this in 1917. *Objection:* most British capital went to the United States, Argentina and the dominions, not to newly conquered Africa.
2. **Strategic (Robinson and Gallagher, *Africa and the Victorians*, 1961).** Ministers were not chasing profits but reacting defensively to crises on the periphery, above all Egypt in 1882. Once in Egypt, Britain had to secure the Nile; hence Sudan and Uganda. They coined "the official mind" for the assumptions inside Whitehall that generated decisions. *Objection:* it underweights business lobbying and men on the spot.
3. **European rivalry.** German unification (1871) and Belgian activity in the Congo turned Africa into a board on which European states scored points; once others were claiming, not claiming became a strategic loss. The **Berlin Conference (15 November 1884 – 26 February 1885)** did not carve up Africa, contrary to popular belief; it set the *rules* for carving it up, above all that claims required "effective occupation" — which turned paper claims into expeditions. No African was present.
4. **Technology and medicine.** Quinine prophylaxis, steamboats on rivers, the telegraph, breech-loaders and, from the 1880s, the Maxim gun collapsed the cost of conquest. At **Omdurman on 2 September 1898** an Anglo-Egyptian force lost around 48 dead; the Mahdist army lost about 10,000. Hilaire Belloc's couplet of 1898 — "Whatever happens, we have got / The Maxim Gun, and they have not" — is a joke about exactly that ratio, and the ratio explains the speed of the scramble better than any ideology.

> **SOURCE F — Lord Salisbury, Prime Minister, House of Lords, 6 August 1890,** on Anglo-French boundary negotiations in Africa.
>
> "We have been engaged in drawing lines upon maps where no white man's foot ever trod; we have been giving away mountains and rivers and lakes to each other, only hindered by the small impediment that we never knew exactly where the mountains and rivers and lakes were."

> **SOURCE G — Cecil Rhodes, in conversation with the journalist W. T. Stead, 1895, as quoted by V. I. Lenin in *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1917).** Rhodes was then Prime Minister of the Cape Colony and the dominant figure in diamonds and gold.
>
> "My cherished idea is a solution for the social problem… in order to save the forty million inhabitants of the United Kingdom from a bloody civil war, we colonial statesmen must acquire new lands… The Empire, as I have always said, is a bread and butter question."

> **▸ THINK 8.3 — provenance drill.** Rhodes said this to a journalist; Lenin quoted it twenty-two years later, in a pamphlet arguing that capitalism causes imperialism. List three separate reasons for caution about using Source G as evidence of *why* the scramble happened. Then give one reason it remains genuinely valuable. Then look at Source F: Salisbury is being funny. What is the consequence of that ignorance for people living in Africa in 1890 — and for African borders today?

Africans were not passive. The Asante fought four wars, the last led in 1900 by the queen mother Yaa Asantewaa. The Zulu destroyed a British column at Isandlwana on 22 January 1879. The Ndebele and Shona rose in the *Chimurenga* of 1896–97. The Mahdist state ruled Sudan independently from 1885 to 1898. Resistance mostly failed militarily. **It succeeded in shaping what colonial rule then looked like: where conquest was costly, Britain governed indirectly and lightly, through the very elites it had failed to destroy.**

### 8.4 The war that frightened Britain

The **South African War (11 October 1899 – 31 May 1902)** was supposed to be a short police action against two small Boer republics sitting on the world's richest goldfield. It took nearly three years, some 450,000 imperial troops and over £200 million. When the Boers turned to guerrilla warfare, Kitchener responded with farm-burning and the mass internment of civilians. Around **28,000 Boer civilians died in the camps, some 22,000 of them children under sixteen**, mostly of measles, typhoid and dysentery. In separate, worse-supplied camps for Black Africans **at least 14,000 died** — the true figure is probably higher, because nobody counted carefully, and it was omitted from British public memory for most of the twentieth century.

> **SOURCE H — Emily Hobhouse, "Report of a Visit to the Camps of Women and Children in the Cape and Orange River Colonies", presented to the South African Women and Children's Distress Fund, London, June 1901.** Hobhouse was a Cornish welfare campaigner with no official standing, who travelled privately, was denounced in Britain as hysterical and pro-Boer, and was later refused re-entry to South Africa. Her report described overcrowded tents, inadequate rations, absent sanitation and children dying of measles and typhoid, and warned that to keep the camps going was "murder to the children". It prompted the appointment of the Fawcett Commission, whose findings largely confirmed hers. Weeks earlier, on 14 June 1901, the Leader of the Opposition Sir Henry Campbell-Bannerman had asked: "When is a war not a war? When it is carried on by methods of barbarism in South Africa."

> **THINK ABOUT THIS.** Hobhouse and Campbell-Bannerman prove that people at the time knew, and objected, in language we would use now. That destroys the excuse "you cannot judge them by our standards." But it also complicates the picture: British opinion contained abolitionists and slave-traders, Hobhouse and Kitchener, all at once. **"The British" is not one thing. Whose Britain are you talking about?** (A worked model answer on Source H appears in section 16.)

The war left Britain victorious, isolated and shaken. Recruiting revealed the poor physical condition of the urban working class, feeding the Edwardian "national efficiency" panic and, indirectly, early welfare legislation. At the Colonial Conference of 1902 Joseph Chamberlain appealed to the self-governing colonies for help, borrowing a line from Matthew Arnold: "The Weary Titan staggers under the too vast orb of its fate." After 1902 Britain hurried to reduce its enemies: alliance with Japan (1902), *entente* with France (1904), agreement with Russia (1907). **The empire had grown so large that it could no longer be defended alone.** That sentence is the bridge to everything that follows.

In **1910** the Union of South Africa joined British colonies to former Boer republics — and the price of that reconciliation between whites was paid by Black South Africans, whose limited Cape franchise was frozen and then destroyed. Apartheid was built on foundations laid under British rule.

## 9. Maximum extent and hidden crisis, 1914–1945

**The empire reached its maximum extent immediately after the First World War and began dying at the same moment. That is not a paradox. It is the central fact of the twentieth century.**

### 9.1 The war the empire fought

Roughly **1.3 million Indians** served overseas and some 74,000 died. Canada raised about 620,000 men, Australia around 416,000, New Zealand over 100,000 from a population of barely a million. Nearly a million Africans served as soldiers or, in far greater numbers, as carriers in the East African campaign, where mortality was appalling and record-keeping negligible. Three consequences followed, all corrosive.

**First, the Dominions came home changed.** Gallipoli (1915) and Vimy Ridge (1917) became founding national myths precisely because they were experienced as *national*, not imperial. The Dominions signed the Treaty of Versailles separately and joined the League of Nations in their own right.

**Second, the promises came due.** Britain had made incompatible commitments to buy support: to Sharif Hussein of Mecca (1915–16), to France (Sykes–Picot, May 1916), and to the Zionist movement in the **Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917**, promising "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine while stipulating that nothing should prejudice "the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities". The mandate that followed was structurally impossible from the day it began, and Britain spent 28 years failing to square it. Egypt exploded in revolution in 1919 and was granted nominal independence on 28 February 1922 with four matters "reserved" to Britain — a useful working definition of independence in name only. Iraq rose in 1920 and was suppressed at heavy cost, partly from the air.

**Third, and most important, India changed.** Indians had been told they fought for self-determination. What they received was the Government of India Act 1919 — a cautious "diarchy" sharing minor provincial powers — accompanied by the Rowlatt Acts extending wartime detention.

On **13 April 1919** at Jallianwala Bagh in Amritsar, Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer marched troops into an enclosed garden where a crowd had gathered in defiance of a ban and ordered them to fire without warning until ammunition ran low; 1,650 rounds were expended. The official Hunter Committee recorded **379 dead and about 1,200 wounded**; Congress's own inquiry put the dead at over a thousand. Dyer told the Committee his object had not merely been to disperse the crowd but "to produce a sufficient moral effect" throughout the Punjab. He was relieved of command — and a public subscription in Britain raised over £26,000 for him. **That last fact tells you as much as the massacre itself.** Rabindranath Tagore, the 1913 Nobel laureate, renounced his knighthood in protest on 31 May 1919.

> **▸ THINK 9.1** — Kim Wagner (*Amritsar 1919*, 2019) argues the massacre was not the aberration of one deranged officer but an expression of a long-standing colonial doctrine that exemplary violence was how order was maintained in an emergency. What kind of evidence would support that claim, and what kind would refute it? Where would you look?

Amritsar is the moment moderate Indian opinion broke. Gandhi, until then a loyalist who had recruited for the war effort, launched the first mass non-cooperation movement in 1920, and Congress ceased to be a debating club of lawyers and became a mass party. **The empire reached its greatest size and lost its moral licence in the same three years.**

### 9.2 Ireland: the first modern decolonisation

The Easter Rising of April 1916 was militarily hopeless and politically transformative, largely because of the executions that followed. Sinn Féin swept the 1918 election in Ireland, established its own parliament, and fought a guerrilla war from 1919 to 1921 against police, army and the notorious "Black and Tans". The **Anglo-Irish Treaty of 6 December 1921** created the Irish Free State as a Dominion (formally established 6 December 1922), partitioning six northern counties; a civil war among nationalists followed. De Valera's constitution of 1937 removed most remaining links, and the Republic of Ireland Act took effect on 18 April 1949.

**Ireland established the template, and every element recurs: guerrilla war Britain could suppress but not end at acceptable cost; negotiated Dominion status short of the demand; partition as the price; and a long constitutional drift to full sovereignty afterwards.** Indian, Zionist, Egyptian and Cypriot activists all studied it.

### 9.3 The white club, 1926–1931

The Imperial Conference of 1926 produced the **Balfour Definition**: the dominions were "autonomous Communities within the British Empire, equal in status, in no way subordinate one to another… though united by a common allegiance to the Crown, and freely associated as members of the British Commonwealth of Nations." The **Statute of Westminster (11 December 1931)** made it law.

Two observations examiners love. First, read that definition with the glossary in hand: every dominion was a settler state with a white electorate. Equality was extended to the settler dominions and withheld from India, whose Congress had declared for *purna swaraj* on 26 January 1930. **The Commonwealth was, at its creation, a white club** — and that fact set the terms of the argument for the next twenty years. Second, Newfoundland proves nothing was inevitable: bankrupted by the Depression, it *surrendered* self-government in 1934, reverted to rule by commission, and voted narrowly to join Canada in 1948.

The 1930s brought a defensive turn: at Ottawa in 1932 Britain and the Dominions built **imperial preference**, and the sterling area drew imperial finance into a bloc. The empire was becoming an economic fortress at exactly the moment its military position became indefensible against three simultaneous enemies. Meanwhile Indian policy ran on a rhythm of concession, repression, concession — Morley–Minto (1909), the 1919 Act, the Government of India Act 1935 — while Gandhi built mass non-violent non-cooperation, of which the **Salt March (12 March – 6 April 1930)**, a 240-mile walk to the sea to make salt in defiance of a state monopoly, was the masterpiece: it made the tax look absurd worldwide.

### 9.4 1939–1945: the empire wins the war and loses itself

India supplied roughly **2.5 million volunteers**, the largest volunteer army in history; African troops fought in Burma and East Africa; Caribbean volunteers served in the RAF. The empire's contribution was indispensable to British survival. And the war destroyed the empire's foundations in five ways.

- **Britain went bankrupt.** By 1945 it owed some £3.4 billion in "sterling balances", well over £1 billion of it to India, which had supplied goods and services on credit. **The empire was now Britain's creditor rather than its asset.**
- **British prestige in Asia collapsed.** **Singapore fell on 15 February 1942**, with around 80,000 troops surrendering to a smaller Japanese force — the largest capitulation in British military history, in front of the whole of Asia. The guarantee of European invincibility had been publicly voided by an Asian power.
- **The war's ideology cut against empire.** The Atlantic Charter of August 1941 affirmed "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live"; Churchill told the Commons on 9 September 1941 that he had meant it to apply to European nations under Nazi occupation. Everyone in the colonies read the Charter and nobody accepted the footnote. At the Mansion House on 10 November 1942 Churchill declared: "I have not become the King's First Minister in order to preside over the liquidation of the British Empire." He was in office when the largest part of it was liquidated.
- **India withdrew its consent.** **Quit India** (8 August 1942, launched with the instruction "Do or die") was suppressed with some 90,000 arrests — but suppression required troops Britain could not spare, and made clear that after the war India would be governed only by force. When soldiers of the Japanese-sponsored Indian National Army were tried in 1945–46 they were widely treated as heroes, and in February 1946 the Royal Indian Navy mutinied at Bombay. **That is the moment the game ended: the instrument of British rule in India could no longer be relied upon.**
- **Famine returned to Bengal.** In 1943 between **2.1 and 3 million people died**. There was no absolute shortage of food in India. There was a cyclone, the loss of Burmese rice imports, war-driven inflation, provincial governments blocking grain movement, a "denial policy" that removed boats and rice from coastal districts, and a London government that, facing a shipping crisis, repeatedly declined to divert vessels. Historians divide sharply: Madhusree Mukerjee (*Churchill's Secret War*, 2010) presents culpable indifference at the highest level, citing Leo Amery's diary record of Churchill's remark that he hated Indians; Tirthankar Roy and others emphasise wartime logistics, provincial failure and market breakdown over any single decision in London. What is not in dispute is that a famine killing millions occurred in the eighth year of a supposedly reformed system of Indian government, while Britain was fighting a war it described as one for freedom.

> **▸ THINK 9.2** — Set the Bengal famine of 1943 beside the Irish famine of 1845–52. What do they share? What makes them different? Is "empire" the most useful category for comparing them, or would "nineteenth-century political economy" or "wartime logistics" explain more?

## 10. Dissolution I: the Asian hinge, 1945–1957

The Labour government elected in July 1945 was not straightforwardly anti-imperial. It hoped to *reform* the empire into a development project that would help pay for the welfare state — hence intensified development schemes in Africa, including the famously disastrous Tanganyika groundnut scheme. Historians sometimes call this the "second colonial occupation": **more British officials went to Africa after 1945 than before it.** It is a mistake to think 1947 began a general retreat.

But India was beyond reform. Negotiations foundered on a question Britain had spent decades making harder: what happened to India's Muslims, roughly a quarter of the population, in a democracy dominated by Congress? Jinnah's Muslim League, which had demanded a separate state since the **Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940**, called Direct Action Day on 16 August 1946; the communal killing that followed in Calcutta convinced London that time had run out. Mountbatten arrived as the last Viceroy on 22 March 1947 and accelerated everything: partition announced 3 June, the Indian Independence Act passed 18 July, power transferred at midnight on **14–15 August 1947**. Cyril Radcliffe, a barrister who had never been to India, was given about five weeks to draw borders through Punjab and Bengal from out-of-date maps and census returns; his award was not published until **17 August** — two days *after* independence. Millions spent the first days of freedom not knowing which country they were in.

> **SOURCE I — Jawaharlal Nehru, address to the Constituent Assembly, New Delhi, shortly before midnight on 14 August 1947.**
>
> "At the stroke of the midnight hour, when the world sleeps, India will awake to life and freedom."

**The cost.** Between 10 and 15 million people were displaced, the largest forced migration in recorded history. Estimates of the dead range from around 200,000 to two million; the range itself tells you how little anyone was counting. Tens of thousands of women were abducted and raped. Gandhi was not in Delhi that night — he was in Calcutta, fasting, trying to stop the killing; he was assassinated on 30 January 1948 by a Hindu nationalist who thought him too conciliatory. Kashmir was left disputed and has been fought over since.

> **▸ THINK 10.1** — Who is responsible for Partition? Candidates: the British, for a century of governing through religious categories and then leaving at speed; the Muslim League, for demanding a state; Congress, for refusing federal compromise; local politicians and militias who organised the killing. Historians distribute blame differently — compare Yasmin Khan's *The Great Partition* (2007) with Ayesha Jalal's *The Sole Spokesman* (1985). **Rank the candidates and defend your ranking in six sentences.** Note also that Gandhi's absence from Delhi is a *symbol*, not an argument: what would you need to show to turn it into one?

Then the cascade. **Burma independent on 4 January 1948** — and, uniquely among the major territories, it left the Commonwealth entirely. **Ceylon on 4 February 1948**, and it stayed. **Palestine**: having suppressed the Arab Revolt of 1936–39 and then restricted Jewish immigration in the 1939 White Paper at the worst possible moment, and facing a Zionist insurgency after 1945, Britain referred the problem to the United Nations in February 1947 and left at midnight on **14/15 May 1948**. It was an abdication, not a transfer. War began the same day.

One brilliant improvisation kept the structure alive. When India resolved to become a republic, membership of a Commonwealth defined by allegiance to the Crown became impossible. The **London Declaration of April 1949** solved it: republics could belong, with the monarch recognised simply as "Head of the Commonwealth". At a stroke the white club became a multiracial association — and acquired an Afro-Asian majority that would, within a decade, be used to pressure Britain over Rhodesia and South Africa.

Where challenged, Britain fought. In Malaya, whose tin and rubber earned dollars the sterling area desperately needed, the **Malayan Emergency (16 June 1948 – 31 July 1960)** pitted a largely Chinese-recruited communist insurgency against a counter-insurgency combining political concession with the forcible resettlement of roughly 500,000 people into guarded "New Villages". It is often taught as the model successful counter-insurgency; it also included the killing of 24 unarmed villagers at Batang Kali in December 1948, never satisfactorily investigated. Malaya became independent on **31 August 1957**, on terms broadly acceptable to Britain — which is precisely why the case is used to argue that Britain "managed" decolonisation. **Malaya shows the pattern at its clearest: fight the nationalists you cannot live with; hand over to the ones you can.**

## 11. Dissolution II: Suez and the African exit, 1956–1968

### 11.1 Suez: the hinge of the whole story

On 26 July 1956 Egypt's President Gamal Abdel Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal Company. Britain, France and Israel secretly agreed a plan at Sèvres: Israel would invade Sinai, and the two European powers would intervene as peacekeepers to seize the canal. Israel invaded on 29 October; Britain and France issued a prearranged ultimatum, bombed Egyptian airfields from 31 October, and landed at Port Said on 5–6 November. Militarily it worked.

Politically it was a catastrophe. The United States, furious at being deceived and at the timing — the Soviet invasion of Hungary was under way — refused to support sterling in a run on the pound and blocked Britain's access to IMF credit until it agreed to withdraw. A ceasefire came on 6–7 November; British forces left in December; Eden resigned on 9 January 1957.

Suez did not by itself cause decolonisation — plans for African independence were already forming. What it did was settle three questions permanently: Britain could not act against American wishes; Britain could not hold a position against determined nationalism plus international opinion; and the psychological premise of great-power status had been publicly falsified. At West Point on 5 December 1962 the American Secretary of State Dean Acheson made the wound public: "Great Britain has lost an Empire and has not yet found a role."

> **▸ THINK 11.1** — Test the standard claim that Suez was the moment Britain discovered it was no longer a great power. (a) Name two things Britain did after 1956 that a non-great power could not have done. (b) Explain how the *American* veto, rather than Egyptian resistance, decided the outcome. (c) Decide whether Suez *caused* the acceleration after 1957 or merely *revealed* conditions that already existed.

### 11.2 The wind of change

Sudan became independent on **1 January 1956**. On **6 March 1957** the Gold Coast became **Ghana**, the first sub-Saharan African colony to gain independence, under Kwame Nkrumah — imprisoned by the British in 1950, winner of an election from his cell in 1951, head of government the following year. His slogan captured a generation's strategy: "Seek ye first the political kingdom, and all things shall be added unto it."

> **SOURCE J — Harold Macmillan, Prime Minister, to the Parliament of South Africa, Cape Town, 3 February 1960.** He had just toured British Africa. His audience was a government committed to apartheid.
>
> "The wind of change is blowing through this continent, and, whether we like it or not, this growth of national consciousness is a political fact. We must all accept it as a fact, and our national policies must take account of it."

> **▸ THINK 11.2 — attribution matters most here.**
> (a) Why deliver this speech in Cape Town rather than London?
> (b) Notice the verb. Macmillan says national consciousness is "a political fact" to be *accepted*, not a right to be honoured. What is the difference, and what does the choice reveal about the government's motive?
> (c) Six weeks later, on 21 March 1960, South African police killed 69 protesters at Sharpeville; South Africa left the Commonwealth on 31 May 1961. Does that sequence make Source J look prophetic, or show that the speech failed in its immediate purpose?

Then the flood: Nigeria, Somaliland and Cyprus (1960); Sierra Leone and Tanganyika (1961); Jamaica, Trinidad and Uganda (1962); Kenya and Zanzibar (1963); Malawi, Zambia and Malta (1964); the Gambia (1965); Guyana, Botswana, Lesotho and Barbados (1966); Mauritius and Swaziland (1968). **In roughly a decade Britain divested itself of most of an empire it had spent three centuries assembling.**

Why so fast? A hard-headed calculation had changed. Colonies were no longer obviously profitable, and Whitehall cost-benefit reviews in the late 1950s concluded it made little economic difference either way. Holding territory against nationalist movements was expensive and, after France's experience in Algeria and Britain's own in Kenya and Cyprus, visibly ugly. Britain's economic future was being redefined as European — the first EEC application came in 1961. The Cold War priority was to grant independence *early enough* to hand power to a friendly government rather than a radicalised one. And the moral vocabulary of international politics had turned: UN General Assembly Resolution 1514, on 14 December 1960, declared colonialism a denial of fundamental human rights.

> ### CASE STUDY: Kenya, or the violence at the end
>
> Kenya is where to look to understand what happened when a substantial white settler population stood in the way. Around 30,000 Europeans held the fertile "White Highlands", from which Kikuyu families had been dispossessed, creating a landless class.
>
> From 1952 an oath-bound insurgency — the British called it **Mau Mau**, its fighters called themselves the Land and Freedom Army — attacked settlers and, far more often, Africans who cooperated with the government. It was as much a Kikuyu civil war as an anti-colonial revolt. A State of Emergency was declared on **20 October 1952** and lasted until 1960.
>
> The counter-insurgency was systematic: mass detention without trial in a network known as "the Pipeline", the forced villagisation of over a million Kikuyu into guarded settlements, and a screening system in which torture — beatings, burning, sexual violence — was routine. Official figures record **11,503 insurgents killed** and **1,090 people hanged**, the largest use of capital punishment in any British counter-insurgency. **Thirty-two European settlers were killed in the entire Emergency.** In March 1959 eleven detainees were beaten to death at **Hola camp**, and the scandal helped destroy the political case for holding on: Enoch Powell told the Commons on 27 July 1959 that "we cannot say, 'We will have African standards in Africa, Asian standards in Asia and perhaps British standards here at home.'"
>
> **How many were detained is disputed, and the dispute is instructive.** Official records give around 80,000. **Caroline Elkins** (*Britain's Gulag*, 2005) argued from oral testimony and demographic analysis for a far higher figure and for tens of thousands of deaths; **David Anderson** (*Histories of the Hanged*, 2005) accepts the brutality but regards her highest numbers as unsupported and criticises her demographic method. Both books won major prizes. Read them against each other.

> **INTERPRETATION 6 — the archive itself.** In the Mau Mau compensation case brought against the Foreign Office from 2009, the government disclosed that thousands of files removed from colonies at independence were held at **Hanslope Park**. These "migrated archives" (FCO 141), released from 2012, documented the systematic destruction of records at the end of empire — Operation Legacy — as well as abuse. On **6 June 2013** the Foreign Secretary William Hague told the Commons that the government "sincerely regrets" the abuses and announced a settlement of **£19.9 million** for **5,228** Kenyan claimants.

> **▸ THINK 11.3 — the most important methodological point in this chapter.** If the losing side of a conflict has its records seized or burned, and the winning side's own incriminating records are removed from the country and hidden for fifty years, what happens to the "balance" of the surviving evidence? How should a historian — and how should you, in an exam — handle a silence in the record that was *created deliberately*? And what might still be missing from the record you are being taught from?

> **▸ THINK 11.4** — Kenya became independent on 12 December 1963 under Jomo Kenyatta, whom the British had imprisoned as a Mau Mau leader on evidence widely regarded as fabricated, and who then governed as a conservative ally of Britain and the West. Nkrumah was jailed in 1950 and head of government by 1952. **What does the recurrence of the "prisoner to premier" pattern tell you about how decolonisation actually worked?** (Consider Robinson's collaboration model, and ask who Britain needed on the other side of the table.)

## 12. Dissolution III: the long tail, 1968–1997, and the empire coming home

Decolonisation did not stop in 1968; it dwindled.

In **January 1968**, after devaluation, the Wilson government announced withdrawal from military commitments **"East of Suez"**. It was an accountant's decision with strategic consequences: the Gulf protectorates, defended by Britain for 150 years, became independent in 1971 — Bahrain (15 August), Qatar (3 September), the United Arab Emirates (2 December) — several of their rulers reportedly offering to pay for the British garrisons to stay. **That is the clearest possible case of withdrawal by budget.** Aden had already gone on 30 November 1967 after a four-year insurgency, with no agreed successor government: one of the few departures that resembled a defeat.

Britain's designs for successor federations mostly failed. The **West Indies Federation** (1958–62) collapsed when Jamaican voters rejected it in a 1961 referendum; Jamaica became independent on 6 August 1962 and Trinidad and Tobago on 31 August. The Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland was dissolved on 31 December 1963.

Two cases refused to resolve cleanly.

**Rhodesia.** On 11 November 1965 Ian Smith's white-minority government issued a **Unilateral Declaration of Independence** rather than accept Britain's condition of eventual majority rule. Britain refused to use force against fellow Britons — a decision that enraged African Commonwealth members and is often cited as evidence of a racial double standard — and relied on sanctions that leaked. It took a fifteen-year guerrilla war by ZANU and ZAPU, and the collapse of Portuguese rule in Mozambique, to force the Lancaster House settlement of December 1979. **Zimbabwe became independent on 18 April 1980.**

**The Falklands.** On 2 April 1982 Argentina invaded islands whose roughly 1,800 inhabitants were determined to remain British. Britain retook them in a ten-week war costing 255 British and 649 Argentine lives. It was, in a strict sense, an anti-decolonisation war — fought to *keep* a territory — and it is the clearest evidence that "empire" and "self-determination" cannot always be sorted into opposing columns.

And then the last great transfer. Hong Kong Island had been ceded in 1842 and Kowloon in 1860, but the overwhelming majority of the colony's land area was the **New Territories, leased for 99 years in 1898**. The lease expired on 30 June 1997 and the colony was not viable without it. The **Sino-British Joint Declaration of 19 December 1984** provided for transfer of the whole territory under a "one country, two systems" formula. At midnight on **30 June/1 July 1997**, in torrential rain, the flag came down. Britain had never allowed Hong Kong's people to elect their own government and introduced significant democratic reform only in the 1990s, on the way out. **This is the one major decolonisation in which the population was neither consulted nor made independent.** Britain's Asian empire had begun with a company's trading post and ended with a lease running out.

Fourteen **British Overseas Territories** remain. Most are small and want to stay. One is not simple: in 1965 Britain detached the **Chagos Archipelago** from Mauritius to create the British Indian Ocean Territory, then between 1968 and 1973 removed its entire population — between 1,500 and 2,000 Chagossians — so that the United States could build an airbase on Diego Garcia. The International Court of Justice held in an advisory opinion of 25 February 2019 that the detachment had been unlawful; a treaty transferring sovereignty to Mauritius, with a long lease on the base, was signed on 22 May 2025.

> **▸ THINK 12.1** — Chagos happened after the wind of change, after most of Africa was free, within the lifetime of people you know. What does it do to the idea that empire is "the past"? And note: **empire has an ending date only if you decide which ending counts.** Which would you choose — 1947, 1968, 1980, 1997, or none of them — and why?

### The empire comes home

Decolonisation was also a migration story, and it is not a separate topic. The **British Nationality Act 1948** made colonial and Commonwealth subjects citizens of the United Kingdom and Colonies with the right of entry; migration from the Caribbean, India, Pakistan, Cyprus, Hong Kong and East Africa followed, and was then progressively legislated away by the Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 and the Immigration Act 1971 — a sequence historians such as Kathleen Paul have argued was designed to keep the door open to white Commonwealth citizens while closing it to others. Enoch Powell's speech of 20 April 1968 made hostility respectable. In 2018 the **Windrush scandal** revealed that people who had arrived legally as children before 1973 had been denied healthcare, sacked, detained and in confirmed cases wrongly removed; the Home Office identified 164 Caribbean-born cases for review, and Wendy Williams's *Lessons Learned Review* (2020) found "institutional ignorance and thoughtlessness towards the issue of race".

**"We are here because you were there,"** wrote the Sri Lankan-British writer Ambalavaner Sivanandan. It is the single most useful sentence about modern Britain. The end of empire abroad and the making of multi-ethnic Britain at home are the same process, not two subjects.

## 14. The great debates: an interpretations dossier

Examiners set questions on interpretations, not on your opinions. Learn the names.

### Debate 1 — Why did Britain expand?

| Position | Historian | Core claim | Strongest evidence for | Strongest objection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Absence of mind | J. R. Seeley (1883) | Expansion was unplanned drift | No master plan; many acquisitions were by-products of European war | Individual acquisitions were highly deliberate; Seeley was arguing *for* imperial federation, not describing dispassionately |
| Capitalist necessity | Hobson (1902); Lenin (1917) | Surplus capital needed outlets; financiers drove policy | Investment flows; Rhodes and the South African War | Most British capital went to the USA, Argentina and the dominions, not to newly conquered Africa |
| Strategic reaction | Robinson & Gallagher (1961) | Crises on the periphery, above all Egypt 1882, dragged reluctant ministers in | Cabinet papers show anxiety, not appetite | Over-reliant on the "official mind"; underweights business lobbying and men on the spot |
| Gentlemanly capitalism | Cain & Hopkins (1993) | The driving interest was the City and the financial elite, socially fused with the political class | Explains why Manchester manufacturers were often anti-imperial while bankers were not | Hard to distinguish influence from coincidence of background |
| Collaboration | Ronald Robinson (1972) | Expansion occurred where local elites could be recruited | Indirect rule; princely states; the Indian Army | Risks making the colonised responsible for their own colonisation |

### Debate 2 — Did empire matter to Britain at home?

**John MacKenzie** (*Propaganda and Empire*, 1984) argued empire saturated British popular culture — school texts, missionary societies, music hall, biscuit tins, exhibitions. **Bernard Porter** (*The Absent-Minded Imperialists*, 2004) replied that most Britons were indifferent, that imperial imagery is not imperial consciousness, and that the empire was run by a small caste. **Catherine Hall** and **Sonya Rose** (*At Home with the Empire*, 2006) reframed it: the issue is not whether people talked about empire but how deeply imperial assumptions structured British class, gender and racial categories even when unspoken.

> **▸ THINK 14.1** — How would you *test* MacKenzie against Porter? What sources would count as evidence of "imperial consciousness", and what would only be evidence of imperial *imagery*? This is a genuine methodological problem, and saying so is a strength, not a dodge.

### Debate 3 — Famine: the hardest case

Between 1876 and 1902 India suffered catastrophic famines; the Great Famine of 1876–78 alone is generally estimated to have killed between five and ten million. Grain continued to be exported.

- **Mike Davis** (*Late Victorian Holocausts*, 2001): the deaths were the product of forced integration into a world market plus an administration ideologically committed to non-interference in grain prices. The El Niño droughts were real; the mortality was policy.
- **Amartya Sen** (*Poverty and Famines*, 1981): famines are usually not caused by an absolute absence of food but by collapses in people's **entitlement** to it — wages, prices, employment. His most quoted claim is that no substantial famine has occurred in a functioning democracy with a free press.
- **Tirthankar Roy** and others: mortality was largely a function of drought in a subsistence agriculture with poor transport; the Famine Codes from 1880 did reduce it; comparable famines occurred in non-colonised regions.

> **▸ THINK 14.2** — Sen's argument is not primarily about empire; it is about political accountability. Explain in three sentences why it nonetheless amounts to a serious charge against British rule in India. Then explain what evidence would be needed to *test* Davis's stronger claim.

### Debate 4 — Why did the empire end?

Four explanations. The best answers refuse to choose only one.

1. **Metropolitan decline — Britain could no longer afford it.** Strong: two world wars converted the world's greatest creditor into a debtor to its own colonies; the 1957 Defence White Paper and the 1968 East of Suez decision were budget decisions. *But* Britain also spent lavishly on holding territory when it judged the stakes high enough. **Poverty explains the direction of travel better than the timing.**
2. **Peripheral nationalism — the colonised drove Britain out.** Strong and indispensable: Congress, the Muslim League, Sinn Féin, the CPP, EOKA, ZANU. *But* movements were often weak or divided, and in the Gulf and much of the Caribbean were barely demanding independence when it was granted. In several territories Britain left before it was pushed.
3. **International pressure.** The United States was structurally anti-colonial (though inconsistently so); the USSR offered nationalists an alternative patron; UN Resolution 1514 (1960) made colonialism diplomatically indefensible. Suez proved the constraint was real.
4. **Loss of collaborators, and of will.** The subtlest and, in this chapter's view, the most powerful. When intermediaries could get more from nationalism than from Britain, the machinery of rule seized. Simultaneously the metropolitan belief that ruling others was legitimate — robust in 1900 — had largely evaporated by 1960, corroded by the war against Nazi racism and by the indefensibility of the imperial position in public argument.

Named positions to cite: **John Darwin** (the empire as a fragile improvisation whose global system — sterling, sea power, the Indian Army, settler loyalty — was broken by the world wars, after which ministers improvised retreat while pretending to control it); **Wm. Roger Louis and Ronald Robinson**, "The Imperialism of Decolonization" (1994), on Britain trading formal empire for junior partnership with the United States, preserving influence by shedding responsibility; **Ronald Hyam** on economic constraint; **Frederick Cooper** and **Elizabeth Buettner** on African labour movements and urban politics undoing colonial states from below, and on how decolonisation reshaped Europe as much as Africa.

> **▸ THINK 14.3** — Rank these explanations for (a) India 1947, (b) Ghana 1957, (c) Aden 1967 and (d) Hong Kong 1997. **If your ranking is different for each, you have discovered why "why did the empire end?" cannot be answered in one sentence — and you have the skeleton of a first-class essay.**

> **THINK ABOUT THIS.** If a country becomes independent but its currency, its army's training, its export markets and its elite's education remain oriented towards London, how much has changed? Now the counter-argument: is that framing at risk of denying new nations the agency they had just won?

## 15. The ledger, and why "was it worth it?" is the wrong question

A serious chapter must state consequences without flinching in either direction.

**It killed and coerced at scale.** The slave trade; the suppression of 1857; the famines in Ireland (1845–52) and India (1770, 1876–78, 1896–1902, Bengal 1943); the concentration camps of the South African War; Amritsar; the Kenyan detention camps. Colonial rule was ultimately a rule of force, and when consent failed the force appeared quickly.

**It moved people, mostly not by choice.** Some three million Africans carried on British ships. Over a million Indians shipped under indenture. More than twenty million emigrants from the British Isles between 1815 and 1914. Then the return current after 1948.

**It extracted, and it also invested — and the balance is contested.**

- *Extraction.* Dadabhai Naoroji, elected to the Commons in 1892, argued in *Poverty and Un-British Rule in India* (1901) for a continuous **"drain of wealth"** — Home Charges, remitted profits, pensions and military costs paid from Indian revenue. Maddison's estimates put India at roughly a quarter of world GDP around 1700 and about 4 per cent by 1950; Bairoch estimated its share of world manufacturing falling from around 25 per cent in 1750 to about 2 per cent by 1900. Utsa Patnaik has put a figure of nearly $45 trillion on the drain from India between 1765 and 1938 — widely quoted (notably by Shashi Tharoor) and widely contested, because it rests on compound-interest assumptions other economists reject. **Cite the figure only with the argument about it.**
- *Empire was expensive for Britain too.* Patrick O'Brien argued (1988) that empire was not necessary to British industrial growth; **Davis and Huttenback** (*Mammon and the Pursuit of Empire*, 1986) calculated that imperial defence was subsidised by ordinary British taxpayers for the benefit of a narrow investing elite.
- *The ledger of goods.* **Niall Ferguson** (*Empire*, 2003) argues Britain spread free trade, capital, law and representative institutions, and that the relevant comparison is not an imagined free India but the alternative empires that might have ruled instead. **Tharoor** (*Inglorious Empire*, 2017) replies that India's railways — the world's fourth-largest network by 1947 — were built for extraction, routed for export and military needs, and paid for by Indian taxpayers guaranteeing returns to British investors, and that "the rule of law" coexisted with the Rowlatt Acts and Amritsar. **Nigel Biggar** (2023) argues for a mixed verdict and against anachronistic condemnation; **Priyamvada Gopal** (2019) inverts the frame, arguing British liberty was itself partly taught by colonial rebels.

> **▸ THINK 15.1 — the most important warning in this chapter.** Notice that the first two positions can both be true: an empire can impoverish its subjects *and* fail to enrich the average citizen of the metropole. The "balance sheet" framing has a hidden flaw — **it assumes costs and benefits fell on the same people. They did not.** Railways in India were real; so was the revenue system that paid for them; so were the famines. Write two sentences explaining why "the empire built railways" and "the empire caused famines" can both be true without cancelling out. Then note: "was it worth it?" is the wrong question, because it assumes a single "it" and a single set of books. **Better: worth it to whom? measured how? compared with what?**

**It left institutions, languages and borders.** English as a world language; common law; parliamentary forms; the Commonwealth of 56 states and some 2.7 billion people, which now includes countries — Mozambique, Rwanda, Gabon, Togo — that were never British at all, which is the strongest evidence that decolonisation produced something other than pure rupture. And borders: the Radcliffe Line, the Durand Line, the ruler-straight frontiers of Africa, the unresolved geographies of Palestine and Cyprus, the Nigeria of three unequal regions, the Iraq of three provinces welded together in 1920, the Ireland of six counties. **Many of the world's most dangerous fault lines were drawn in London, Cairo or Simla in a hurry, and are still under load.**

**It is still being argued over, and not only by historians.** The Benin Bronzes — several thousand objects removed when a British punitive expedition sacked Benin City in February 1897, many auctioned to defray the expedition's cost, around 900 of them in the British Museum — are the subject of live restitution claims; Jesus College, Cambridge and the Horniman Museum have returned or transferred objects, the British Museum has not, citing the British Museum Act 1963. The CARICOM Ten-Point Plan for Reparatory Justice (2014) asks European states for apology, debt cancellation and investment. Britain has expressed regret for slavery but has not apologised.

**And it was, from the beginning, argued about.** Burke against Hastings; Gladstone against the Opium War; abolitionists against the slave lobby; Hobhouse and Campbell-Bannerman against Kitchener; Naoroji and Tagore against the Raj; Powell over Hola. **There was never a settled British consensus that the empire was good.** Judging the past by the standards of the present is a real historical error — but the record shows that the past contained plenty of people applying critical standards at the time.

## 16. Skills workshop

### 16.1 Source utility: a method that survives exam pressure

Weak answers say "this source is biased, therefore unreliable". That sentence gains almost no marks, because every source has a point of view, and **a point of view is information**. Use four steps instead.

1. **Nature, Origin, Purpose (NOP).** What kind of document? Who made it, when, for whom? What was it trying to achieve?
2. **Content.** What does it say — including what it implies, assumes or omits?
3. **Own knowledge.** What corroborates or contradicts it? *This is where marks are won or lost.*
4. **Utility for a specified question.** Never say a source is "useful" in the abstract. Say what it is useful *for*, and what it cannot tell you.

**Worked example — Source H (Emily Hobhouse, 1901).**

> *Weak:* "Hobhouse was against the war so she was biased and might exaggerate the camps."
>
> *Strong:* "Hobhouse's report is a campaigning document produced for a relief fund and written to shock: its purpose was to force intervention, so it selects the worst conditions she saw and describes them emotively. That purpose limits its use as a measure of average camp conditions across the whole system. But it is highly valuable for two other questions. First, as eyewitness testimony from someone with no official position, it records what a hostile outsider could see in camps whose administration had every incentive to conceal failure. Second, its consequences are themselves evidence: the government's response was not to disprove it but to appoint the Fawcett Commission, whose findings substantially confirmed her account, and roughly 28,000 Boer civilians, mostly children, died. The source is therefore weak evidence of typicality and strong evidence both of conditions and of the political mechanism by which imperial atrocity could occasionally be forced into British public view — while the parallel deaths of at least 14,000 Black Africans in separate camps, which her report barely touches, shows the limits of even critical contemporary vision."

Notice what the strong version does: it converts "bias" into a precise statement about *what the source can and cannot establish*; it deploys specific own knowledge (the Fawcett Commission, the death tolls); and it ends by identifying a silence.

### 16.2 "How far do you agree?" — a structure for interpretation questions

Use a **three-move** paragraph, repeated:

1. **State the interpretation in its strongest form** — the version its author would recognise, not a straw man.
2. **Test it against specific evidence** — at least one example that supports and one that strains it.
3. **Judge its scope.** Most historical interpretations are not true or false; they are true *of some cases, in some periods, at some scale*. **Saying where an argument works and where it stops is the single highest-value move available to you.**

**Worked plan — "How far do you agree that the British Empire ended because Britain could no longer afford it?"**

> *Opening judgement:* Economic weakness set the outer limits of British choice after 1945, but it was decisive only where nationalist pressure had already made the cost of staying visible; where cost was low and pressure weak, Britain stayed for decades longer.
>
> *Paragraph A (supports):* Sterling balances of over £1 billion owed to India; the 1947 convertibility crisis; the American financial veto at Suez in November 1956; the January 1968 East of Suez announcement, taken immediately after devaluation. In the Gulf, independence in 1971 was granted to rulers who did not want it and offered to pay for the garrison — withdrawal by budget, in its purest form.
>
> *Paragraph B (complicates):* Britain spent lavishly on holding territory when it judged the stakes high enough — Malaya from 1948, Kenya from 1952, Cyprus from 1955, Aden from 1963 — and invested *more* in colonial development after 1945 than before. Cost was not an independent variable; it was the price of resistance.
>
> *Paragraph C (rival factor):* Nationalist mobilisation changed that price. Robinson's collaboration model supplies the mechanism: once educated elites and, crucially, the intermediary classes stopped cooperating, indirect rule became direct rule, and direct rule was unaffordable.
>
> *Paragraph D (scope test):* The model fails entirely for Hong Kong in 1997, which was profitable, stable, and handed over because a lease expired and China was stronger. It also fails for Southern Rhodesia, where Britain's problem was not cost but the political impossibility of coercing white settlers.
>
> *Conclusion:* Affordability is best understood not as a cause but as an exchange rate — the mechanism by which nationalist pressure, imperial overstretch and American priorities were converted into cabinet decisions.

### 16.3 Comparing two interpretations

When a question gives you two extracts by historians, do **not** summarise them in turn. Instead:

- Identify the **question each is answering**. Ferguson and Elkins often appear to contradict each other while answering different questions — one about aggregate economic effects, one about the character of colonial governance.
- Identify the **evidence base**. Cain and Hopkins read City records; Elkins read detention-camp files and oral testimony; MacKenzie read biscuit tins. **Different archives produce different empires.**
- Identify the **scale**. Claims about "the empire" made from one colony, one decade or one class are vulnerable, and saying so is analysis, not evasion.
- Identify the **politics of the moment of writing**. Gallagher and Robinson wrote in 1953 as the empire was being reappraised; Ferguson in 2003, during debates about American intervention; Elkins after the Hanslope files were disclosed. Context is not an accusation — it is an explanation of what a historian could see.

### 16.4 Three errors that cost marks every year

1. **The "for and against" list with no judgement.** Balance is not the absence of an argument.
2. **Anachronistic vocabulary.** Writing that Victorians "should have known better" tells the examiner nothing. Show instead that critics *at the time* — Burke, Gladstone, Hobhouse, Naoroji, Tagore — said so, which proves the alternatives were available and known.
3. **Treating "the British" as a single actor.** Settlers, missionaries, the Colonial Office, the India Office, the Treasury, the Admiralty, planters and Manchester manufacturers frequently wanted opposite things. **Naming *which* British is almost always an upgrade.**

## 17. Summary: the argument in twelve sentences

1. The empire was assembled over roughly 350 years, largely by private companies and settlers before the state caught up, and dismantled in roughly 35.
2. Ireland was the rehearsal — plantation, dispossession and a language of civilising others, exported outward from the 1550s — and it was also a full participant in colonising elsewhere.
3. The first empire was Atlantic and built on sugar and enslaved labour: British ships carried some 3.1–3.4 million Africans, and in 1833 British taxpayers compensated the enslavers, not the enslaved.
4. Britain repeatedly expanded by delegating empire to chartered companies because it did not want to pay for it, and repeatedly had to nationalise the results.
5. War with France, not design, delivered the largest single accessions — 1713, 1763, 1815.
6. Losing America in 1783 showed that empires break when the metropolis tries to make colonies pay their own costs, and that trade could survive independence — the germ of "informal empire".
7. India was conquered through collaboration, by Indian soldiers paid from Indian revenue, which is why 1857 and 1946 were both existential crises.
8. Abolition in 1807 and 1833 was genuine, and was entangled with compensation to owners, apprenticeship, indenture, and a new licence for intervention in Africa.
9. Responsible government from 1848 secured settler loyalty by transferring power to settlers — at the direct expense of indigenous peoples, for whom the dominions were the empire's most permanent form.
10. The empire reached its maximum extent in 1922 and lost its moral licence in the same three years — Amritsar, the Irish war, the Egyptian and Iraqi revolts — at the very moment two world wars destroyed the financial and military basis for holding it.
11. Indian independence in 1947 removed the manpower, revenue and strategic purpose that had organised the whole eastern system; Suez in 1956 destroyed the illusion of independent great-power action; the African exit was rapid because cost calculations, international climate and the availability of acceptable successors all changed together.
12. The empire ended for the same reason it grew — because the balance between profit, cost and consent shifted, and because the people it ruled, who had always been essential to it, withdrew their cooperation.

> **▸ FINAL THINK** — Return to your answer to THINK 1.1. Would you now defend "absence of mind" as a description of *acquisition*? Would you use it of *dissolution*? Write 150 words on whether the same phrase can fairly describe both halves of the story.

**And one thing to hold on to.** The empire was not a plan. It was a series of decisions taken by people who mostly could not see more than a few years ahead, in a competitive world, under financial constraint, and who were repeatedly surprised — by American colonists in 1775, by sepoys in 1857, by Boers in 1899, by the Japanese in 1942, by Nasser in 1956. That is not an excuse for the consequences. It is a warning against reading history backwards, as though everything that happened had to happen. **Contingency is not absolution; but determinism is not analysis.**
