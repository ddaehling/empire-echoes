On 22 June 1948 the Empire Windrush docked at Tilbury with 1,027 passengers, 802 of them from the Caribbean. They were British subjects. The British Nationality Act passed a month later said so in law.

Fourteen years afterwards Parliament took the right away again. The Commonwealth Immigrants Act of 1962 introduced employment vouchers, and the number of people who could come fell by more than half within a year.

    English ships landed on an uninhabited Barbados in 1627. Within forty years sugar had remade it: by 1680 the island held more enslaved Africans than English settlers, and its planters were the richest men in English America.

    The Barbados Slave Code of 1661 gave that arrangement its law. It called enslaved people property, set no penalty worth the name for killing one, and was copied into Jamaica, South Carolina and Antigua. Emancipation came in 1834 — followed by four years of unpaid apprenticeship, and £20 million in compensation paid to owners, not to the people they had owned.

    Source Hilary McD. Beckles, A History of Barbados (2006) 1627 landfall date contested
